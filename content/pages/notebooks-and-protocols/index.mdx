## **Weekly Summaries**

This section documents our laboratory work, including our e-notebooks, and the protocols we used for our experiments.

Each week of the lab is described separately, beginning with a weekly summary of what we did, followed by main results of the week, where we present and discuss the most relevant findings, and ending with a link to the PDF of the e-notebook used for that week.

After the weekly summaries section, there is a [protocols section](#protocols), where you can find the protocols we used in the lab.

### Summer Lab

Summer Lab was the main time we spent in the lab. We had a summer break from other university responsibilities, so we could dedicate ourselves to being in the lab full-time.

  The second week of the lab was dedicated to repeating and troubleshooting restriction digestion of pET-11a with BamHI and NdeI. Different protocols were tested, with different incubation times.     E.Z.N.A. plasmid miniprep kit was used as an alternative to Jena Bioscience kit, resulting in a lower concentration of DNA, but with higher purity (<Ref target="tab-ah0v2" />). For future experiments, E.Z.N.A. plasmid miniprep kit was used.

  **Weekly summary:**

  During Week 27, we focused mainly on transforming *E. coli* TG1 with our remaining gene constructs. Positive ci2350-His colonies were sequenced, and one colony with correct sequence was identified and stored for subsequent transformation into *E. coli* BL21(DE3).

    **Main results of the week:**

    Agarose gel electrophoresis of colony PCR products (<Ref target="fig-nsr9f" />) showed positive results in wells 2-9, corresponding to the expected size of the amplified ci2350-His insert region, confirming successful transformation of *E. coli* TG1 with ci2350-His. Multiple controls were tested, including colonies transformed with empty pET-11a, and colonies from a ligation control (ligation without insert). All controls proved negative, indicating the ligation and transformation were correct. Following our PI Johan's recommendation, in future transformations we will screen only 1-2 colonies from control plates and prioritize testing more transformant colonies, to increase the chance of identifying a correctly transformed colony. Four ci2350-His positive colonies were sent for sequencing, where one was identified as having a correct construct.

    **Main results of the week:**

    On the SDS-PAGE gels, the bands across the gel formed a ‘frowning’ pattern: the middle lanes migrated less than outer lanes. The current during the run was also very low (\~7 mA). After troubleshooting with our PI Johan, we linked this to an incorrectly prepared SDS running buffer. The stock was assumed to be 50X and was diluted 1:50 prior to running the gel. It was in fact a 10X stock, so the working buffer was too dilute. The resulting low ion concentration gave the buffer low conductivity, explaining the low current and the poor run. When the buffer was instead diluted 1:10, the gel ran better and the current was higher (\~50 mA).     Protein concentrations of each sample were measured by <Tooltip text="A color-based test that measures protein concentration.">Bradford assay</Tooltip> and multiplied by the target band's fraction of the total lane intensity on SDS-PAGE, allowing us to estimate the mass concentration of the expressed recombinant protein in the whole-cell and soluble fraction for each AKR1D1 construct.

    The Bradford assay results showed that protein concentration in the soluble fraction was almost equal to, or slightly higher than, that of the whole-cell fraction. This is not physically possible, since the soluble fraction is a portion of the whole-cell fraction, an error must have occurred somewhere in the experiment. The most likely cause is incomplete resuspension of the <Tooltip text="The part of a cell sample that does not dissolve, such as clumped protein.">insoluble material</Tooltip> in the whole-cell samples before the Bradford assay, which would have led to underestimating the concentration of whole-cell protein. If the experiment were to be repeated, we would ensure complete solubilization of the insoluble fraction prior to quantification, for example boiling the sample in a buffer compatible with the Bradford assay, to obtain consistent and reproducible results.

    The uninduced cultures showed unexpectedly high protein concentrations measured by Bradford assay, even though all samples had been normalized to OD 20 before the assay. Because of the high total protein content in these samples, SDS-PAGE lanes may also have been overloaded, increasing background and affecting baseline determination and band quantification (volume ratios and raw volumes). In future runs, we would ensure complete solubilization before quantification and loading an equal amount of total protein per lane.

    The solubility percentages of the uninduced cultures were implausible, due to the quantification problems described above, and were therefore excluded from the analysis. The induced cultures gave more plausible values, although solubility was low overall. AKR1D1-SUMO had the lowest solubility (\~10%), and AKR1D1-TrxA the highest (\~26%). Given the issues with the Bradford assay and band quantification, these values should be considered estimations.

    AKR1D1-TrxA is the most promising candidate for continued work among AKR1D1 constructs. However, solubility alone does not establish the best construct, since soluble yield and enzymatic activity also matter, and the quantification problems this week make the values approximate. Further experiments are needed to confirm these results, to compare soluble yield and activity across different constructs along with testing expression and activity under hypoxic conditions, as this experiment was performed aerobically.

    The testosterone reduction assays were inconclusive, as the empty pET-11a control samples have also shown a decrease in NADPH concentration. Possible explanations include native *E. coli* reductases which might have been purified by accident that use NADPH as a cofactor, or cross-sample contamination. The assays need to be repeated, with additional controls, such as heat-inactivated proteins, and taking extra care not to contaminate samples during purification.
