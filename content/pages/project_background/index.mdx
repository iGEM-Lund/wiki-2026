## Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome and Hyperandrogenism

**Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome** (PMOS), formerly known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), is one of the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders affecting women of reproductive age. 

The recent transition from the term PCOS to PMOS reflects an important conceptual shift in the field: the syndrome is no longer regarded primarily as a disorder of ovarian morphology, but rather as a systemic condition involving endocrine, metabolic, reproductive, dermatological, and psychological manifestations. This renaming is particularly relevant because the previous term “polycystic ovary syndrome” placed disproportionate emphasis on ovarian cysts, which are neither universally present nor sufficient to explain the broad clinical phenotype of the disorder. In contrast, the term PMOS better captures the multi-hormonal and metabolic nature of the condition.&#x20;

PMOS represents a **major global health burden**. Epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 10–13% of women worldwide are affected, while up to 70% of cases may remain undiagnosed. This diagnostic gap reflects both the heterogeneity of the syndrome and the historical under-recognition of women’s endocrine and metabolic health conditions. Clinically, PMOS is characterized by variable combinations of ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology, after exclusion of other endocrine disorders. However, the impact of the syndrome extends far beyond reproductive function. Women with PMOS may experience irregular menstrual cycles, anovulation, infertility, hirsutism, acne, androgenic alopecia, weight gain, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, and reduced quality of life. Psychological comorbidities, including anxiety, depression, and body-image distress, are also frequently reported, further supporting the interpretation of PMOS as a complex, lifelong condition rather than a purely reproductive disorder.

\
Among the different features of PMOS, **hyperandrogenism** is one of the most clinically and biologically relevant. Androgen excess contributes directly to dermatological symptoms such as hirsutism, acne, and alopecia, but it also interferes with follicular development, ovulation, and endocrine homeostasis. At the ovarian level, excess androgens can impair normal folliculogenesis, contributing to the accumulation of immature follicles and chronic anovulation. At the systemic level, hyperandrogenism is closely associated with insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysfunction, creating a reinforcing cycle between endocrine imbalance and metabolic impairment. This interaction is central to the pathophysiology of PMOS: insulin resistance can increase ovarian androgen production and reduce sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), thereby increasing the fraction of bioavailable androgens. In turn, elevated androgen levels may further contribute to adipose tissue dysfunction, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation.

## **Existing Treatment Options**

**Current therapeutic** strategies for PMOS are mainly symptom-oriented and depend on the patient’s clinical priorities, such as menstrual regulation, treatment of hyperandrogenic symptoms, fertility, or metabolic risk reduction. International guidelines recommend lifestyle interventions as a core component of management, while combined oral contraceptive pills are commonly used for menstrual irregularity and clinical hyperandrogenism. Metformin is frequently used for metabolic indications, particularly in the context of insulin resistance, while anti-androgens may be considered in selected cases when other options are insufficient or contraindicated. Although these approaches can provide important benefits, they do not cure PMOS and were not designed to directly treat women with the syndrome. Moreover, recent evidence assessments have highlighted that, despite short-term benefits of several pharmacological options, major gaps remain regarding their long-term efficacy, safety, and impact on quality of life. Therefore, there is a strong rationale for exploring complementary strategies that target mechanistic contributors to hyperandrogenism and metabolic dysfunction.

In recent years, the **gut microbiome** has emerged as a potential contributor to PMOS pathophysiology. The intestinal microbiota is increasingly recognized as an active metabolic and endocrine interface capable of influencing host energy metabolism, immune signaling, bile acid turnover, and steroid hormone homeostasis. Several studies have reported alterations in gut microbial diversity and composition in women with PCOS/PMOS, and these changes have been associated with hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance, and inflammatory markers. 

In this context, the **gut microbiome** has emerged **as a compelling target**. A growing body of evidence suggests that the gut microbiome is not only associated with PMOS, but may also play a functional role in steroid metabolism and androgen regulation. Microbial steroid-modifying enzymes can reshape intestinal pools of androgens and related metabolites, and recent studies indicate that these activities may also influence circulating androgen levels, suggesting that microbial steroid metabolism may contribute to systemic androgen tone.

\
**Testosterone** and related androgens are produced endogenously by the ovaries, adrenal glands, and peripheral tissues, and circulate in blood either bound to proteins such as SHBG and albumin or in a free bioactive fraction. The liver plays a central role in androgen metabolism, where steroid hormones undergo reactions (phase I and phase II) that increase their polarity and facilitate excretion. Phase II conjugation, including **glucuronidation** and **sulfation**, generates more hydrophilic androgen metabolites that can be eliminated through urine or secreted into bile and delivered to the intestine. However, conjugated steroids entering the gut lumen are not necessarily irreversibly eliminated. Microbial enzymes can deconjugate and chemically transform these molecules, potentially regenerating bioactive steroids or producing new steroid metabolites that may be reabsorbed into circulation through enterohepatic recirculation.

The concept of the “**testobolome**” has recently been proposed to describe gut microbes and microbial genes involved in testosterone metabolism. The testobolome includes microbial enzymes such as β-glucuronidases, sulfatases, hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases, and steroid reductases. β-glucuronidases and sulfatases can remove conjugated groups from testosterone glucuronide and testosterone sulfate, respectively, generating free testosterone. Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases can interconvert testosterone, androstenedione, and downstream oxidized or reduced metabolites. In addition, microbial reductases can catalyze structural modifications of the steroid A-ring, including 5α- and 5β-reduction. These reactions may alter androgen receptor activity, steroid stability, and the likelihood of reabsorption. Importantly, many reductive reactions are favored under anaerobic gut-like conditions, m5aking the intestinal lumen a biologically plausible site for local steroid modification.

\
This microbiome-mediated steroid metabolism is particularly relevant in the context of PMOS because hyperandrogenism is a central driver of symptoms and disease progression. If intestinal microbial activities contribute to the regeneration, persistence, or structural conversion of androgenic metabolites, then the gut may represent a previously underexplored site for localized intervention. However, most current PMOS treatments act systemically and are not designed to modulate intestinal steroid metabolism directly, this creates an opportunity for synthetic biology approaches aimed at engineering controllable, localized systems capable of modifying steroid bioactivity within the gut environment.
