***We began with a simple question: could synthetic biology offer a new way of approaching Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)?***

From there, we had to decide where our contribution could be most meaningful. PMOS is a complex and highly heterogeneous condition, with symptoms and priorities that vary between individuals and  no single intervention can address every aspect of it. We therefore looked for a feature that was both biologically relevant and important to the people affected. Review of the literature and patient stories led us to hyperandrogenism. Androgen excess contributes directly to dermatological features but interferes also at the ovarian and more broadely at the systemic level, creating a reinforcing cycle between endocrine imbalance and metabolic impairment. Hyperandrogenism therefore became the focus of our project and the starting point for our engineering.

The project begins with the people who spent years trying to have the complexity of their condition recognised. Their experiences remind us that developing a new solution is not only a question of whether it can work biologically. It must also respond to a real need and be described, tested and communicated with greater precision than the problem it is intended to address.&#x20;

## Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome and Hyperandrogenism

**Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome** (PMOS), formerly known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), is one of the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders affecting women of reproductive age. 

The recent transition from the term PCOS to PMOS reflects an important conceptual shift in the field: the syndrome is no longer regarded primarily as a disorder of ovarian morphology, but rather as a systemic condition involving endocrine, metabolic, reproductive, dermatological, and psychological manifestations.

PMOS represents a **major global health burden**. Epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 10–13% of women worldwide are affected, while up to 70% of cases may remain undiagnosed.&#x20;

**The burden often begins before a diagnosis is made.**

In an international survey of 1.385 women reporting a diagnosis of PMOS, 33.6% had waited more than two years for a diagnosis, and 47.1% had consulted at least three healthcare professionals. These findings illustrate how prolonged uncertainty can become part of living with the condition.&#x20;

This diagnostic gap reflects both the heterogeneity of the syndrome and the historical under-recognition of women’s endocrine and metabolic health conditions. Clinically, PMOS is characterized by variable combinations of ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology, after exclusion of other endocrine disorders. Women with PMOS may experience irregular menstrual cycles, anovulation, infertility, hirsutism, acne, androgenic alopecia, weight gain, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, and reduced quality of life. Clinical features of PMOS can also carry social consequences with psychological comorbidities, including anxiety, depression, and body-image distress, being frequently reported.

\
Within this broader picture, hyperandrogenism is relevant to our project, because it connects a measurable biological process with symptoms that can matter greatly to those affected. Understanding its relationship with ovarian function and metabolism help explain why we choose andcrogenic activity as our experimental focus. At the same time diversity of pmos means that reducing androgenic activity would address only part of the condition, and its value would ultimately need to be assessed through outcomes meaningful to patients .

Hyperandrogenism is a medical condition defined by an excess of androgen hormones, such as testosterone. This particular feature of PMOS contributes directly to dermatological symptoms such as hirsutism, acne, and alopecia, but it also interferes with follicular development, ovulation, and endocrine homeostasis. At the ovarian level, excess androgens can impair normal folliculogenesis, contributing to the accumulation of immature follicles and chronic anovulation. At the systemic level, hyperandrogenism is closely associated with insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysfunction, creating a reinforcing cycle between endocrine imbalance and metabolic impairment. This interaction is central to the pathophysiology of PMOS: insulin resistance can increase ovarian androgen production and reduce sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), thereby increasing the fraction of bioavailable androgens. In turn, elevated androgen levels may further contribute to adipose tissue dysfunction, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation.

**Current therapeutic** strategies for PMOS are mainly symptom-oriented and depend on the patient’s clinical priorities, such as menstrual regulation, treatment of hyperandrogenic symptoms, fertility, or metabolic risk reduction. International guidelines recommend lifestyle interventions as a core component of management. Although these approaches can provide important benefits, they do not cure PMOS and were not designed to directly treat women with the syndrome. Moreover, recent evidence assessments have highlighted that major gaps remain regarding their long-term efficacy, safety, and impact on quality of life.&#x20;

## **Gut-Androgen Axis**

In recent years, the **gut microbiome** has emerged as a potential contributor to PMOS pathophysiology. The intestinal microbiota is increasingly recognized as an active metabolic and endocrine interface capable of influencing host energy metabolism, immune signaling, bile acid turnover, and steroid hormone homeostasis. Several studies have reported alterations in gut microbial diversity and composition in women with PCOS/PMOS, and these changes have been associated with hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance, and inflammatory markers. 

In this context, the **gut microbiome** has emerged **as a compelling target**. A growing body of evidence suggests that the gut microbiome is not only associated with PMOS, but may also play a functional role in steroid metabolism and androgen regulation. Microbial steroid-modifying enzymes can reshape intestinal pools of androgens and related metabolites, and recent studies indicate that these activities may also influence circulating androgen levels, suggesting that microbial steroid metabolism may contribute to systemic androgen tone.

**Testosterone** and related androgens are produced endogenously by the ovaries, adrenal glands, and peripheral tissues, and circulate in blood either bound to proteins such as SHBG and albumin or in a free bioactive fraction. The liver plays a central role in androgen metabolism, where steroid hormones undergo reactions (phase I and phase II) that increase their polarity and facilitate excretion. Phase II conjugation, including **glucuronidation** and **sulfation**, generates more hydrophilic androgen metabolites that can be eliminated through urine or secreted into bile and delivered to the intestine. However, conjugated steroids entering the gut lumen are not necessarily irreversibly eliminated. Microbial enzymes can deconjugate and chemically transform these molecules, potentially regenerating bioactive steroids or producing new steroid metabolites that may be reabsorbed into circulation through enterohepatic recirculation.

The concept of the “**testobolome**” has recently been proposed to describe gut microbes and microbial genes involved in testosterone metabolism. The testobolome includes different microbial enzymes such as steroid reductases that can catalyze structural modifications of the steroid A-ring, including 5α- and **5β-reduction**. These reactions may alter androgen receptor activity, steroid stability, and the likelihood of reabsorption.
