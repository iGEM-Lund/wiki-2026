## **Overview**

**AnTigone** targets hyperandrogenism driven by the gut-androgen axis, a pathway in which colonic bacteria convert steroid precursors into bioactive androgens, particularly testosterone, which are then reabsorbed into circulation. Our dry-lab work asks which engineered enzyme variants are worth building to reduce testosterone, and how those variants would be expected to behave once placed in a living bacterial system.&#x20;

Two enzymes are central to this axis. **AKR1D1** is the human <Tooltip text="Of the liver.">hepatic</Tooltip> <Tooltip text="An enzyme that adds hydrogen to a steroid that has a double bond at position 4 and a ketone (C=O) at position 3.">**Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductase**</Tooltip>, a member of the <Tooltip text="A large family of enzymes that reduce aldehydes and ketones using NADPH.">aldo-keto reductase superfamily</Tooltip>, which inactivates androgens and <Tooltip text="A class of steroid hormones, such as cortisol, that regulate metabolism and the immune response.">glucocorticoids</Tooltip> in the liver and initiates <Tooltip text="The liver's production of bile acids, which help digest fats.">bile acid synthesis</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2014" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="faucher-et-al-2008" narrative={false} />).

**ci2350** is a bacterial Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductase from *Clostridium innocuum*, identified by <Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative /> as a member of the <Tooltip text="Flavin-dependent enzymes that reduce carbon-carbon double bonds.">**Old Yellow Enzyme family (COG1902)**</Tooltip>, a flavin-dependent ene-reductase that couples steroid reduction to NADH oxidation under the <Tooltip text="Conditions without oxygen.">anaerobic conditions</Tooltip> of the gut. It has been shown to reduce progesterone, cortisone and hydrocortisone directly, and *C. innocuum* extracts have been reported to reduce testosterone (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="stokes-et-al-1985" narrative={false} />).

The two enzymes are not <Tooltip text="Sharing a common evolutionary origin.">homologous</Tooltip>: they belong to unrelated families and have converged independently on the same 5β-reduction chemistry, with AKR1D1 acting on Δ4-3-ketosteroids spanning across <Tooltip text="Steroids grouped by how many carbon atoms they have; C21 steroids include progesterone and cortisol.">**C18** to **C21 classes**</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2011" narrative={false} />), while ci2350 has been shown to reduce **C21 steroids** (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />).

### **The selectivity problem**

The capacity of both enzymes to accept testosterone as a substrate makes it a candidate for engineering, but <Tooltip text="The natural, unmodified version of an enzyme.">wild-type</Tooltip> activity alone is insufficient. Effective therapeutic application requires that the enzyme preferentially reduces testosterone over structurally similar steroids, and progesterone is the most demanding comparison. The two molecules share the same core steroid scaffold and differ critically at a single position: testosterone carries a **17β-hydroxyl group**, whereas progesterone bears a **C17 acetyl substituent** (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2014" narrative={false} />). Any engineered selectivity must therefore be achieved by discriminating between these two chemically subtle substituents on an otherwise identical molecule.

This problem cannot be addressed experimentally without first knowing which variants are structurally worth testing. The sequence space of possible <Tooltip text="The pocket of an enzyme where the substrate binds and is converted.">active site</Tooltip> mutations is far larger than can be screened at the bench, so a computational filter is needed to narrow it to a tractable set.

Binding affinity alone, however, does not predict how a variant would behave in a bacterial system. **Cell growth, enzyme expression levels, and competition between substrates all shape the outcome**, and a variant that binds testosterone well in isolation may still perform poorly when those factors are considered.

## **Our approach**

Our dry lab work addresses both challenges through two integrated strands.

1. **Enzyme specificity:** Perform <Tooltip text="Choosing specific mutations based on the protein's structure, rather than mutating at random.">rational mutagenesis</Tooltip> on ci2350 and AKR1D1 that make the enzyme prefer testosterone over progesterone, using <Tooltip text="A computer simulation that predicts how well a molecule fits into a protein's binding site.">molecular docking</Tooltip> to guide which residues to change.
2. **Mathematical modeling:** Build a mechanistic model of bacterial growth, enzyme expression and competitive kinetics that uses the docking results to predict how each variant would perform in practice and which parameters most affect the prediction.

  Together, these form a connected predictive layer: the first narrows a **large design space to a small set of structurally plausible candidates**, and the second takes those candidates and asks **whether their effect would actually show up in a real bacterial system**.

### **Structural Analysis and Active-Site Mapping**

#### **Protein structure acquisition**

Three-dimensional structures for the two candidate enzymes were obtained through different routes, reflecting the availability of experimental data for each. The human AKR1D1 structure was retrieved from the <Tooltip text="A public database of experimentally determined 3D structures of proteins.">RCSB Protein Data Bank</Tooltip> ([PDB entry 3CAV](https://www.rcsb.org/structure/3CAV)) (<Citation reference="berman-et-al-2000" narrative={false} />), resolved at <Tooltip text="Ångström, a unit of length equal to one ten-billionth of a metre. A smaller number means a sharper structure.">1.90 Å</Tooltip> in complex with NADPH (<Citation reference="faucher-et-al-2008" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

No experimentally determined structure was available for ci2350. A three-dimensional model was generated using <Tooltip text="An AI program that predicts a protein's 3D structure from its amino acid sequence.">AlphaFold</Tooltip> v2.2.0 (<Citation reference="jumper-et-al-2021" narrative={false} />), with the input sequence taken from Arp et al., 2025. Per-residue confidence scores (<Tooltip text="AlphaFold's per-residue confidence score from 0 to 100. Above 90 is very high.">pLDDT</Tooltip>) exceeded 90 (pLDDT > 90, very high) across the model, confirming that the predicted structure met the threshold for structure-based analysis.

#### **Structure preparation**

All structures were prepared following the standard <Tooltip text="Free docking software that predicts how a small molecule fits into a protein.">AutoDock</Tooltip> receptor preparation protocol (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />). Water molecules and non-essential <Tooltip text="Atoms other than carbon and hydrogen, such as water, ions or bound molecules in a structure file.">heteroatoms</Tooltip> were removed. NADPH was present in the PDB structure of AKR1D1 and was retained to preserve the biologically relevant binding geometry. For ci2350, no cofactor is present, since it is a predicted structure.&#x20;

Hydrogen atoms were added, and <Tooltip text="Whether each chemical group carries an extra hydrogen ion at a given pH.">protonation states</Tooltip> were assigned at **physiological pH (7.4)**, consistent with standard receptor preparation for molecular docking (<Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />). Structures were then converted to <Tooltip text="The file format AutoDock uses for docking inputs, storing atom positions, charges and rotatable bonds.">PDBQT</Tooltip> format using MGLTools (prepare\_receptor4.py) (<Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />), which assigns <Tooltip text="A fast method for estimating the electric charge on each atom.">Gasteiger partial charges</Tooltip> and defines rotatable bonds for the docking software.

#### **Ligand preparation**

Testosterone and progesterone share the four-ring steroid scaffold, with rings labeled A to D from the end bearing the C3 ketone (<Citation reference="the-nomenclature-of-steroids-1989" narrative={false} />). They are identical across rings A to C, including the Δ4-3-keto motif on the A ring that is the site of reduction, and differ only at the C17 substituent on the D ring, with 17β-hydroxyl in testosterone and an acetyl group in progesterone (<Ref target="mod-zqfnc" /> and <Ref target="mod-az7ct" />).

Both <Tooltip text="The small molecules that bind to a protein; here the steroids.">ligands</Tooltip> were retrieved from <Tooltip text="A public database of chemical molecules and their structures.">PubChem</Tooltip> ([CID 6013](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/6013) and [CID 5994](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/5994), respectively) (<Citation reference="kim-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />) as three-dimensional SDF structures. Each ligand was processed using MGLTools (prepare\_ligand4.py) (<Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />) to assign partial charges and define rotatable bonds, generating PDBQT files for docking input. No additional <Tooltip text="Adjusting a molecule's shape to the arrangement with the lowest energy.">energy minimization</Tooltip> was performed prior to docking, as AutoDock Vina performs internal <Tooltip text="Trying out many shapes (conformations) of the molecule.">conformational sampling</Tooltip> through multiple independent search runs during the docking process (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" narrative={false} />).

#### **Conserved domain analysis**

The sequence of each candidate enzyme was submitted to the NCBI [Conserved Domain Search (CD Search)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) (<Citation reference="marchler-bauer-et-al-2017" narrative={false} />), which compares a query sequence against the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) to identify evolutionarily <Tooltip text="Stretches of protein found across a family of related proteins, usually because they matter for function.">conserved functional domains</Tooltip> and their annotated residue positions. These annotations cover substrate binding, cofactor coordination and catalytic function, and are inferred from structurally and biochemically characterized members of the same protein family. They provided a sequence-level map of the expected functional site and served as the reference for interpreting and validating the predicted active site pockets.

#### &#x20;For AKR1D1:&#x20;

CD Search assigned the query sequence ([NP\_005980.1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/NP_005980.1/)) to the AKR\_AKR1D1-3 family within the aldo-keto reductase superfamily, returning 32 annotated active site positions and a separately annotated <Tooltip text="The four residues that together carry out the chemical reaction.">catalytic tetrad (</Tooltip><Ref target="fig-rqmx8" /> and <Ref target="tab-vaps6" />).

The enzyme uses NADPH and a conserved catalytic tetrad of D53, Y58, K87 and E120 to deliver a <Tooltip text="A hydrogen atom carrying two electrons, which the enzyme passes to the steroid.">hydride</Tooltip> to the Δ4 double bond on the steroid A-ring. Y58 acts as the general acid, with K87 and D53 tuning its acidity. E120 is what distinguishes AKR1D1 from the rest of the superfamily: it replaces the histidine conserved in all other AKRs and acts as a superacid, polarizing the C3 ketone (<Citation reference="di-costanzo-et-al-2008" narrative={false} />).

#### **Active site identification**

Binding pockets were identified computationally using fpocket v4.0 (<Citation reference="le-guilloux-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />), which detects cavities through <Tooltip text="Geometric methods: the first divides space into regions around each atom, the second fits spheres into the gaps between atoms. Groups of neighbouring spheres mark a cavity.">Voronoi tessellation and alpha-sphere clustering</Tooltip> and returns a ranked list of candidate pockets, each described by geometric and physicochemical descriptors. These predicted pockets were then cross-referenced against residue annotations from NCBI CD Search to identify which pocket best corresponded to the known catalytic and substrate-binding residues of each enzyme family. The pocket showing the greatest agreement with the annotated functional site was selected.

#### For AKR1D1:

fpocket identified 22 candidate pockets in the AKR1D1 structure. Cross-referencing against the CD Search annotations showed a single dominant match: Pocket 1 contained 29 of the 32 annotated active site residues and all four members of the catalytic tetrad (<Ref target="tab-ogugt" />). The remaining pockets carried five or fewer annotated residues each, and twelve contained none. Pocket 1 was therefore selected as the functional active site.

The box center was taken as the mean x, y and z coordinates of the pocket’s alpha spheres, and each dimension was set from the span of those coordinates along that axis (maximum – minimum) plus a 4 Å margin to ensure sufficient room for the ligand to sample orientations within a tightly enclosed pocket.

### **Establishing the Selectivity Baseline** &#x20;

#### **Molecular docking**&#x20;

Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.2 (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" narrative={false} />) with an <Tooltip text="A docking setting: higher values search more thoroughly.">exhaustiveness</Tooltip> setting of 32 and an energy range of 4 kcal/mol. Vina combines a <Tooltip text="Involving randomness, so results can differ slightly from run to run.">stochastic</Tooltip> global search for scoring, which samples ligand position, orientation and <Tooltip text="The angles at which parts of a molecule can rotate around its bonds.">torsion angles</Tooltip>, with local gradient-based optimization that refines promising candidates into nearby energy minima.

Each ligand was docked independently into each prepared enzyme structure, and the top-ranked pose was recorded along with its predicted binding affinity in kcal/mol. To establish a selectivity baseline, we defined a **selectivity score** as the difference in predicted affinity between testosterone and progesterone, <Formula latex="ΔG(testosterone) – ΔG(progesterone)" />. A **negative value indicates that testosterone** is preferred by the enzyme. The inherent stochastic variability of the Vina scoring function, approximately ±0.5 kcal/mol, was treated as the noise threshold (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" narrative={false} />), and selectivity differences below this value were not considered meaningful.

After docking, the two enzymes gave comparable absolute affinities but opposite directions of preference (<Ref target="tab-sznaf" />).&#x20;

  | **AKR1D1** | −9.8                       | −9.3                       | +0.5                        | Progesterone   |

This is consistent with AKR1D1's biological role, where progesterone intermediates are the primary physiological substrates. #### **Contact analysis and residue identification**

From the top-ranked docking pose, all enzyme residues within 4 Å of each docked ligand were identified. This is a standard cut-off for direct protein–ligand contacts and captures residues involved in <Tooltip text="Weak attractions between atoms that are very close to each other.">van der Waals</Tooltip> interactions and short-range <Tooltip text="Attraction or repulsion between electrically charged groups.">electrostatics</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="le-guilloux-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />). Residues in proximity to the C17 position were given particular attention, as this is the only position at which the two substrates differ, as discussed previously in the ligand preparation (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2014" narrative={false} />).

#### For AKR1D1:

Both substrates share an identical Δ4-3-keto motif on the A ring, so the residues recognizing that end cannot distinguish between them. Hence, the six shared contacts, two of which (Y58, E120) are the catalytic tetrad (<Ref target="tab-53c3s" />), confirm that both ligands dock in a catalytically productive orientation.

Progesterone contacts two hydrophobic residues, **V309** and **L311**, which can form a nonpolar surface that accommodates its acetyl group, while testosterone contacts **I57**, which could offer a <Tooltip text="A weak attraction between a hydrogen atom on one molecule and an oxygen or nitrogen atom on another.">hydrogen bond</Tooltip> for the 17β-hydroxyl. V309 and L311 were targeted against progesterone, while I57 favors testosterone to increase affinity. All four were mutated singly and in combination.

#### For ci2350:

As in AKR1D1, the shared contacts cluster around the conserved Δ4-3-keto end of the molecule and include the catalytic residue, indicating that both ligands adopt a productive orientation. Y167 was excluded from mutagenesis, since altering it would be expected to compromise catalysis rather than selectivity.

**A107, R215** and **V263** appear to line the space progesterone’s acetyl group occupies (<Ref target="tab-zkjil" />). R215 is the most interesting of the three: the other two are small hydrophobic residues, but arginine can donate hydrogen bonds, so its contact may be a specific polar interaction rather than a shape fit, which would make it the cleanest target, along with **V59, F71, Q100, M254** and **L259** to increase the specificity towards testosterone.

#### **Rational mutagenesis**

Rational mutagenesis is a protein engineering approach in which specific amino acid substitutions are chosen in advance from structural and mechanistic knowledge of the enzyme, rather than generating them at random and screening (<Citation reference="lutz-et-al-2010" narrative={false} />).

The residues in <Ref target="tab-53c3s" /> and <Ref target="tab-zkjil" /> were substituted on physicochemical grounds, altering the size, polarity or hydrogen-bonding capacity of the pocket near C17 in a direction consistent with a shift towards testosterone (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" narrative={false} />). Both single-point variants and multi-residue combinations were designed for both enzymes.

#### For AKR1D1:

At V309 and L311, the substitutions cover three different mechanisms: serine (S) and threonine (T) shrink the <Tooltip text="The part of an amino acid that differs between the 20 types and gives it its properties.">side chain</Tooltip> and add polarity to the pocket; tyrosine (Y), phenylalanine (F) and tryptophan (W) fill the cavity so the acetyl group has less room; and glutamate (E) and lysine (K) place a charge against a neutral substituent (<Ref target="tab-6z476" />). Comparing them should indicate which property actually governs discrimination towards progesterone.

At I57, all five substitutions replace an <Tooltip text="Made of plain carbon chains, without charges or rings.">aliphatic</Tooltip> side chain with one capable of hydrogen bonding, any of which could pair with testosterone’s 17β-hydroxyl. Combinations test whether the two mutation effects are independent. If they are, the selectivity shifts should roughly sum.

At A107 and V263, the substitutions add volume, narrowing the space progesterone’s acetyl group occupies. For R215, the approach is removal rather than bulk. ### **Screening and mutant analysis**

#### **Stability screening**

Structural stability was evaluated using PyRosetta (<Citation reference="chaudhury-et-al-2010" narrative={false} />) with the ref2015 scoring function (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" narrative={false} />). The ref2015 function returns a single score summing van der Waals packing, hydrogen bonding, electrostatics, <Tooltip text="Treating the surrounding water as a continuous medium instead of modelling individual water molecules.">implicit solvation</Tooltip> and backbone and sidechain torsional strain, with lower, more negative values corresponding to a better-packed and more stable structure.

Local <Tooltip text="Re-optimising the positions of nearby amino acid side chains after a mutation.">sidechain repacking</Tooltip> was performed within a 5.0 Å radius of the mutated position, capturing the first layer of residues most likely to be directly affected by the substitution (<Citation reference="chaudhury-et-al-2010" narrative={false} />). Stability was expressed as the difference between mutant and wild-type <Formula latex="(ΔΔG = E(mutant) − E(wild-type))" />, cancelling the common baseline and isolating the effect of the substitution. A high positive ΔΔG indicates that the mutation destabilizes the structure (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" narrative={false} />).

Variants with ΔΔG above +5 <Tooltip text="Rosetta Energy Units, the score PyRosetta uses. Lower means more stable.">REU</Tooltip> were excluded; those below this threshold were considered structurally acceptable and advanced to docking (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="kellogg-et-al-2011" narrative={false} />). The threshold is deliberately permissive. It is set to reject severely destabilized variants, such as unresolvable <Tooltip text="Atoms placed so close together that they overlap and push each other apart.">steric clashes</Tooltip> or lost buried hydrogen bonds.

## **Mathematical modeling of enzyme production and activity**

Biological systems combine processes that run at different speeds and affect one another, so the outcome of an experiment is rarely obvious from the individual parts. Mathematical models address this by describing each process with an equation whose terms correspond to defined biological events (<Citation reference="kraikivski-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />). These equations are <Tooltip text="Equations describing how quantities change continuously over time.">Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs)</Tooltip>, solved numerically. Writing the system this way states every assumption openly and lets us change one parameter at a time, which is rarely practical at the bench (<Citation reference="alon-et-al-2006" narrative={false} />).

The specificity analysis found no AKR1D1 variant that shifted preference towards testosterone, while ci2350 produced several promising candidates, so the **model was built around ci2350 alone**. These findings are described on the [Results page](/results#enzyme-specificity-engineering).

### **Integrated growth and expression model**

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/dry-lab-3d-model/integrated-model.avif" alt="Image of workflow" caption="Workflow of the integrated growth and expression model. Growth data and literature values define the parameters of two coupled layers, which are solved numerically and then tested by parameter sweeps and Monte Carlo sampling." numbered refId="fig-ldjtv" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

#### **System definition**

The system modelled is ci2350 produced from a Twist <Tooltip text="A common plasmid for producing proteins in E. coli from a T7 promoter.">pET vector</Tooltip> in *E. coli* <Tooltip text="An E. coli strain made for high-level protein production.">*BL21(DE3)*</Tooltip>, with the gene under the <Tooltip text="A strong promoter read by T7 RNA polymerase, which BL21(DE3) makes when IPTG is added.">T7 promoter</Tooltip> and induced with 0.8 mM <Tooltip text="A lab chemical that switches on the T7 system.">IPTG</Tooltip>. The quantity of interest is the amount of enzyme per cell because it determines how much enzyme is available to act on the steroids. Described in [Parts](/parts) page.

#### **Growth model**

A batch culture in a closed flask grows exponentially while nutrients are plentiful and then slows as they are consumed. The <Tooltip text="A growth equation that starts exponential and levels off as the culture fills its container.">logistic equation</Tooltip> is the simplest description of this behavior (<Citation reference="verhulst-et-al-1838" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dX/dt = µmax · X · (1 − X/K)
"
/>

X is the culture density (<Tooltip text="Light absorbance at 600 nm, used to measure how dense a bacterial culture is.">OD600</Tooltip>), µmax the maximum specific growth rate (per hour) and K the <Tooltip text="The maximum culture density the medium can support.">carrying capacity</Tooltip>, the OD600 at which growth stops. The factor <Formula latex="(1 − X/K)" /> falls from 1 to 0 as the culture fills, so it acts as the brake on growth. A <Tooltip text="The initial period before bacteria start growing.">lag phase</Tooltip> was not included, so the simulated culture starts to grow immediately from the starting density.

From our experimental data, the cells **double** **every 28 minutes** (µmax = 1.48 h⁻¹). We did not measure the plateau, so K was set to OD600 15 (<Ref target="tab-vzdvj" />), a plausible density for a rich medium, and its influence is examined in the <Tooltip text="Changing one input at a time to see how much the result depends on it.">sensitivity analysis</Tooltip>. From these parameters, the growth saturates near 7 h.

#### **Expression model**

Since <Tooltip text="Messenger RNA, the copy of a gene that the cell reads to make a protein.">mRNA</Tooltip> and the enzyme behave differently inside the cell, we examined them separately. m is the mRNA level in the cell and E the enzyme level, both in relative units. mRNA is short-lived: messages are transcribed at rate α and degraded at rate δm, so the cell maintains a small working pool at a steady level, giving the <Tooltip text="The cell's protein-making machines.">ribosomes</Tooltip> a constant supply to work from (<Citation reference="alon-et-al-2006" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dm/dt = α − δm · m
"
/>

**At steady-state maintenance**

<FormulaBlock
  latex="m* = α / δm 
"
/>

The enzyme is the opposite. It is built from those messages at rate β. We assume it is stable, so nothing degrades it, and the only loss is growth itself. At each division, the enzyme already made is shared between two daughter cells, so the faster the culture grows, the more it dilutes its own protein (<Citation reference="klumpp-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />). This is where the two models are integrated: dilution takes the culture’s current growth rate, <Formula latex="µ(t) = µmax · (1 − X/K)" />, rather than a fixed value. Enzyme therefore stays low while the culture divides fast and accumulates as growth slows (<Citation reference="klumpp-et-al-2009" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dE/dt = β · m − µ(t) · E = β · m − µmax · (1 − X/K) · E
"
/>

<Card refId="card-q6eoq">
  **Five assumptions:**

  1. ci2350’s mRNA turns over like a typical *E. coli* transcript, since its <Tooltip text="The time it takes for half of the molecules to break down.">half-life</Tooltip> has not been measured.
  2. The promoter is fully induced at 0.8 mM IPTG, so transcription runs at a constant rate.
  3. The enzyme is stable, so dilution by growth is its only loss.
  4. Expression does not burden the cell, so it does not slow growth.
  5. mRNA and enzyme are in relative units, since absolute counts would require data we do not have.
</Card>

Bacterial messages break down within minutes of being made (<Citation reference="bernstein-et-al-2002" narrative={false} />). We used a half-life (3 to 8 mins) to predict the decay constants <Formula latex="(δ = ln2 / t½)" /> range and used their average of 9.53 h⁻¹. The transcription rate (α) and translation rate (β) are placeholders, as neither has been measured for ci2350 (<Ref target="tab-aqmwv" />).

#### **Mathematical simulation and sensitivity of the enzyme level**

The integrated three-equation system was solved in R using the deSolve package (<Citation reference="soetaert-et-al-2010" narrative={false} />), with the <Tooltip text="A standard numerical method for solving differential equations.">Runge–Kutta</Tooltip> method ode45 over **24 h in 100 time steps**, starting from **X = 0.05, m = 0** and **E = 0**. Since several parameters are assumptions or literature ranges rather than measurements, we then asked how much the result depends on them, and which the experiments should pin down first (<Citation reference="saltelli-et-al-2008" narrative={false} />). Each of K, µmax, α and δm was swept by **±20% around its baseline** individually, a standard window for local sensitivity, and the whole system was re-run at every step, recording culture density, mRNA level and enzyme level after 24 h as a percentage change from baseline. Because all three are recorded together, a single run shows how much the enzyme level moves and how a growth parameter affects expression, and vice versa. β was not varied separately since the enzyme level depends on α and β only through their product.

#### **Uncertainty analysis of the enzyme level**

Sweeps change one parameter at a time, whereas in practice all of them are uncertain together. We therefore ran a <Tooltip text="Repeating a calculation many times with randomly drawn inputs to see how uncertain the result is.">Monte Carlo analysis</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="saltelli-et-al-2008" narrative={false} />), drawing **500 parameter sets at random**, each value taken from a distribution centred on its baseline with a spread of 10%, and recorded the enzyme level after 24 h for each. The **10% is an assumed level of uncertainty**, not a measured one.

The analysis was run three times: with only the growth parameters (K, µmax) varied, with only the expression parameters (α, δm) varied, and with all four varied together. Comparing the three shows which layer contributes most to the uncertainty in the enzyme level and we reported the mean and the 95% interval.

The results of both per-parameter sweep and Monte Carlo analyses of enzyme production are presented on the [Results page](/results#mathematical-modelling-of-enzyme-production-and-activity). The scripts for the integrated model are available on [GitLab](https://gitlab.igem.org/2026/software/lund/model).

### **Enzyme activity model**

#### **Activity model**

Testosterone and progesterone are close in shape and compete for the same active site of ci2350, so each slows down the conversion of the other. This is described by <Tooltip text="A model of how fast an enzyme works as the amount of substrate changes.">Michaelis–Menten kinetics</Tooltip> for two competing substrates. Each substrate has its own turnover rate, but the two rates share the same denominator, which makes the substrates compete (<Citation reference="michaelis-et-al-1913" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

<FormulaBlock
  latex="v_T = (kcat,T / Km,T) · Etot · T / (1 + T/Km,T + P/Km,P)
"
/>

<FormulaBlock
  latex="v_P = (kcat,P / Km,P) · Etot · P / (1 + T/Km,T + P/Km,P)
"
/>

T and P are the testosterone and progesterone concentrations (µM), and v\_T and v\_P are their rates of consumption (µM per hour). E\_tot is the enzyme concentration in the culture; Km,T and Km,P are the <Tooltip text="The substrate concentration at which an enzyme works at half its top speed. Lower means tighter binding.">Michaelis constants</Tooltip> (estimated from docking, see <Ref target="tab-xr0un" />), and kcat,T and kcat,P are the <Tooltip text="The number of substrate molecules one enzyme converts per second when fully busy.">turnover numbers</Tooltip>. The ratio kcat/Km measures how efficiently each substrate is used, so the preference of a variant is the ratio of kcat,T/Km,T to kcat,P/Km,P.

The enzyme availability comes from the integrated model of enzyme production: enzyme per cell multiplied by culture density, converted to a concentration by a single scaling factor (model assumption). The enzyme in the flask therefore rises as the culture grows, and continues to rise more slowly once growth stops, because the enzyme is no longer diluted.&#x20;

**Model Assumption:**

<FormulaBlock latex="Etot = Escale · E · X " />

**The three layers run together as one system of five equations:**&#x20;

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dX/dt = µmax · X · (1 − X/K)

"
/>

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dm/dt = α · sind(t) − δm · m
"
/>

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dE/dt = β · m − µmax · (1 − X/K) · E
"
/>

<FormulaBlock
  latex="dT/dt = −v_T · ssub(t)
"
/>

<FormulaBlock latex="dP/dt = −v_P · ssub(t)" />

The first three are the growth and expression layers described in the integrated model of enzyme production; the last two are the substrates being consumed. In the earlier model, the expression started at time zero. Here α is multiplied by s\_ind, so that mRNA is made only after IPTG is added, and a second switch, s\_sub(t), allows the steroids to be consumed only after they have been added. These switches replicates the bench protocol: s\_ind(t) turns transcription on when the culture reaches **OD600 0.5**, which the **growth model identifies at 1.58 h**, and s\_sub(t) turns substrate consumption on **four hours** later, when substrate is added and enough enzyme has built up. Both steroids start at **50 µM**, and the reaction is followed for eight hours (<Ref target="tab-i8ink" />).&#x20;

#### **Linking docking to Km**

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/dry-lab-3d-model/docking-to-km.avif" alt="Image of docking to Km" caption="From docking score to Km. Each docking score (ΔG) is converted to a binding constant, Kd = exp(ΔG/RT), and Km is assumed to follow Kd. The wild-type progesterone score is anchored to its measured Kd (0.432 µM), and each variant’s Km shifts by its score difference from wild type. A variant with a lower Km for testosterone than for progesterone prefers testosterone. This link between Km and Kd is an assumption, and docking errors are larger than most differences between variants." numbered refId="fig-z9s4i" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

The activity model needs a Michaelis constant (Km) for testosterone and progesterone for every ci2350 variant, but none has been published. The only kinetic information on the enzyme is the wild-type <Tooltip text="A measure of binding strength: a smaller value means tighter binding.">dissociation constant (Kd)</Tooltip> for progesterone, **0.432 µM**, measured by <Tooltip text="A lab method that measures binding by the heat it gives off.">isothermal titration calorimetry (</Tooltip><Ref target="fig-z9s4i" /><Tooltip text="A lab method that measures binding by the heat it gives off.">)</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />).

We therefore **propose** to use our docking results to estimate the missing values. Docking gives a binding energy ΔG, which relates to <Formula latex="Kd = exp(ΔG/RT)" /> (<Citation reference="gilson-et-al-2007" narrative={false} />). Here R is the gas constant and RT = 0.616 kcal/mol at 37 °C, so a more **negative ΔG** means tighter binding and a **smaller Kd**. Docking scores are not reliable as absolute energies, since Vina can deviate from experiment by 1 to 2 kcal/mol (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="trott-et-al-2010" narrative={false} />), so we used only the differences between scores calculated with the same protocol.

We took the wild type with progesterone as the reference point and gave it the measured value, 0.432 µM, as its Km. This treats **Km as proportional to Kd**, which is a **good approximation** when catalysis (kcat) is slower than the release of the steroid from the enzyme (<Citation reference="wilhelmova-1996" narrative={false} />). Whether this holds for ci2350 is not known, because no kcat has been measured. From the reference, the Km values of all variants follow in two steps:

1. Km for progesterone is scaled from the wild-type value by the difference between the progesterone scores of the variant and the wild type (<Ref target="tab-xr0un" />).
2. Testosterone has no measured value at all; its Km is calculated from the progesterone Km of the same variant, shifted by the difference between the two steroid scores of that variant (<Ref target="tab-xr0un" />).&#x20;

<FormulaBlock
  latex="Km,P(variant) = 0.432 µM · exp[(ΔG_P,variant − ΔG_P,wild type) / RT]
"
/>

<FormulaBlock
  latex="Km,T(variant) = Km,P(variant) · exp(ΔΔG_T–P / RT); ΔΔG_T–P = ΔG_T − ΔG_P
"

A negative ΔΔG\_T–P means that the variant binds testosterone more tightly than progesterone, which gives testosterone the lower Km. We assumed the same kcat for both steroids because docking describes binding and not catalysis. The Km values obtained this way are predictions that guide the experiments, not measured constants. The assumptions behind them (Km proportional to Kd, equal kcat) are tested in the sensitivity analysis and are the first targets of the planned kinetic measurements.

#### **Sensitivity analysis of activity model**

The activity layer adds more uncertain values, such as kcat, E\_scale, the wild-type Km,P and the docking scores, on top of the uncertain expression parameters, so two checks were run.

For the variant with the largest predicted preference, R215A + V263I (see [Results page](/results#enzyme-specificity-engineering)), each parameter was varied by ±20% with the others held at baseline, and the progesterone docking score was shifted by ±0.3 kcal/mol to see how a small docking error changes the outcome. The expression settings were swept more widely: α from 0.25 to 4 times baseline, β from 0.4 to 12 times, and the induction point from OD600 0.3 to 1.0.

#### **Uncertainty analysis of activity**

Most of the values in the model are guesses, so running it once would only tell us what happens if every guess is right. The Monte Carlo analysis instead ran the model 200 times for each variant, picking a different random value for each uncertain input every time.

Each variant was compared against a wild type given the same random expression and kcat values, so the only difference between the two was the docking scores. Every score was given a random error of about 1 kcal/mol, the typical accuracy of Vina, and the analysis was repeated with 0.5 kcal/mol as a more optimistic case. The other values were drawn over the ranges we consider plausible.

What comes out is a percentage for each variant: how often testosterone is preferred, and how often the variant beats the wild type. The wild type was also compared against a second random copy of itself, which shows how often a difference appears purely by chance when there is no real one.

The results of activity modeling are presented on the [Results page](/results#prediction-of-activity-model). The scripts for the integrated model are available on [GitLab](https://gitlab.igem.org/2026/software/lund/model).

## Limitations&#x20;

* **Single-run docking:** Vina’s <Tooltip text="The starting number of a random process; a different seed gives slightly different results.">random seed</Tooltip> gives about ±0.5 kcal/mol of variation, which is at or above most selectivity differences (0.1–0.5 kcal/mol). Averaging at least three runs per ligand–receptor pair would be needed to confirm them.
* **AlphaFold structure:** ci2350 has no experimental structure, so subtle side-chain positions in the active site may differ from the real enzyme.
* **Rigid receptor:** Vina keeps the protein rigid, so <Tooltip text="The way a protein reshapes slightly when a molecule binds.">induced fit</Tooltip> of flexible residues such as Q100 and R215 is not captured.
* **Fast stability scoring:** PyRosetta ΔΔG with local repacking only is an approximation, and a <Tooltip text="A Rosetta protocol that thoroughly adjusts the whole structure to find a lower-energy arrangement.">FastRelax</Tooltip> protocol would be more accurate.
* **Single-pose contacts:** The contact analysis used only the top-ranked pose (Mode 1), so the real binding mode may involve different residues.
* **Placeholder parameters:** The transcription rate, translation rate, carrying capacity and the enzyme amount per cell are placeholders, and the mRNA half-life is poorly known. The absolute times are therefore not predictions. Only the order of conversion and the trends across the sweeps can be trusted.
* **Km from docking:** Km values were derived from docking scores by assuming Km is proportional to Kd. This has not been tested for this enzyme.
* **Unmeasured kcat:** The turnover rate for each steroid is unknown and was assumed equal. A testosterone turnover about four times slower than progesterone would remove the advantage of R215A + V263I.
* **Missing cell processes:** Steroid uptake into the cell, solubility of the steroids and any toxicity to the cells are not modelled.
* **Incomplete uncertainty analysis:** The activity Monte Carlo did not vary the growth rate or carrying capacity, and some draws without a defined half-time were excluded.
