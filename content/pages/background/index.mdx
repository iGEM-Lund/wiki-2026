***We began with a simple question: could synthetic biology offer a new way of approaching Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)?***

From there, we had to decide where our contribution could be most meaningful. PMOS is a complex and highly heterogeneous condition, with symptoms and priorities that vary between individuals and no single intervention can address every aspect of it. We therefore looked for a feature that was both biologically relevant and important to the people affected. Review of the literature and patient stories led us to <Tooltip text="Higher than normal levels or effects of androgens, the male sex hormones such as testosterone.">**hyperandrogenism**</Tooltip>. Androgen excess contributes directly to dermatological features but interferes also at the ovarian and more broadly at the systemic level, creating a reinforcing cycle between <Tooltip text="The endocrine system is responsible for the release and control of hormones in the body.">endocrine</Tooltip> imbalance and metabolic impairment (<Citation reference="teede-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="helvaci-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />). Hyperandrogenism therefore became the focus of our project and the starting point for our engineering.

The project begins with the people who spent years trying to have the complexity of their condition recognised. Their experiences remind us that developing a new solution is not only a question of whether it can work biologically. It must also respond to a real need and be described, tested and communicated with greater precision than the problem it is intended to address.&#x20;

## Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome and Hyperandrogenism

**Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome** (PMOS), formerly known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), is one of the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders affecting women of reproductive age (<Citation reference="world-health-organization-2026" narrative={false} />). 

The **recent transition from the term PCOS to PMOS** reflects an important conceptual shift in the field: the syndrome is no longer regarded primarily as a disorder of ovarian morphology, but rather as a systemic condition involving endocrine, metabolic, reproductive, dermatological, and psychological manifestations (<Citation reference="teede-et-al-2026" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="wang-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />).

PMOS represents a **major global health burden**. Epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 10–13% of women worldwide are affected, while up to 70% of cases may remain undiagnosed (<Citation reference="world-health-organization-2026" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

> **The burden often begins before a diagnosis is made.**

In an international survey of 1,385 women reporting a diagnosis of PMOS, 33.6% had waited more than two years for a diagnosis, and 47.1% had consulted at least three healthcare professionals (<Citation reference="gibson-helm-et-al-2017" narrative={false} />). These findings illustrate how prolonged uncertainty can become part of living with the condition.&#x20;

This diagnostic gap reflects both the heterogeneity of the syndrome and the historical under-recognition of women’s endocrine and metabolic health conditions. Clinically, PMOS is characterized by variable combinations of <Tooltip text="Ovulation, the release of an egg from the ovary, happens irregularly or not at all.">ovulatory dysfunction</Tooltip>, hyperandrogenism, and <Tooltip text="Ovaries that look enlarged or hold many small, immature follicles on an ultrasound scan.">polycystic ovarian morphology</Tooltip>, after exclusion of other endocrine disorders (<Citation reference="teede-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />). Women with PMOS may experience irregular menstrual cycles, <Tooltip text="Ovulation, the release of an egg from the ovary, does not happen.">anovulation</Tooltip>, infertility, <Tooltip text="Excess dark, coarse hair growth in a male-like pattern, e.g. on the face, chest or back.">hirsutism</Tooltip>, acne, <Tooltip text="Hair thinning on the scalp driven by androgens.">androgenic alopecia</Tooltip>, weight gain, <Tooltip text="The body's cells respond poorly to insulin, so more insulin is needed to keep blood sugar in range.">insulin resistance</Tooltip>, <Tooltip text="Abnormal levels of fats such as cholesterol in the blood.">dyslipidemia</Tooltip>, chronic low-grade inflammation, and reduced quality of life. Clinical features of PMOS can also carry social consequences with psychological comorbidities, including anxiety, depression, and body-image distress, being frequently reported (<Citation reference="teede-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="helvaci-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />).

**Hyperandrogenism** is a medical condition defined by an excess of androgen hormones, such as testosterone. Within this broader picture, hyperandrogenism is relevant to our project as it connects a measurable biological process with symptoms that can matter greatly to those affected. Diversity of PMOS means that reducing androgenic activity would address only part of the condition, and **its value would ultimately need to be assessed through outcomes meaningful to patients.**

This particular feature of PMOS contributes directly to dermatological symptoms such as hirsutism, acne, and alopecia, but it also interferes with follicular development, ovulation, and endocrine homeostasis (<Citation narrative={false} />) . At the ovarian level, excess androgens can impair normal <Tooltip text="The maturation of follicles, the small sacs in the ovary that each hold a developing egg.">folliculogenesis</Tooltip>, contributing to the accumulation of immature follicles and chronic anovulation (<Citation narrative={false} />). At the systemic level, hyperandrogenism is closely associated with insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysfunction, creating a reinforcing cycle between endocrine imbalance and metabolic impairment (<Citation narrative={false} />). This interaction is central to the <Tooltip text="How a disease disrupts the body's normal functions.">pathophysiology</Tooltip> of PMOS: insulin resistance can increase ovarian androgen production and reduce <Tooltip text="A blood protein that binds sex hormones and keeps them inactive while bound.">sex hormone-binding globulin</Tooltip> (SHBG), thereby increasing the fraction of <Tooltip text="The share of androgens not tightly bound to SHBG, which can still act on tissues.">bioavailable androgens</Tooltip>. In turn, elevated androgen levels may further contribute to adipose tissue dysfunction, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation.

## **Existing Treatment Options**

| Treatment                                                    | How it works                                                                                      | Role in PMOS and limitations                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Combined oral contraceptive pills (COCs)                     | Suppress ovarian androgen production and increase SHBG, reducing free testosterone                | Common first-line pharmacological options for hirsutism and irregular cycles. The treatment is not compatible with pregnancy and suitability depends on individual contraindications and risks, including thrombosis.    |
| Anti-androgens, e.g. Spironolactone, Flutamide, Finasteride. | Reduce androgen action in target tissues. Spironolactone blocks androgen receptors.               | Used for persistent hirsutism, and/or when COCs are unsuitable or poorly tolerated. Effective contraception is needed because of risks to male fetal development, evidence is heterogeneous and mostly of low certainty. |
| Metformin                                                    | Improves metabolic function and insulin sensitivity and may reduce insulin driven androgen excess | Primarily used for metabolic indications; it is not a first-line treatment for hirsutism alone.                                                                                                                          |

**Current therapeutic** strategies for PMOS are mainly symptom-oriented and depend on the patient’s clinical priorities, such as menstrual regulation, treatment of hyperandrogenic symptoms, fertility, or metabolic risk reduction. International guidelines recommend lifestyle interventions as a core component of management (<Citation reference="teede-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />). Although these approaches can provide important benefits, they do not cure PMOS and were not designed to directly treat women with the syndrome. Moreover, recent evidence assessments have highlighted that major gaps remain regarding their long-term efficacy, safety, and impact on quality of life (<Citation reference="sbu-n-d" narrative={false} />).&#x20;

## **Gut-Androgen Axis**

In recent years, the **gut microbiome** has emerged as a potential contributor to PMOS pathophysiology. The intestinal microbiota is increasingly recognized as an active metabolic and endocrine interface capable of influencing host energy metabolism, immune signaling, bile acid turnover, and steroid hormone homeostasis. Several studies have reported alterations in gut microbial diversity and composition in women with PMOS, and these changes have been associated with hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance, and inflammatory markers. 

In this context, the **gut microbiome** has emerged **as a compelling target**. A growing body of evidence suggests that the gut microbiome is not only associated with PMOS, but may also play a functional role in steroid metabolism and androgen regulation. Microbial steroid-modifying enzymes can reshape intestinal pools of androgens and related metabolites, and recent studies indicate that these activities may also influence circulating androgen levels, suggesting that microbial steroid metabolism may contribute to systemic androgen tone (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="veerus-et-al-2026" narrative={false} />; <Citation narrative={false} />).

**Testosterone** and related androgens are produced endogenously by the ovaries, adrenal glands, and peripheral tissues, and circulate in blood either bound to proteins such as SHBG and albumin or in a free bioactive fraction. The liver plays a central role in androgen metabolism, where steroid hormones undergo reactions (phase I and phase II) that increase their polarity and facilitate excretion (<Citation narrative={false} />). Phase II conjugation, including <Tooltip text="The liver attaches a sugar acid to a hormone, making it water-soluble so it can be excreted.">**glucuronidation**</Tooltip> and <Tooltip text="The liver attaches a sulfate group to a hormone, making it water-soluble so it can be excreted.">**sulfation**</Tooltip>, generates more hydrophilic androgen metabolites that can be eliminated through urine or secreted into bile and delivered to the intestine. However, conjugated steroids entering the gut lumen are not necessarily irreversibly eliminated. Microbial enzymes can <Tooltip text="Remove the group the liver attached, which can turn the hormone back into its active form.">deconjugate</Tooltip> and chemically transform these molecules, potentially regenerating bioactive steroids or producing new steroid metabolites that may be reabsorbed into circulation through <Tooltip text="Substances released from the liver into the gut via bile are reabsorbed and carried back to the liver, instead of leaving the body.">enterohepatic recirculation (</Tooltip><Citation narrative={false} />).

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/background/enteropatic-ric.avif" alt="Testobolome" numbered refId="fig-4xw28" scale={100} plain noZoom={false} />

The concept of the “**testobolome**” has recently been proposed by <Citation narrative={false} /> to describe gut microbes and microbial genes involved in testosterone metabolism. The testobolome includes different microbial enzymes such as steroid reductases that can catalyze structural modifications of the steroid A-ring, including <Tooltip text="Two ways enzymes add hydrogen to the first ring of a steroid. The two products have different shapes, and the 5β form barely activates the androgen receptor.">5α-reduction and **5β-reduction**</Tooltip>. These reactions may alter <Tooltip text="The protein in cells that androgens bind to in order to have their effect.">androgen receptor</Tooltip> activity, steroid stability, and the likelihood of reabsorption <Citation narrative={false} />.
