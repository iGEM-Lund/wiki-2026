### Summary

As our engineered probiotic is a living biotherapeutic product, meant to be administered directly into the human gut, it comes with safety considerations that go beyond those of a standard lab strain. Since the bacterium is meant to survive, act, and eventually be cleared from a living host, we need to ensure that it behaves predictably while inside the gut, cannot cause harm if it escapes into the environment, and does not pass its engineered traits on to the native gut microbiota. We approached this through two layers of biological control, and checked both against the regulatory framework.

## \{% table %}

* **Layer**
* **Question it answers**
* **What we did**

---

* **In-Host Control**
* Does the bacterium act only where and when it should, and how long does it stay?
* A hypoxia-responsive gut gate, a testosterone-responsive feedback loop, a transient (non-colonizing) design, and a review of how the native gut community may limit or be affected by AnTigone (resident gut communities).

---

* **Environmental Biocontainment**
* Can it survive or spread if it escapes the gut?
* Auxotrophy, kill-switches (CRISPR-based and quorum-sensing-based) and genetic firewalling (amber-suppression semantic firewalls), plus the genetic stability of these safety circuits under selection pressure.

---

* **GMO Regulations**
* What must we meet before AnTigone could reach patients?
* EU directives (2009/41/EC, 2001/83/EC, 2001/18/EC), the EMA environmental risk assessment guideline, the Cartagena Protocol and the FDA pathway in the USA. \{% /table %}

**Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)** is a risk that runs across both layers rather than a layer of its own. HGT refers to the transfer of genetic material between organisms. While our engineered bacteria are in the gut, therapeutic genes could potentially spread to native gut bacteria. To reduce this risk, we considered integrating the therapeutic cassette into the EcN chromosome rather than using a plasmid. Previous studies have demonstrated successful integration at specific sites, such as *flu*, *fim*, and *ecpA* (Seco & Fernández, 2022). We also considered avoiding mobile genetic elements and unnecessary antibiotic resistance markers in our construct, rather than removing EcN's naturally occurring transposons, which could disrupt bacterial functions (Grozdanov et al., 2004).

For each strategy, we researched multiple possible approaches, compared them against our project's specific constraints, and selected the combination that best balances safety, feasibility, and relevance to AnTigone as a testosterone-lowering gut therapeutic.

### In-Host Control

#### Gut Gate

Our effector should only be active when our engineered probiotic is in the gut. The distinct physicochemical signals in the gut vary from both the outside environment and between different compartments within the gut itself, and these signals can be used to build a location-based switch. We compared three strategies from the literature and chose hypoxia sensing for our proof-of-concept.

Chien et al. (2022) built oxygen, pH and lactate biosensors that control essential genes (asd, glms) in *E. coli* and *S.* Typhimurium, so that growth depends on the gut-like signal, and tested them in gut-mimicking media and in mice. This gives three sensing options for a location-based gate:

Hypoxia sensing was chosen as our gate. In principle, the oxygen gap between the gut and outside conditions is wide and more consistent than the gut's pH gradient or bile-acid fluctuations, which vary with time of day and diet. Hypoxia promoters are also well established in *E. coli*, since they rely on the bacterium's native FNR-based anaerobic response rather than an engineered bile-acid receptor or pH sensor. Our choice was discussed with Dr. Tsz Yan Clement Chan, who confirmed hypoxia sensing as a valid biosafety layer, while noting it is one layer rather than a complete containment solution on its own.

**What the literature supports, and what it does not.** In Chien et al. (2022), the hypoxia sensor (the FNR-regulated promoter pPepT) allowed growth mainly under large-intestine-like conditions (0% oxygen) and was about 10-fold enriched in the large versus the small intestine of mice. The authors also state that single sensors were not sufficient to achieve targeting in vivo, and the lowest escape rate (about 10⁻⁸) came from combining hypoxia with lactate sensing in an AND gate in *S.* Typhimurium. In a separate screen of EcN promoters, an FNR-activated promoter (ynfEFGH) responded to anaerobic growth and was active in the mouse gut (Naydich et al., 2019). Neither study tested PfnrS itself, so our part is supported by the general FNR mechanism and not by direct gut data. We therefore treat hypoxia gating as a partial restriction that works together with the feedback loop and auxotrophy, not as strict gating on its own.

#### Feedback-Responsive Expression

Our effector should be expressed only when testosterone levels exceed a threshold. To ensure the enzyme is produced only to lower elevated testosterone, we propose combining our gut gate with a testosterone-responsive transcription factor to form an AND gate, building directly on feedback from the Malaga iGEM meet-up, where judges recommended an AND gate combining our biosensor with a feedback loop.

**Other sensing options we looked at.** A yeast sex-hormone sensor reads testosterone only after human aromatase converts it to estradiol (working range 0.98–2000 nM; Wu et al., 2025), so it is a eukaryotic system and not a bacterial one. A DNA aptamer assay for testosterone binds with a dissociation constant of about 240 nM and detects down to 0.29 µM in an electrochemical readout (Cánovas et al., 2022), which is an in vitro assay and not a gene circuit. Transcription-factor sensors can be tuned in EcN by scanning the factor's expression level before fixing a constitutive promoter (Lebovich & Andrews, 2022), which is the approach we would use for TeiR.

**Evidence for the effector.** Our effector enzyme ci2350 is a 5β-reductase that reduced progesterone, cortisone and hydrocortisone in the study that identified it; testosterone was not assayed there (Arp et al., 2025), so its activity on testosterone must be tested directly. A related gut-bacterial reductase pathway (OsrB) was functional when expressed in anaerobically grown *E. coli*, and colonization by the donor bacterium lowered prednisolone 2–10-fold in mouse cecum, colon and feces (Jacoby et al., 2025). This shows that a gut bacterium can remove a steroid in vivo, but it is not a sense-and-degrade circuit, and no study has built one for testosterone.

#### Colonization Control

Colonization control is the decision about how long AnTigone should remain in the gut after dosing: either transient (non-colonizing, cleared between doses and requiring redosing) or engineered to be self-limiting. Even with a gut gate in place, a bacterium that persists indefinitely keeps shedding into the environment and keeps its chances of passing on genes, so how long it stays is itself a safety parameter.

**Options we considered.**

* **Transient, relying on natural clearance.** Wild-type EcN is not reliably transient in humans. In a randomized, placebo-controlled study of 48 healthy volunteers dosed for 17 days, 45 of 48 (94%) tested positive for EcN during that run-in period, and after dosing stopped, the share of volunteers still testing positive fell only gradually, to 4 of 20 (placebo group) and 7 of 20 (mesalamine group) by week 12 (Joeres-Nguyen-Xuan et al., 2010). Persistence therefore varies between individuals and can last weeks to months, so natural clearance alone is not a dependable limit.
* **Self-limiting by engineering.** Auxotrophy makes persistence depend on a nutrient the gut does not supply, which is the role of our Δ*dapA* strategy (see Auxotrophy). The closest precedent is SYNB1618, an engineered EcN for phenylketonuria that carries a *dapA* deletion and depends on exogenous DAP. In mice, a single oral dose was cleared from the gut and feces within 48 h, and in cynomolgus monkeys it was undetectable in stool within 7 days (Isabella et al., 2018). In its first-in-human study, participants received repeated doses (up to three times per day for up to 7 days), no stool sample was above the detection limit 4 days after the last dose and the authors found no evidence of colonization (Puurunen et al., 2021).

**Evaluation.** The contrast between the two options is encouraging but should be read carefully. The 4-day clearance and the 12-week wild-type persistence come from different studies, with different strains, doses, and populations, so they are not a controlled comparison, and the SYNB1618 report to date gives clearance timing rather than a test of why clearance occurred. Isabella et al. (2018) report clearance in mice and monkeys but no human data, and they state that EcN is not present in human feces a week after ingestion, which conflicts with the 12-week persistence above. We also have not yet measured persistence of our own strain, which would require the Δ*dapA* construct to be built and tested.

**Other evidence on persistence.** Thymidine auxotrophy (Δ*thyA*) is the other common choice, but the gut supplies thymidine: it is about 1 µM in porcine ileum and up to 0.075 µM in human ileal lavage (Steidler et al., 2003), and a Δ*thyA Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* strain colonized mice for at least 10 days (Hayashi et al., 2024), which is why we prefer *dapA*. For EcN in mice, neither of two strains was detectable without antibiotic pretreatment, and a co-administered isogenic strain removed a kill-switch strain from stool within 48 h (Rottinghaus et al., 2022). Competition for gut carbohydrates, notably gluconate, shapes which EcN variants persist (Crook et al., 2018), and commensal *E. coli* occupy overlapping nutrient niches (Maltby et al., 2013). Persistence in an intact human gut will therefore depend on the resident community (see Resident Gut Communities).

**What our therapeutic needs.** Our assumption is that lowering gut testosterone is a continuous effect rather than a one-time correction, which favours either repeated dosing or longer persistence. Longer persistence means fewer doses but more shedding into the environment, so we favour a transient design in which persistence is limited by auxotrophy and the therapeutic effect is maintained by redosing. This is a design assumption that will need to be tested, including how dosing frequency relates to the duration of enzyme activity.

**Conclusion.** AnTigone is intended to be transient by design, with persistence limited by Δ*dapA* auxotrophy and the effect maintained by repeat dosing, a choice we will test by measuring clearance of our own strain.

#### Resident Gut Communities

Resident gut communities are the native bacteria that AnTigone meets when it is dosed. This section covers how that community limits an introduced EcN, how AnTigone could change the community, and how the community's own steroid-metabolising enzymes could interact with a testosterone-lowering design.

**Why we need this.** A living therapeutic is not alone in the gut. The resident community can limit how long it stays, change the host hormones it is meant to act on, and be changed by it in turn. We found no study of an androgen-degrading bacterium in the human gut, so the table separates what each paper shows from what we infer.

**Evaluation.** Three conclusions follow. First, the community is more likely to limit AnTigone than to be overrun by it: mouse work shows that engraftment needs antibiotic pretreatment or a free niche, and the only human data (Puurunen et al., 2021) show clearance within 4 days, though this was credited to the auxotrophy and not tested against the community. Second, evidence on AnTigone's effect on the community is thin: the only EcN community data come from one mouse model with an unengineered strain, and no study profiled the community in people dosed with an engineered EcN. Third, the steroid literature gives two risks we have not tested: resident enzymes could regenerate androgens from our effector's products (an inference from reversible HSDH reactions), and lowering luminal androgens might affect gut motility (hypothesis).

**Genetic stability.** In mice, EcN acquired no plasmids over 5 weeks and is reported to accept foreign DNA poorly (Crook et al., 2018).

**Conclusion.** We treat the resident community as a constraint and as a safety variable. AnTigone is designed to pass through the gut in an existing community and to act there briefly, and we make no claim that it leaves the community unchanged. Checks we propose, not yet agreed by the team: (1) profile stool before, during and after dosing in an animal or fecal-culture model, comparing engineered with unengineered EcN; (2) test our effector's activity on testosterone directly; (3) test whether resident bacteria regenerate androgen from its products; (4) monitor transit and stool consistency alongside hormone levels.

### Environmental Biocontainment

#### Auxotrophy

Auxotrophy is a biocontainment strategy that makes an organism unable to synthesize a substance essential for growth, so its normal growth depends on an exogenous supply of that substance. The engineered *E. coli* in AnTigone is designed to metabolize testosterone in the gut using enzymes encoded by AKR1D1, ci2350/CIMBP, and atcABC; for future therapeutic use, the bacteria must not proliferate indefinitely or persist long-term after performing their function. We chose Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy to permit brief metabolic activity while restricting long-term proliferation.

How it works

*dapA* encodes dihydrodipicolinate synthase (DHDPS), which catalyzes the condensation of pyruvate with L-aspartate-β-semialdehyde to form an early intermediate of the DAP pathway; downstream enzymes convert this into *meso*-DAP, which *Enterobacteriaceae* incorporate into peptidoglycan for cell-wall cross-linking. Deleting *dapA* blocks this pathway, preventing normal cell-wall synthesis and division unless DAP is supplied externally. In *E. coli* K-12, exogenous DAP enters via the FliY–YecSC ABC transport system and bypasses the block, so a Δ*dapA* strain can be rescued in the lab by adding DAP but cannot proliferate where DAP is absent, and free DAP has been reported undetectable in mouse intestinal effluent. Our *E. coli* TG1 strain is K-12-derived and does not carry deletions in *fliY*, *yecS*, or *yecC*, so this strategy is theoretically applicable to it.

Stability considerations

Δ*dapA* creates selective pressure, so any compensatory mutation restoring DHDPS activity or acquisition of *dapA* (or an equivalent gene) via HGT could let the bacteria escape containment; cross-feeding of *meso*-DAP in the gut is a further, though less likely, risk. To improve stability, we are considering combining DAP auxotrophy with a second, independent essential dependency, and a CRISPR-based system (following Hayashi et al.'s approach for *thyA*) that targets and disrupts any exogenous *dapA* acquired via HGT. Synthetic auxotrophy is not being pursued now, given the genome-editing complexity involved, but remains a candidate for a future therapeutic-grade strain. We also plan to periodically measure escape frequency and characterize any escape variants to monitor long-term stability. Given the direct link between *dapA* and cell-wall synthesis, its precedent in engineered bacteria, and its relatively simple construction, we selected Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy as our biocontainment strategy.

#### Kill-Switch

As our final product is a probiotic, it is important that it will not pose a risk to the environment or users if it is accidentally released. We looked at two families of kill-switch strategy, CRISPR-based and quorum-sensing-based, and expect combining strategies to be more effective than any single one alone.

CRISPR-based approaches

CRISPR is a gene-editing strategy naturally present in bacteria as phage defense, adaptable to recognize, remove, or express genes as needed. The system has two parts: a Cas enzyme that cuts DNA, and a guide RNA (crRNA + tracrRNA) that tells it where to cut, next to a short PAM motif near the target sequence. The resulting double-strand break is typically repaired by non-homologous end joining, which disrupts the target sequence and its function. We looked at four strategies for applying this to biocontainment:

1. **Environmental (condition-gated) kill switch.** Rottinghaus et al. built a single-input switch responding to the chemical inducer aTc (triggering *tet* gene expression that kills the bacteria) and a double-input switch adding a temperature-sensing layer (PtlpA-TlpA) as an OR gate on top. The single-input switch killed bacteria efficiently inside the gut of test mice; the double-input switch also killed bacteria that had already been excreted, extending containment beyond the gut itself.
2. **Plasmid/chromosome targeting.** Caliando & Voigt designed a CRISPR array matching the bacterium's own plasmid or chromosome sequence, using Cas3 (rather than Cas9) to trigger long-range DNA breakage and bacterial death.
3. **Targeting native repeated sequences.** In *Pseudomonas putida* KT2440, guide RNAs were designed against repeated genomic sequences (cREP, ISPpu9) using a ScCas9 variant; introducing Cas9 triggered CRISPR expression and killed the cell. A limitation is that Cas9 itself can be toxic, making controlled expression difficult.
4. **Essential-gene targeting in a eukaryotic chassis.** Umashankar et al. targeted genes essential for yeast survival (ERG13, PGA3, TPI1, CDC19); disrupting even one was enough to kill the cell, unlike bacterial systems that typically need multiple genomic breaks.

Our chosen approach

* It is also possible to control the gene's expression with the help of the hypoxia promoter, as the gut environment is naturally low in oxygen. Our current approach also includes the use of an FNR-responsive promoter (pPepT or nirB) to drive the expression of our gene only under low oxygen, as shown by Chien et al.
* We aim to integrate the CRISPR technology as a kill switch. This ensures that the organism is no longer able to multiply if it is not present in the gut.
* This Cas9 enzyme is coupled with the FNR-repressed promoter, such as *Pndh*. In this approach, the FNR remains active in the gut, a low-oxygen environment, and represses the *Pndh*-*cas9* cassette. This ensures that Cas9 is not produced.
* However, when the microorganism is in the presence of oxygen, the FNR becomes inactive. This results in the production of Cas9, alongside its guide RNAs, which in turn cleave the target sites based on the guide RNAs designed, working as an effective kill switch that ensures that the microorganism does not survive outside the gut, as shown by Rottinghaus et al.

Quorum sensing

Quorum sensing (QS) is a bacterial communication system using signaling molecules to sense population density and regulate gene expression. In the LuxI/LuxR system, bacteria produce AHL, which accumulates with population size; once a threshold is reached, AHL binds LuxR and activates downstream genes. We looked at QS as a population-dependent safety layer based on precedent from other iGEM teams: Utrecht 2025 used high AHL to activate MazF and inhibit growth at high density; GEMS-Taiwan 2023 used low AHL to trigger toxin-mediated death of isolated bacteria, preventing escape; and KU Leuven 2024 combined QS with temperature sensing for more specific containment. For AnTigone, we could adapt the Utrecht strategy to limit excessive EcN accumulation, relevant since EcN is known to preferentially colonize colorectal neoplastic tissue (Gurbatri et al., 2024), or combine QS with our hypoxia-based gate, so survival requires both low oxygen and an appropriate population density.

#### Genetic Firewalling: Amber-Suppression Semantic Firewall

**Principle.** Amber suppression uses the amber stop codon UAG as a genetic lock: a TAG codon is inserted into the engineered gene, and normally the ribosome stops there, producing only an incomplete protein. A matching suppressor tRNA, provided in the intended host, recognizes UAG and inserts an amino acid instead, allowing translation to continue and producing a full-length, functional protein. If the gene is transferred to a bacterium lacking that suppressor tRNA, translation stops early and the protein is non-functional, making the engineered gene depend on a matching suppressor present only in the intended host.

**Why we considered it.** In the gut, engineered DNA could potentially transfer to other bacteria. Amber suppression would not stop that transfer, but could make the transferred gene less likely to work in a new host, without relying on a dedicated toxin, an attractive additional safety layer.

Main limitations

* It does not control bacterial survival: the engineered *E. coli* can still survive and spread, and DNA transfer can still occur; amber suppression only reduces the chance a transferred gene stays functional.
* It can interfere with normal translation: in *E. coli*, RF1 also recognizes UAG as a stop codon, so RF1 and the suppressor tRNA compete for the same site, and strong suppression may cause read-through of natural UAG stops in other genes.
* The system can become unstable over time: long-term amber suppression creates selective pressure, and in one study *E. coli* adapted over roughly 500 generations by inactivating parts of the orthogonal translation system and promoting plasmid loss.
* Gut evidence is limited: amber suppression has been demonstrated in engineered *E. coli* inside the mouse gastrointestinal tract, but for bacterial tracking rather than biocontainment. We did not find direct validation of an amber-suppression firewall as a containment system in the human gut.

**Overall assessment.** Amber suppression is a useful additional genetic safety layer, since it reduces the function of transferred genes without requiring a toxin, but it does not directly control *E. coli* survival, can interfere with normal UAG translation, may lose stability over long-term growth, and has not been validated as a standalone containment system in the human gut. We treat it as a supporting genetic safeguard alongside an organism-level containment system, rather than a containment system on its own.

### GMO Regulations

As AnTigone is a genetically engineered live bacterial therapeutic, we considered regulations covering its development from laboratory use and potential human administration through to environmental exposure. In the EU, genetically modified live biotherapeutics are covered by both pharmaceutical and GMO regulatory frameworks. In the EU this spans laboratory use (Directive 2009/41/EC), potential human administration as a medicinal product (Directive 2001/83/EC), environmental release (Directive 2001/18/EC), environmental risk assessment for GMO-containing medicinal products (EMA guideline), and international movement of living modified organisms (the Cartagena Protocol).

#### USA

In the US, AnTigone would mainly follow the existing FDA drug/biologic pathway: an Investigational New Drug (IND) application before clinical trials, followed by the appropriate pathway to market approval. As a recombinant bacterial product, we would also need to determine whether an Environmental Assessment is required or whether AnTigone qualifies for a categorical exclusion. The FDA IND/BLA framework sets this pathway: an IND is required before clinical trials, and a Biologics License Application (BLA) is submitted for marketing approval, which would apply to AnTigone if regulated as a biological product.
