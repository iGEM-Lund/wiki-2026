### Genetic and ecological safety

#### **Summary**

As our engineered probiotic is a living biotherapeutic product, meant to be administered directly into the human gut, it comes with safety considerations that go beyond those of a standard lab strain. Since the bacterium is meant to survive, act, and eventually be cleared from a living host, we need to ensure that it behaves predictably while inside the gut, cannot cause harm if it escapes into the environment, and does not pass its engineered traits on to the native gut microbiota. We approached this through three layers: 

* **In-Host Control** governs what the bacterium does while it is still in the gut. This includes a gut gate, which restricts effector expression to the anaerobic gut environment using a hypoxia-responsive promoter, and a feedback-responsive expression mechanism, which aims to further restrict expression to only occur when testosterone is present above a set threshold. Beyond controlling expression itself, we also considered colonization control by weighing whether our probiotic should be transient, requiring repeat dosing, or self-limiting by design. 

- **Environmental Biocontainment** ensures the organism cannot survive or spread if it escapes the gut. This covers auxotrophy (engineering a dependency on a nutrient not available outside controlled conditions), kill-switches (including quorum-sensing-based and CRISPR-based approaches), and genetic firewalling (such as amber-suppression semantic firewalls, which make the organism's genetic code incompatible with natural systems). Across these strategies, we also considered the genetic stability of our safety circuits and how we ensure kill-switches and other mechanisms aren't lost over time through mutation under selection pressure.


- **Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT)** refers to the transfer of genetic material between organisms. While our engineered bacteria are in the gut, therapeutic genes could potentially spread to native gut bacteria. To reduce this risk, we considered integrating the therapeutic cassette into the EcN chromosome rather than using a plasmid. Previous studies have demonstrated successful integration at specific sites, such as *flu*, *fim*, and *ecpA* (Seco & Fernández, 2022). We also considered avoiding mobile genetic elements and unnecessary antibiotic resistance markers in our construct, rather than removing EcN's naturally occurring transposons, which could disrupt bacterial functions (Grozdanov et al., 2004).

* **GMO Regulations** also plays an important role in our design choices. In the EU, this span contained laboratory use (Directive 2009/41/EC), potential human administration as a medicinal product (Directive 2001/83/EC), environmental release (Directive 2001/18/EC), environmental risk assessment for GMO-containing medicinal products (EMA guidelines), and international movement of living modified organisms (the Cartagena Protocol).

For each strategy, we researched multiple possible approaches, compared them against our project's specific constraints, and selected the combination that best balances safety, feasibility, and relevance to AnTigone as a testosterone-lowering gut therapeutic.

### **Gut Gate**

Our effector should only be active when our engineered probiotic is in the gut. The distinct physicochemical signals in the gut vary from both the outside environment and between different compartments within the gut itself, and these signals can be used to build a location-based switch. We compared three strategies from the literature and chose hypoxia sensing for our proof-of-concept.

Chien et al. (2022) built three separate biosensor-driven circuits in *E. coli* and *S.* Typhimurium and validated them under gut-mimicking conditions and in live mice:

| **Option**                  | **How it works**                                                                                                                                                     | **Registry part**                                                                      |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hypoxia sensing             | A promoter (e.g. PfnrS: hypoxia-induced, FNR-regulated) is only active under low-oxygen conditions. The gut lumen is anaerobic while conditions outside are aerobic. | **BBa\_25DMNRIB** — sequence-verified match to the PfnrS promoter in our own construct |
| pH sensing                  | A promoter responds to the gut's pH gradient (small intestine \~pH 6.3, colon \~pH 7).                                                                               | —                                                                                      |
| Lactate / bile acid sensing | A promoter responds to gut-specific metabolites like lactate or bile acids, present at much higher concentration in the gut than elsewhere.                          | —                                                                                      |

Hypoxia sensing was chosen as our gate. In principle, the oxygen gap between the gut and outside conditions is wide and more consistent than the gut's pH gradient or bile-acid fluctuations, which vary with time of day and diet. Hypoxia promoters are also well established in *E. coli*, since they rely on the bacterium's native FNR-based anaerobic response rather than an engineered bile-acid receptor or pH sensor. Our choice was discussed with Dr. Tsz Yan Clement Chan, who confirmed hypoxia sensing as a valid biosafety layer, while noting it is one layer rather than a complete containment solution on its own.

### **Auxotrophy**

Auxotrophy is a biocontainment strategy that makes an organism unable to synthesize a substance essential for growth, so its normal growth depends on an exogenous supply of that substance. The engineered *E. coli* in AnTigone is designed to metabolize testosterone in the gut using enzymes encoded by AKR1D1, ci2350/CIMBP, and atcABC; for future therapeutic use, the bacteria must not proliferate indefinitely or persist long-term after performing their function. We chose Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy to permit brief metabolic activity while restricting long-term proliferation.

#### **How it works**

*dapA* encodes dihydrodipicolinate synthase (DHDPS), which catalyzes the condensation of pyruvate with L-aspartate-β-semialdehyde to form an early intermediate of the DAP pathway; downstream enzymes convert this into *meso*-DAP, which *Enterobacteriaceae* incorporate into peptidoglycan for cell-wall cross-linking. Deleting *dapA* blocks this pathway, preventing normal cell-wall synthesis and division unless DAP is supplied externally. In *E. coli* K-12, exogenous DAP enters via the FliY–YecSC ABC transport system and bypasses the block, so a Δ*dapA* strain can be rescued in the lab by adding DAP but cannot proliferate where DAP is absent  and free DAP has been reported undetectable in mouse intestinal effluent. Our *E. coli* TG1 strain is K-12-derived and does not carry deletions in *fliY*, *yecS*, or *yecC*, so this strategy is theoretically applicable to it.

#### **Stability considerations**

Δ*dapA* creates selective pressure, so any compensatory mutation restoring DHDPS activity or acquisition of *dapA* (or an equivalent gene) via HGT could let the bacteria escape containment; cross-feeding of *meso*-DAP in the gut is a further, though less likely, risk. To improve stability, we are considering combining DAP auxotrophy with a second, independent essential dependency, and a CRISPR-based system (following Hayashi et al. 's approach for *thyA*) that targets and disrupts any exogenous *dapA* acquired via HGT. Synthetic auxotrophy is not being pursued now, given the genome-editing complexity involved, but remains a candidate for a future therapeutic-grade strain. We also plan to periodically measure escape frequency and characterize any escape variants to monitor long-term stability. Given the direct link between *dapA* and cell-wall synthesis, its precedent in engineered bacteria, and its relatively simple construction, we selected Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy as our biocontainment strategy.\
**Kill-Switch**

As our final product is a probiotic, it is important that it will not pose a risk to the environment or users if it is accidentally released. We looked at two families of kill-switch strategy CRISPR-based and quorum-sensing-based  and expect combining strategies to be more effective than any single one alone.

### **CRISPR-based approaches.**&#x20;

CRISPR is a gene-editing strategy naturally present in bacteria as phage defense, adaptable to recognize, remove, or express genes as needed. The system has two parts: a Cas enzyme that cuts DNA, and a guide RNA (crRNA + tracrRNA) that tells it where to cut, next to a short PAM motif near the target sequence. The resulting double-strand break is typically repaired by non-homologous end joining, which disrupts the target sequence and its function. We looked at four strategies for applying this to biocontainment:

1. **Environmental (condition-gated) kill switch.** Rottinghaus et al. built a single-input switch responding to the chemical inducer aTc (triggering *tet* gene expression that kills the bacteria) and a double-input switch adding a temperature-sensing layer (PtlpA-TlpA) as an OR gate on top. The single-input switch killed bacteria efficiently inside the gut of test mice; the double-input switch also killed bacteria that had already been excreted, extending containment beyond the gut itself.
2. **Plasmid/chromosome targeting.** Caliando & Voigt designed a CRISPR array matching the bacterium's own plasmid or chromosome sequence, using Cas3 (rather than Cas9) to trigger long-range DNA breakage and bacterial death.
3. **Targeting native repeated sequences.** In *Pseudomonas putida* KT2440, guide RNAs were designed against repeated genomic sequences (cREP, ISPpu9) using a ScCas9 variant; introducing Cas9 triggered CRISPR expression and killed the cell. A limitation is that Cas9 itself can be toxic, making controlled expression difficult.
4. **Essential-gene targeting in a eukaryotic chassis.** Umashankar et al. targeted genes essential for yeast survival (ERG13, PGA3, TPI1, CDC19); disrupting even one was enough to kill the cell, unlike bacterial systems that typically need multiple genomic breaks.

#### **Our Chosen approach**

* It is also possible to control the gene’s expression with the help of the hypoxia promoter , as the gut environment is naturally low in oxygen. Our current approach also includes the use of an FNR-responsive promoter (pPepT or nirB) to drive the expression of our gene only under low oxygen, as shown by Chien et al. 
* We aim to integrate the CRISPR technology as a kill switch. This ensures that the organism is no longer able to multiply if it is not present in the gut.
* This Cas9 enzyme is coupled with the FNR-repressed promotor, such as the *Pndh*. In this approach, the FNR remains active in the gut, a low-oxygen environment and represses the *Pndh*-*cas9* cassette. This ensures that Cas9 is not produced. 
* However, when the microorganism is in the presence of oxygen, the FNR becomes inactive. This results in the production of Cas9, alongside its guide RNAs and in turn cleaves the target sites based on the guide RNAs designed, working as an effective kill switch that ensures that the microorganism does not survive outside the gut, as shown by Rottinghaus et al.

### **Quorum sensing**

Quorum sensing (QS) is a bacterial communication system using signaling molecules to sense population density and regulate gene expression. In the LuxI/LuxR system, bacteria produce AHL, which accumulates with population size; once a threshold is reached, AHL binds LuxR and activates downstream genes. We looked at QS as a population-dependent safety layer based on precedent from other iGEM teams: Utrecht 2025 used high AHL to activate MazF and inhibit growth at high density; GEMS-Taiwan 2023 used low AHL to trigger toxin-mediated death of isolated bacteria, preventing escape; and KU Leuven 2024 combined QS with temperature sensing for more specific containment. For AnTigone, we could adapt the Utrecht strategy to limit excessive EcN accumulation  relevant since EcN is known to preferentially colonize colorectal neoplastic tissue (Gurbatri et al., 2024)  or combine QS with our hypoxia-based gate, so survival requires both low oxygen and an appropriate population density.

#### **Genetic Firewalling : Amber-Suppression Semantic Firewall**

**Principle.** Amber suppression uses the amber stop codon UAG as a genetic lock: a TAG codon is inserted into the engineered gene, and normally the ribosome stops there, producing only an incomplete protein. A matching suppressor tRNA, provided in the intended host, recognizes UAG and inserts an amino acid instead, allowing translation to continue and producing a full-length, functional protein. If the gene is transferred to a bacterium lacking that suppressor tRNA, translation stops early and the protein is non-functional making the engineered gene depend on a matching suppressor present only in the intended host.

**Why we considered it.** In the gut, engineered DNA could potentially transfer to other bacteria. Amber suppression would not stop that transfer, but could make the transferred gene less likely to work in a new host, without relying on a dedicated toxin, an attractive additional safety layer.

#### **Main limitations.**

* It does not control bacterial survival: the engineered *E. coli* can still survive and spread, and DNA transfer can still occur; amber suppression only reduces the chance a transferred gene stays functional.
* It can interfere with normal translation: in *E. coli*, RF1 also recognizes UAG as a stop codon, so RF1 and the suppressor tRNA compete for the same site, and strong suppression may cause read-through of natural UAG stops in other genes.
* The system can become unstable over time: long-term amber suppression creates selective pressure, and in one study *E. coli* adapted over roughly 500 generations by inactivating parts of the orthogonal translation system and promoting plasmid loss.
* Gut evidence is limited: amber suppression has been demonstrated in engineered *E. coli* inside the mouse gastrointestinal tract, but for bacterial tracking rather than biocontainment . We did not find direct validation of an amber-suppression firewall as a containment system in the human gut.

**Overall assessment.** Amber suppression is a useful additional genetic safety layer, since it reduces the function of transferred genes without requiring a toxin, but it does not directly control *E. coli* survival, can interfere with normal UAG translation, may lose stability over long-term growth, and has not been validated as a standalone containment system in the human gut. We treat it as a supporting genetic safeguard alongside an organism-level containment system, rather than a containment system on its own.\

### GMO regulations

As AnTigone is a genetically engineered live bacterial therapeutic, we considered regulations covering its development from laboratory use and potential human administration through to environmental exposure. In the EU, genetically modified live biotherapeutics are covered by both pharmaceutical and GMO regulatory frameworks.\

#### **USA**

In the US, AnTigone would mainly follow the existing FDA drug/biologic pathway: an Investigational New Drug (IND) application before clinical trials, followed by the appropriate pathway to market approval. As a recombinant bacterial product, we would also need to determine whether an Environmental Assessment is required or whether AnTigone qualifies for a categorical exclusion. The FDA IND/BLA framework sets this pathway: an IND is required before clinical trials, and a Biologics License Application (BLA) is submitted for marketing approval, which would apply to AnTigone if regulated as a biological product.
