## Genetic and ecological safety:&#x20;

### Introduction:&#x20;

Biosafety is a specific set of measures which help keep genetically modified organisms from causing harm to people, other organisms or the environment. For most lab work, laboratory containment is enough. Our probiotic, AnTigone is a living biotherapeutic which has to be administered orally and has to work inside the gut. It passes through patients, can be shed into faeces and could meet the wider environment. Once released, living cells can multiply, survive and pass genes to other bacteria. To avoid all of these dangers we have incorporated a safety design. Our target questions while building the safety design were:&#x20;

* Does it act only when and where it should?&#x20;
* Can it survive or spread outside the gut?&#x20;
* Which safety standards must be followed before the product reaches patients?\

### Summary

As our engineered probiotic is a <Tooltip text="A medicine made of live microorganisms, here a bacterium that works inside the body.">living biotherapeutic</Tooltip> product, meant to be administered directly into the human gut, it comes with safety considerations that go beyond those of a standard lab strain. Since the bacterium is meant to survive, act, and eventually be cleared from a living host, we need to ensure that it behaves predictably while inside the gut, cannot cause harm if it escapes into the environment, and does not pass its engineered traits on to the native gut microbiota. We approached this through two layers of biological control, and checked both against the regulatory framework.\
\
These measures reduce several important risks, but AnTigone is still a proof-of-concept design rather than a clinically validated product. Further preclinical testing would be required before its safety could be assessed for human use.

Beyond these two layers, we also considered risks that could affect both in-host and environmental safety, particularly horizontal gene transfer (HGT). HGT refers to the transfer of genetic material between organisms. While our engineered bacteria are in the gut, therapeutic genes could potentially spread to native gut bacteria. To reduce this risk, we considered integrating the therapeutic genes into the <Tooltip text="Inserting a gene into the bacterium's main genome means it is passed on only through cell division and is harder to transfer to other bacteria.">chromosome</Tooltip> of *Escherichia coli* Nissle 1917 (EcN), a well-studied probiotic strain, rather than using a <Tooltip text="A small circular piece of DNA that bacteria can copy and pass to each other.">plasmid</Tooltip>. Previous studies have demonstrated successful integration at specific sites, such as flu, fim, and ecpA (<Citation reference="seco-and-fernandez-2022" narrative={false} />). We also considered avoiding <Tooltip text="DNA sequences that can move within a genome or between cells, such as transposons.">mobile genetic elements</Tooltip> and unnecessary <Tooltip text="Genes that make a bacterium survive an antibiotic, used in the lab to select cells that took up new DNA.">antibiotic resistance markers</Tooltip> in our construct, rather than removing EcN's naturally occurring transposons, which could disrupt bacterial functions (<Citation reference="grozdanov-et-al-2004" narrative={false} />).

For each strategy, we researched multiple possible approaches, compared them against our project's specific constraints, and selected the combination that best balances safety, feasibility, and relevance to AnTigone as a testosterone-lowering gut therapeutic. &#x20;

### In-Host Control

#### **Gut Gate**

Our effector should only be active when our engineered probiotic is in the gut. The distinct physicochemical signals in the gut vary from both the outside environment and between different compartments within the gut itself, and these signals can be used to build a location-based switch. <Citation reference="chien-et-al-2022" narrative /> built oxygen, pH and lactate biosensors that control essential genes (*asd, glms) in E. coli* and S.Typhimurium, so that growth depends on the gut-like signal, and tested them in gut-mimicking media and in mice. Why Hypoxia Sensing?&#x20;

* The oxygen gap between the gut and outside conditions is wide and more consistent than the gut's pH gradient or bile-acid fluctuations, which vary with time of day and diet.
* Hypoxia promoters are also well established in *E. coli ,* since they rely on the bacterium's native FNR-based anaerobic response rather than an engineered bile-acid receptor or pH sensor.&#x20;
* Dr. Tsz Yan Clement Chan confirmed hypoxia sensing as a valid biosafety layer, while noting it is one layer rather than a complete containment solution on its own.

  We therefore treat hypoxia gating as a partial restriction that works together with the feedback loop and auxotrophy, not as strict gating on its own.

#### **Colonization Control**

Colonization control is the decision about how long our probiotic should remain in the gut after dosing. It can either be transient (not colonizing the gut, cleared between doses and requiring redosing) or engineered to be self-limiting. Residence time matters because shedding continues for as long as the bacterium stays in the gut, and a gut gate does not shorten that. We assume that lowering gut testosterone is a continuous process, so a transient design fits better: the effect is maintained by redosing and the bacterium does not have to persist.

### Environmental Biocontainment

#### **Auxotrophy**

Auxotrophy is a <Tooltip text="Built-in measures that stop an engineered organism from surviving or spreading outside its intended place.">biocontainment</Tooltip> strategy that makes an organism unable to synthesize a substance essential for growth, so its normal growth depends on an exogenous supply of that substance. The engineered *E. coli* in AnTigone is designed to metabolize testosterone in the gut using enzymes encoded by AKR1D1, ci2350/CIMBP, and atcABC; for future therapeutic use, the bacteria must not proliferate indefinitely or persist long-term after performing their function. We chose Δ*dapA*-mediated <Tooltip text="Diaminopimelic acid, a molecule bacteria need to build their cell wall, which our strain can no longer make.">DAP</Tooltip> auxotrophy to permit brief metabolic activity while restricting long-term proliferation.

#### **Stability considerations**

Δ*dapA* creates selective pressure, so any compensatory mutation restoring DHDPS activity or acquisition of *dapA* (or an equivalent gene) via HGT could let the bacteria escape containment; cross-feeding of *meso*-DAP in the gut is a further, though less likely, risk. To improve stability, we are considering combining DAP auxotrophy with a second, independent essential dependency, and a CRISPR-based system (following Hayashi et al.'s approach for *thyA*) that targets and disrupts any exogenous *dapA* acquired via HGT. Synthetic auxotrophy is not being pursued now, given the genome-editing complexity involved, but remains a candidate for a future therapeutic-grade strain. We also plan to periodically measure escape frequency and characterize any escape variants to monitor long-term stability. Given the direct link between *dapA* and cell-wall synthesis, its precedent in engineered bacteria, and its relatively simple construction, we selected Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy as our biocontainment strategy.

#### **Kill-Switch**

As our final product is a probiotic, it is important that it will not pose a risk to the environment or users if it is accidentally released. We looked at two families of kill-switch strategy, CRISPR-based and quorum-sensing-based, and expect combining strategies to be more effective than any single one alone.

CRISPR-based approaches

CRISPR is a gene-editing strategy naturally present in bacteria as phage defense, adaptable to recognize, remove, or express genes as needed. The system has two parts: a Cas enzyme that cuts DNA, and a <Tooltip text="The short RNA that tells Cas9 where to cut.">guide RNA</Tooltip> (crRNA + tracrRNA) that tells it where to cut, next to a short PAM motif near the target sequence. The resulting double-strand break is typically repaired by <Tooltip text="A repair process that glues broken DNA back together, often introducing errors.">non-homologous end joining</Tooltip>, which disrupts the target sequence and its function, with <Ref target="fig-f6yot" /> displaying different CRISPR-based genome editors. We looked at four strategies for applying this to biocontainment:

1. **Environmental (condition-gated) kill switch.** <Citation reference="rottinghaus-et-al" narrative={false} /> built a single-input switch responding to the chemical inducer aTc (triggering *tet* gene expression that kills the bacteria) and a double-input switch adding a temperature-sensing layer (PtlpA-TlpA) as an <Tooltip text="A genetic circuit that switches on when either input is present.">OR gate</Tooltip> on top. The single-input switch killed bacteria efficiently inside the gut of test mice; the double-input switch also killed bacteria that had already been excreted, extending containment beyond the gut itself.
2. **Plasmid/chromosome targeting.** <Citation reference="caliando-et-al-2015" narrative /> designed a CRISPR array matching the bacterium's own plasmid or chromosome sequence, using Cas3 (rather than Cas9) to trigger long-range DNA breakage and bacterial death.
3. **Targeting native repeated sequences.** In *Pseudomonas putida* KT2440 (<Citation reference="asin-garcia-et-al-2024" narrative={false} />), guide RNAs were designed against repeated genomic sequences (cREP, ISPpu9) using a ScCas9 variant; introducing Cas9 triggered CRISPR expression and killed the cell. A limitation is that Cas9 itself can be toxic, making controlled expression difficult.
4. **Essential-gene targeting in a eukaryotic chassis.** <Citation reference="umashankar-et-al-2026" narrative /> targeted genes essential for yeast survival (ERG13, PGA3, TPI1, CDC19); disrupting even one was enough to kill the cell, unlike bacterial systems that typically need multiple genomic breaks.

#### **Our chosen approach**

* It is also possible to control the gene's expression with the help of the hypoxia promoter, as the gut environment is naturally low in oxygen. Our current approach also includes the use of an FNR-responsive promoter (pPepT or nirB) to drive the expression of our gene only under low oxygen, as shown by Chien et al.
* We aim to integrate the CRISPR technology as a kill switch. This ensures that the organism is no longer able to multiply if it is not present in the gut.
* This Cas9 enzyme is coupled with the FNR-repressed promoter, such as *Pndh*. In this approach, the FNR remains active in the gut, a low-oxygen environment, and represses the *Pndh*-*cas9* cassette. This ensures that Cas9 is not produced.
* However, when the microorganism is in the presence of oxygen, the FNR becomes inactive. This results in the production of Cas9, alongside its guide RNAs, which in turn cleave the target sites based on the guide RNAs designed, working as an effective kill switch that ensures that the microorganism does not survive outside the gut, as shown by Rottinghaus et al.

#### **Quorum sensing**

Quorum sensing (QS) is a bacterial communication system using signaling molecules to sense population density and regulate gene expression. In the LuxI/LuxR system, bacteria produce AHL, which accumulates with population size; once a threshold is reached, AHL binds LuxR and activates downstream genes. We looked at QS as a population-dependent safety layer based on precedent from other iGEM teams:&#x20;

* Utrecht 2025 used high AHL to activate MazF and inhibit growth at high density
* GEMS-Taiwan 2023 used low AHL to trigger toxin-mediated death of isolated bacteria, preventing escape
* KU Leuven 2024 combined QS with temperature sensing for more specific containment.&#x20;

For AnTigone, we could adapt the Utrecht strategy to limit excessive EcN accumulation, particularly given previous findings that EcN can preferentially colonize colorectal neoplastic tissue (<Citation reference="gurbatri-et-al-2024" narrative={false} />). Alternatively, we could combine quorum sensing with our hypoxia-based gate, so that bacterial survival depends on both low oxygen availability and an appropriate population density.

#### **Genetic Firewalling: Amber-Suppression Semantic Firewall**

**Principle.**&#x20;

Amber suppression uses the amber stop codon UAG as a genetic lock: a TAG codon is inserted into the engineered gene, and normally the ribosome stops there, producing only an incomplete protein. A matching suppressor tRNA, provided in the intended host, recognizes UAG and inserts an amino acid instead, allowing translation to continue and producing a full-length, functional protein. If the gene is transferred to a bacterium lacking that suppressor tRNA, translation stops early and the protein is non-functional, making the engineered gene depend on a matching suppressor present only in the intended host.

**Why we considered it.** In the gut, engineered DNA could potentially transfer to other bacteria. Amber suppression would not stop that transfer, but could make the transferred gene less likely to work in a new host, without relying on a dedicated toxin, an attractive additional safety layer.

**Main limitations**

* It does not control bacterial survival: the engineered *E. coli* can still survive and spread, and DNA transfer can still occur; amber suppression only reduces the chance a transferred gene stays functional.
* It can interfere with normal translation: in *E. coli*, <Tooltip text="A protein that ends translation at the UAG stop codon.">RF1</Tooltip> also recognizes UAG as a stop codon, so RF1 and the suppressor tRNA compete for the same site, and strong suppression may cause <Tooltip text="Translation continuing past a stop codon where it should stop.">read-through</Tooltip> of natural UAG stops in other genes.
* The system can become unstable over time: long-term amber suppression creates selective pressure, and in one study *E. coli* adapted over roughly 500 generations by inactivating parts of the orthogonal translation system and promoting plasmid loss.
* Gut evidence is limited: amber suppression has been demonstrated in engineered *E. coli* inside the mouse gastrointestinal tract, but for bacterial tracking rather than biocontainment. We did not find direct validation of an amber-suppression firewall as a containment system in the human gut.

**Overall assessment.** Amber suppression is a useful additional genetic safety layer, since it reduces the function of transferred genes without requiring a toxin, but it does not directly control *E. coli* survival, can interfere with normal UAG translation, may lose stability over long-term growth, and has not been validated as a standalone containment system in the human gut. We treat it as a supporting genetic safeguard alongside an organism-level containment system, rather than a containment system on its own.

#### US

In the United States, AnTigone would likely follow the FDA's regulatory pathway for drugs and biological products. If classified as a biological product, an <Tooltip text="The request to start clinical trials of a new medicine.">Investigational New Drug (IND) application</Tooltip> would generally be required before initiating clinical trials, followed by a <Tooltip text="The request to sell a biological product.">Biologics License Application (BLA)</Tooltip> to obtain marketing approval. As AnTigone involves genetically engineered bacteria, we would also need to assess whether an <Tooltip text="A review of a product's impact on the environment.">Environmental Assessment (EA)</Tooltip> is required or whether the product qualifies for a <Tooltip text="An exemption from a full environmental assessment for categories of actions known to have little impact.">categorical exclusion</Tooltip> under FDA regulations.
