### Summary

As our engineered probiotic is a living biotherapeutic product, meant to be administered directly into the human gut, it comes with safety considerations that go beyond those of a standard lab strain. Since the bacterium is meant to survive, act, and eventually be cleared from a living host, we need to ensure that it behaves predictably while inside the gut, cannot cause harm if it escapes into the environment, and does not pass its engineered traits on to the native gut microbiota. We approached this through two layers of biological control, and checked both against the regulatory framework.

| **Layer**                        | **Question it answers**                                                          | **What we did**                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **In-Host Control**              | Does the bacterium act only where and when it should, and how long does it stay? | A hypoxia-responsive gut gate, a testosterone-responsive feedback loop, a transient (non-colonizing) design, and a review of how the native gut community may limit or be affected by our probiotic (resident gut communities). |
| **Environmental Biocontainment** | Can it survive or spread if it escapes the gut?                                  | Auxotrophy, kill-switches (CRISPR-based and quorum-sensing-based) and genetic firewalling (amber-suppression semantic firewalls), plus the genetic stability of these safety circuits under selection pressure.                 |
| **GMO Regulations**              | What must we meet before AnTigone could reach patients?                          | EU directives (2009/41/EC, 2001/83/EC, 2001/18/EC), the EMA environmental risk assessment guideline, the Cartagena Protocol and the FDA pathway in the USA.                                                                     |

Beyond these two layers, we also considered risks that could affect both in-host and environmental safety, particularly horizontal gene transfer (HGT). HGT refers to the transfer of genetic material between organisms. While our engineered bacteria are in the gut, therapeutic genes could potentially spread to native gut bacteria. To reduce this risk, we considered integrating the therapeutic genes into the chromosome of Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), a well-studied probiotic strain, rather than using a plasmid. Previous studies have demonstrated successful integration at specific sites, such as flu, fim, and ecpA <Citation reference="seco-and-fernandez-2022" />. We also considered avoiding mobile genetic elements and unnecessary antibiotic resistance markers in our construct, rather than removing EcN's naturally occurring transposons, which could disrupt bacterial functions <Citation reference="grozdanov-et-al-2004" />.

For each strategy, we researched multiple possible approaches, compared them against our project's specific constraints, and selected the combination that best balances safety, feasibility, and relevance to AnTigone as a testosterone-lowering gut therapeutic. &#x20;

### In-Host Control

#### **Gut Gate**

Our effector should only be active when our engineered probiotic is in the gut. The distinct physicochemical signals in the gut vary from both the outside environment and between different compartments within the gut itself, and these signals can be used to build a location-based switch. We compared three strategies from the literature and chose hypoxia sensing for our proof-of-concept.

Chien et al. (2022) built oxygen, pH and lactate biosensors that control essential genes (asd, glms) in *E. coli* and *S.* Typhimurium, so that growth depends on the gut-like signal, and tested them in gut-mimicking media and in mice. This gives three sensing options for a location-based gate:

| **Option**                  | **How it works**                                                                                                                                                     | **Registry part**                                                                      |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hypoxia sensing             | A promoter (e.g. PfnrS: hypoxia-induced, FNR-regulated) is only active under low-oxygen conditions. The gut lumen is anaerobic while conditions outside are aerobic. | **BBa\_25DMNRIB** — sequence-verified match to the PfnrS promoter in our own construct |
| pH sensing                  | A promoter responds to the gut's pH gradient (small intestine \~pH 6.3, colon \~pH 7).                                                                               | —                                                                                      |
| Lactate / bile acid sensing | A promoter responds to gut-specific metabolites like lactate or bile acids, present at much higher concentration in the gut than elsewhere.                          | —                                                                                      |

Hypoxia sensing was chosen as our gate. In principle, the oxygen gap between the gut and outside conditions is wide and more consistent than the gut's pH gradient or bile-acid fluctuations, which vary with time of day and diet. Hypoxia promoters are also well established in *E. coli*, since they rely on the bacterium's native FNR-based anaerobic response rather than an engineered bile-acid receptor or pH sensor. Our choice was discussed with Dr. Tsz Yan Clement Chan, who confirmed hypoxia sensing as a valid biosafety layer, while noting it is one layer rather than a complete containment solution on its own.

We therefore treat hypoxia gating as a partial restriction that works together with the feedback loop and auxotrophy, not as strict gating on its own.

#### **Feedback-Responsive Expression**

Our effector should be expressed only when testosterone levels exceed a threshold. To ensure the enzyme is produced only to lower elevated testosterone, we propose combining our gut gate with a testosterone-responsive transcription factor to form an AND gate, building directly on feedback from the Malaga iGEM meet-up, where judges recommended an AND gate combining our biosensor with a feedback loop.

Our effector enzyme ci2350 is a 5β-reductase that reduced progesterone, cortisone and hydrocortisone in the study that identified it; testosterone was not assayed there (Arp et al., 2025), so its activity on testosterone must be tested directly. A related gut-bacterial reductase pathway (OsrB) was functional when expressed in anaerobically grown *E. coli*, and colonization by the donor bacterium lowered prednisolone 2–10-fold in mouse cecum, colon and feces (Jacoby et al., 2025). This shows that a gut bacterium can remove a steroid in vivo, but it is not a sense-and-degrade circuit, and no study has built one for testosterone.

#### **Colonization Control**

Colonization control is the decision about how long our probiotic should remain in the gut after dosing. It can either be transient (not colonizing the gut, cleared between doses and requiring redosing) or engineered to be self-limiting. Residence time matters because shedding continues for as long as the bacterium stays in the gut, and a gut gate does not shorten that.

We compared two designs. A transient design relies on natural clearance, so the bacterium is cleared between doses and the effect is maintained by redosing. A self-limiting design uses engineering to control how long the bacterium stays. We assume that lowering gut testosterone is a continuous process rather than a one-time correction. Under this assumption, a transient design fits our therapeutic strategy better, because the effect is maintained by redosing and the bacterium does not have to persist for long.

Wild-type *E. coli* Nissle 1917 (EcN) is not reliably transient in humans. In a placebo-controlled study of 48 healthy volunteers dosed for 17 days, 45 of 48 tested positive for EcN during dosing. After dosing stopped, the share still testing positive fell only gradually, to 4 of 20 (placebo group) and 7 of 20 (mesalamine group) by week 12 (Joeres-Nguyen-Xuan et al., 2010). Natural clearance alone is therefore not dependable, and we need to limit persistence through engineering, as explained in our auxotrophy strategy. Engineering can achieve this: in a first-in-human study of a Δ*dapA* EcN strain, no stool sample was above the detection limit 4 days after the last dose (Puurunen et al., 2021).

In conclusion, AnTigone is intended to be transient by design, with persistence limited by Δ*dapA* auxotrophy and the effect maintained by repeat dosing. We will test this by measuring clearance of our own strain.

#### **Resident Gut Communities**

Resident gut communities are the native bacterial communities present in the gut. A living biotherapeutic is not alone in the gut, so we considered how it might affect this community.

We came across two possibilities that could fit our model. First, a study in a mouse model reported that after four weeks, wild-type *E. coli* Nissle 1917 (EcN) changed the community composition (Luo et al., 2023). This was an unengineered strain in a hormone-perturbed model and may not apply to healthy hosts, but it suggests that the bacterium itself may shift the community.

Second, lowering gut testosterone levels may shift the resident gut community. In another mouse study, testosterone treatment altered composition in both sexes (Moadi et al., 2024), although the effect of lowering testosterone was not tested.

In conclusion, we found no study that profiled the gut community in humans dosed with an engineered EcN, so we cannot say how large any effect would be. We therefore treat the effect on resident communities as an open question.

### Environmental Biocontainment

#### **Auxotrophy**

Auxotrophy is a biocontainment strategy that makes an organism unable to synthesize a substance essential for growth, so its normal growth depends on an exogenous supply of that substance. The engineered *E. coli* in AnTigone is designed to metabolize testosterone in the gut using enzymes encoded by AKR1D1, ci2350/CIMBP, and atcABC; for future therapeutic use, the bacteria must not proliferate indefinitely or persist long-term after performing their function. We chose Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy to permit brief metabolic activity while restricting long-term proliferation.

#### **Stability considerations**

Δ*dapA* creates selective pressure, so any compensatory mutation restoring DHDPS activity or acquisition of *dapA* (or an equivalent gene) via HGT could let the bacteria escape containment; cross-feeding of *meso*-DAP in the gut is a further, though less likely, risk. To improve stability, we are considering combining DAP auxotrophy with a second, independent essential dependency, and a CRISPR-based system (following Hayashi et al.'s approach for *thyA*) that targets and disrupts any exogenous *dapA* acquired via HGT. Synthetic auxotrophy is not being pursued now, given the genome-editing complexity involved, but remains a candidate for a future therapeutic-grade strain. We also plan to periodically measure escape frequency and characterize any escape variants to monitor long-term stability. Given the direct link between *dapA* and cell-wall synthesis, its precedent in engineered bacteria, and its relatively simple construction, we selected Δ*dapA*-mediated DAP auxotrophy as our biocontainment strategy.

#### **Kill-Switch**

As our final product is a probiotic, it is important that it will not pose a risk to the environment or users if it is accidentally released. We looked at two families of kill-switch strategy, CRISPR-based and quorum-sensing-based, and expect combining strategies to be more effective than any single one alone.

CRISPR-based approaches

CRISPR is a gene-editing strategy naturally present in bacteria as phage defense, adaptable to recognize, remove, or express genes as needed. The system has two parts: a Cas enzyme that cuts DNA, and a guide RNA (crRNA + tracrRNA) that tells it where to cut, next to a short PAM motif near the target sequence. The resulting double-strand break is typically repaired by non-homologous end joining, which disrupts the target sequence and its function. We looked at four strategies for applying this to biocontainment:

1. **Environmental (condition-gated) kill switch.** Rottinghaus et al. built a single-input switch responding to the chemical inducer aTc (triggering *tet* gene expression that kills the bacteria) and a double-input switch adding a temperature-sensing layer (PtlpA-TlpA) as an OR gate on top. The single-input switch killed bacteria efficiently inside the gut of test mice; the double-input switch also killed bacteria that had already been excreted, extending containment beyond the gut itself.
2. **Plasmid/chromosome targeting.** Caliando & Voigt designed a CRISPR array matching the bacterium's own plasmid or chromosome sequence, using Cas3 (rather than Cas9) to trigger long-range DNA breakage and bacterial death.
3. **Targeting native repeated sequences.** In *Pseudomonas putida* KT2440, guide RNAs were designed against repeated genomic sequences (cREP, ISPpu9) using a ScCas9 variant; introducing Cas9 triggered CRISPR expression and killed the cell. A limitation is that Cas9 itself can be toxic, making controlled expression difficult.
4. **Essential-gene targeting in a eukaryotic chassis.** Umashankar et al. targeted genes essential for yeast survival (ERG13, PGA3, TPI1, CDC19); disrupting even one was enough to kill the cell, unlike bacterial systems that typically need multiple genomic breaks.

#### **Our chosen approach**

* It is also possible to control the gene's expression with the help of the hypoxia promoter, as the gut environment is naturally low in oxygen. Our current approach also includes the use of an FNR-responsive promoter (pPepT or nirB) to drive the expression of our gene only under low oxygen, as shown by Chien et al.
* We aim to integrate the CRISPR technology as a kill switch. This ensures that the organism is no longer able to multiply if it is not present in the gut.
* This Cas9 enzyme is coupled with the FNR-repressed promoter, such as *Pndh*. In this approach, the FNR remains active in the gut, a low-oxygen environment, and represses the *Pndh*-*cas9* cassette. This ensures that Cas9 is not produced.
* However, when the microorganism is in the presence of oxygen, the FNR becomes inactive. This results in the production of Cas9, alongside its guide RNAs, which in turn cleave the target sites based on the guide RNAs designed, working as an effective kill switch that ensures that the microorganism does not survive outside the gut, as shown by Rottinghaus et al.

#### **Quorum sensing**

Quorum sensing (QS) is a bacterial communication system using signaling molecules to sense population density and regulate gene expression. In the LuxI/LuxR system, bacteria produce AHL, which accumulates with population size; once a threshold is reached, AHL binds LuxR and activates downstream genes. We looked at QS as a population-dependent safety layer based on precedent from other iGEM teams:&#x20;

* Utrecht 2025 used high AHL to activate MazF and inhibit growth at high density
* GEMS-Taiwan 2023 used low AHL to trigger toxin-mediated death of isolated bacteria, preventing escape
* KU Leuven 2024 combined QS with temperature sensing for more specific containment.&#x20;

For AnTigone, we could adapt the Utrecht strategy to limit excessive EcN accumulation, particularly given previous findings that EcN can preferentially colonize colorectal neoplastic tissue <Citation reference="gurbatri-et-al-2024" />. Alternatively, we could combine quorum sensing with our hypoxia-based gate, so that bacterial survival depends on both low oxygen availability and an appropriate population density.

#### **Genetic Firewalling: Amber-Suppression Semantic Firewall**

**Principle.** Amber suppression uses the amber stop codon UAG as a genetic lock: a TAG codon is inserted into the engineered gene, and normally the ribosome stops there, producing only an incomplete protein. A matching suppressor tRNA, provided in the intended host, recognizes UAG and inserts an amino acid instead, allowing translation to continue and producing a full-length, functional protein. If the gene is transferred to a bacterium lacking that suppressor tRNA, translation stops early and the protein is non-functional, making the engineered gene depend on a matching suppressor present only in the intended host.

**Why we considered it.** In the gut, engineered DNA could potentially transfer to other bacteria. Amber suppression would not stop that transfer, but could make the transferred gene less likely to work in a new host, without relying on a dedicated toxin, an attractive additional safety layer.

**Main limitations**

* It does not control bacterial survival: the engineered *E. coli* can still survive and spread, and DNA transfer can still occur; amber suppression only reduces the chance a transferred gene stays functional.
* It can interfere with normal translation: in *E. coli*, RF1 also recognizes UAG as a stop codon, so RF1 and the suppressor tRNA compete for the same site, and strong suppression may cause read-through of natural UAG stops in other genes.
* The system can become unstable over time: long-term amber suppression creates selective pressure, and in one study *E. coli* adapted over roughly 500 generations by inactivating parts of the orthogonal translation system and promoting plasmid loss.
* Gut evidence is limited: amber suppression has been demonstrated in engineered *E. coli* inside the mouse gastrointestinal tract, but for bacterial tracking rather than biocontainment. We did not find direct validation of an amber-suppression firewall as a containment system in the human gut.

**Overall assessment.** Amber suppression is a useful additional genetic safety layer, since it reduces the function of transferred genes without requiring a toxin, but it does not directly control *E. coli* survival, can interfere with normal UAG translation, may lose stability over long-term growth, and has not been validated as a standalone containment system in the human gut. We treat it as a supporting genetic safeguard alongside an organism-level containment system, rather than a containment system on its own.

### GMO Regulations

As AnTigone is a genetically engineered live bacterial therapeutic, we considered regulations covering its development from laboratory use and potential human administration through to environmental exposure. In the EU, genetically modified live biotherapeutics are covered by both pharmaceutical and GMO regulatory frameworks. In the EU this spans laboratory use (Directive 2009/41/EC), potential human administration as a medicinal product (Directive 2001/83/EC), environmental release (Directive 2001/18/EC), environmental risk assessment for GMO-containing medicinal products (EMA guideline), and international movement of living modified organisms (the Cartagena Protocol).

In the US, our probiotic would mainly follow the existing FDA drug/biologic pathway: an Investigational New Drug (IND) application before clinical trials, followed by the appropriate pathway to market approval. As a recombinant bacterial product, we would also need to determine whether an Environmental Assessment is required or whether AnTigone qualifies for a categorical exclusion. The FDA IND/BLA framework sets this pathway: an IND is required before clinical trials, and a Biologics License Application (BLA) is submitted for marketing approval, which would apply to AnTigone if regulated as a biological product.
