### AnTigone: engineering controlled steroid transformation

<Quote />

AnTigone explores whether engineered bacteria could be used to modify <Tooltip text="Having the effects of androgens, the male sex hormones such as testosterone.">androgenic</Tooltip> <Tooltip text="Hormones built on a shared four-ring skeleton, such as testosterone and progesterone.">steroids</Tooltip> within the intestine. Building on the biological rationale presented in the [Background](/background), we propose a system that combines two functions: an enzyme capable of transforming steroid molecules and an expression system responsive to low-oxygen conditions.

Our central hypothesis is that **controlled expression of a steroid 5β-modulating enzyme could support the conversion of excess androgen into products with lower or no androgenic activity and/or a reduction in potent androgen <Tooltip text="How much of a substance is free to act on the body's tissues.">bioavailability</Tooltip>**.

### **Why steroid 5β-reduction?**

The biological activity of a steroid depends partly on its molecular structure. <Tooltip text="Enzymes that add hydrogen to a steroid at the 5β position, changing the molecule's 3D shape and, for testosterone, greatly lowering its androgen activity.">Steroid 5β-reductases</Tooltip> modify the <Tooltip text="The part of the steroid's first ring that the enzyme changes. Its geometry decides how the steroid fits into receptors.">double bond between carbon atoms 4 and 5</Tooltip>, changing the geometry of the steroid molecule (<Citation reference="di-costanzo-et-al-2008" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="penning-2023" narrative={false} />). Human <Tooltip text="The human enzyme that performs 5β-reduction. We use it as our established reference.">AKR1D1</Tooltip> provides an established example of this chemistry and can act on testosterone and other steroid <Tooltip text="The molecule an enzyme acts on and converts.">substrates</Tooltip>.&#x20;

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/background/reduction-mechanism.avif" alt="Diagram comparing testosterone conversion by 5α-reduction and 5β-reduction, highlighting 5β-reduction as AnTigone's proposed route to lower androgenic activity." caption="Alternative outcomes of testosterone 5-reduction. Reduction of the C4–C5 double bond produces different steroid configurations depending on the stereochemistry of the reaction. Whereas 5α-reduction produces the potent androgen 5α-dihydrotestosterone, 5β-reduction is a one-way reaction that produces a bent steroid configuration associated with substantially reduced androgenic activity. AnTigone investigates the latter pathway as a potential route for modifying androgen activity." numbered refId="fig-eplat" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

Our decision to investigate 5β-reduction was **informed by discussions** with researchers in microbiome biology and steroid metabolism. <Ref target="card-08v4z" /> considered <Tooltip text="Chemical conversion of a molecule by living cells or their enzymes.">direct androgen biotransformation</Tooltip> an experimentally tractable starting point because it provides measurable outputs <Tooltip text="In a test tube or culture dish, rather than in a living organism.">in vitro</Tooltip>. <Ref target="card-ttroq" /> supported the biochemical rationale for investigating AKR1D1-mediated testosterone conversion, while emphasizing the difficulty of achieving specificity for a single steroid. Also the reaction is one of the few that is one-way and the products generated (<Citation reference="veerus-et-al-2026" narrative={false} />). Together, this information and these discussions strengthened our decision to begin with a defined enzymatic reaction and assess both its activity and its limitations.

<Ref target="card-vw0vs" /> further encouraged us to distinguish a useful <Tooltip text="A first test showing that an idea can work, before it is developed into something usable.">proof-of-concept</Tooltip> enzyme from a definitive therapeutic target. We therefore approach human AKR1D1 and microbial ci2350 5β-reductases as tools for investigating the proposed mechanism, **without assuming that they would be the most suitable <Tooltip text="The part of the system that carries out the intended action, here the enzyme that transforms the steroid.">effector</Tooltip> for a future clinical application**. The full consultation summaries are available on our [Human Practices page](/human-practices).

For AnTigone, this reaction offers a **defined starting point for engineering**. We can investigate enzyme production, substrate conversion and the biological activity of the resulting mixture as separate experimental questions.&#x20;

### Substrate selectivity: a computational rationale&#x20;

A 5β-reductase acting in the gut encounters a chemically diverse steroid pool. Androgens (mainly testosterone) and <Tooltip text="A class of steroid hormones that includes progesterone.">progestogens</Tooltip> share the same core <Tooltip text="The shared core structure of a group of molecules.">scaffold</Tooltip>, and an enzyme with broad activity could act on both. **Understanding what a candidate enzyme is likely to act on and how that preference might be modified was therefore a necessary step before committing to experimental characterization.**

The structural distinction between these two steroid classes lies at the <Tooltip text="One specific carbon atom on the steroid skeleton, where testosterone and progesterone differ.">**C17 position**</Tooltip>: testosterone carries a <Tooltip text="The OH group attached at C17 in testosterone.">**17β-hydroxyl group**</Tooltip>, while progesterone carries an <Tooltip text="The larger two-carbon functional group attached at C17 in progesterone.">**acetyl group**</Tooltip>. These substituents differ in size and polarity yet occupy the same region of the <Tooltip text="The pocket of an enzyme where the substrate binds and is converted.">active site</Tooltip>. This provides a defined structural target for engineering.

<Ref target="card-cozyv" /> and <Ref target="card-x82l4" /> encouraged us to approach selectivity through <Tooltip text="Designing specific mutations based on a protein's structure, rather than mutating at random.">**rational mutagenesis**</Tooltip>: using the three-dimensional structure of each candidate enzyme to identify active-site <Tooltip text="The individual amino acids that make up a protein.">residues</Tooltip> near C17, selecting substitutions with a chemical basis for altering substrate preference, and screening variants for structural stability before assessing their predicted binding profiles.

This analysis allowed us to generate specific sequence variants with a mechanistic rationale, a structured starting point for experimental characterization rather than an exhaustive screen. The full pipeline is documented on our [Model](/model) page.

### **A predictive model for enzyme activity**

<Tooltip text="A computer simulation that predicts how well a molecule fits into a protein's binding site.">***Docking***</Tooltip>***&#x20;tells us how well a variant might bind a steroid. It does not tell us how that variant would behave in bacterial culture.***

Steroid conversion in bacterial culture depends on more than <Tooltip text="How strongly a molecule sticks to a protein.">binding affinity</Tooltip>: on how many cells are present, how much enzyme cells produce, and how the enzyme handles testosterone and progesterone when both compete for it. To connect these steps, we built a **mathematical model** with three linked layers: **bacterial growth, enzyme <Tooltip text="How much of the enzyme the cells make from its gene.">expression</Tooltip> and enzyme activity**. Our docking results feed into the activity layer, linking the structural analysis to the model.

The model ranks our variants by a testable readout, the time needed to consume half of each steroid, and identifies the parameters the prediction depends on most. It serves as a guide and predictive layer to support our experimental work. Its output is a prediction to be tested, not a forecast of absolute rates. The equations, parameters and results are documented on our [Model](/model#modeling-enzyme-production-and-activity) page.

### **Connecting activity to environmental conditions**&#x20;

The second component of AnTigone is <Tooltip text="Switched on by low oxygen levels, as in the gut.">hypoxia-responsive</Tooltip> expression guided by a hypoxia-responsive <Tooltip text="A stretch of DNA that switches a gene on, so that the cell starts making its protein.">promoter</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="sen-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="wichmann-et-al-2023" narrative={false} />). Our intended design uses low oxygen availability as an environmental input to regulate production of the testosterone-transforming enzyme.&#x20;

The purpose of these designs is to connect enzyme production to the gut low-oxygen input signal." numbered refId="fig-eplat" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

Low oxygen is only one feature of the intended intestinal environment: a response to hypoxia would demonstrate environmental regulation, but would not by itself establish exclusive expression in the gut, a relevant clinical situation or sufficient <Tooltip text="Measures that keep engineered bacteria from surviving or spreading outside their intended place.">biological containment</Tooltip>, which is planned to be implemented in the future following further literature research and expert input.

### **Designing for protein production and recovery**

A final product and a biochemical characterization require sufficient <Tooltip text="Protein that stays dissolved in the cell instead of clumping, so it can be purified and used.">soluble protein</Tooltip>. As we were aware of <Tooltip text="Clumps of misfolded protein that form inside bacteria and are hard to use.">inclusion bodies</Tooltip> and possible problems regarding human <Tooltip text="Made in a host organism from a gene that was introduced into it.">recombinant</Tooltip> protein expression in a microbial system, we decided to incorporate and test <Tooltip text="Affinity tags are short additions to a protein that let it be pulled out of a mixture and purified.">affinity</Tooltip> and <Tooltip text="Protein partners fused to the target to help it stay dissolved and fold properly.">solubility tags</Tooltip>, documented on our [Parts](/parts) page. Inclusion bodies are intracellular aggregates of misfolded or partially folded proteins, which are generally inactive, requiring laborious refolding procedures to regain activity. Due to this, a significant fraction of the expressed protein may be lost during expression (<Citation reference="costa-et-al-2014" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="malik-2016" narrative={false} />).

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/background/general-construct.avif" alt="Modular expression construct showing a T7 promoter, ribosome-binding site, 6×His tag, alternative MBP, SUMO or Trx fusion partners, a GSG linker, ci2350 or AKR1D1, and a terminator." caption="General architecture of the enzyme-expression constructs. The schematic shows a T7 promoter, ribosome-binding site, 6×His affinity tag, alternative solubility partners, a glycine–serine–glycine (GSG) linker, a candidate enzyme coding sequence and a transcriptional terminator. ci2350 and AKR1D1 were investigated using different tag configurations to support protein production and recovery. The diagram summarizes the modular design rather than indicating that every possible combination was constructed and validated; individual constructs are documented on the Parts page." numbered refId="fig-10jlu" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

The construct collection includes a <Tooltip text="A short string of six histidines added to a protein so it can be purified.">6xHis affinity tag</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="bornhorst-et-al-2000" narrative={false} />) and <Tooltip text="Two proteins joined into one.">fusion</Tooltip> designs using <Tooltip text="A well-folding bacterial protein often fused to others to help them dissolve.">maltose-binding protein</Tooltip> (MBP) (<Citation reference="sachdev-et-al-2000" narrative={false} />), <Tooltip text="Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier, a small protein fused to a target to help it fold and stay soluble. It can be cut off again afterwards.">SUMO</Tooltip> (<Citation reference="costa-et-al-2014" narrative={false} />) and <Tooltip text="A small bacterial protein that helps others fold, used as a solubility partner.">Thioredoxin A</Tooltip> (TrxA) (<Citation reference="paraskevopoulou-et-al-2018" narrative={false} />). The chosen designs allow us to investigate whether different fusion partners improve protein availability for downstream characterization and applications. Construct sequences and architectures are documented on the Parts page; experimental iterations and informed decisions are described in [Engineering](/engineering).
