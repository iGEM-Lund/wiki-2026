## <Formula />**Enzyme specificity engineering**

### **Overview**

Two Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductases were taken forward for engineering: human **AKR1D1** and the bacterial enzyme **ci2350** from *Clostridium innocuum*. The aim was to shift substrate preference towards testosterone and away from progesterone, two steroids that differ only at the **C17 substituent**.

Structures were obtained from the PDB (AKR1D1, **3CAV**) and from **AlphaFold** (ci2350), and the functional pocket in each was located with **fpocket** and confirmed against **NCBI CD Search** annotations. Docking both substrates into the wild-type enzymes gave similar absolute affinities but opposite preferences: **AKR1D1 favoured progesterone by 0.5 kcal/mol,** while **ci2350 showed a marginal testosterone preference of 0.1 kcal/mol,** within the scoring noise.

Residues within **4 Å** of each docked ligand were sorted by which substrate they contacted. This identified three specificity determinants in AKR1D1 (**V309, L311, I57**) and six in ci2350 (**A107, R215, V263, V59, F71, Q100**). Substitutions at these positions were designed to weaken progesterone binding or strengthen testosterone binding, then screened for structural stability in **PyRosetta**. Of 24 AKR1D1 variants, 13 passed the **+5 REU** threshold; of 31 ci2350 variants, 17 passed. These variants were then docked with both substrates to assess their specificity towards testosterone.

The full workflow for both AKR1D1 and ci2350, from protein structure acquisition through to stability screening, is described on the [Modeling page](/modeling#enzyme-specificity-engineering-towards-testosterone).&#x20;

### **Docked poses of the AKR1D1 variants**

Thirteen stable AKR1D1 variants were docked with both steroids. Selectivity is reported as <Formula latex="ΔG(testosterone) − ΔG(progesterone)" />, so a **positive value means progesterone is bound more tightly**; the wild type sits at +0.5 kcal/mol. A variant improves only if this value falls.

No variants shifted the preference. The largest gain was 0.1 kcal/mol, far below the ±0.5 kcal/mol noise of the scoring function, so V309S, V309E and I57Y cannot be called improvements. The only changes large enough to read as real went the wrong way: I57N moved the preference 0.4 kcal/mol further towards progesterone.&#x20;

The mutations alter how well the pocket holds a steroid, not which steroid it holds. Testosterone and progesterone differ only at C17, and if a substitution cannot distinguish that one group, it will always move both ligands together.

Two substitutions were examined in detail: V309T, which replaces a valine methyl group with the hydroxyl of threonine, and L311S, which trims a leucine side chain down to serine. Both were intended to introduce polarity near the steroid and make the pocket less comfortable for progesterone’s acetyl group. Both sit above the plane of the progesterone, over the B and C rings, while the C17 substituent extends past them into the open end of the pocket (<Ref target="fig-tonmw" />, <Ref target="fig-bifbg" />, <Ref target="fig-m5401" /> and <Ref target="fig-4z0rf" />).

Since neither mutated side chain reaches the C17 group, there is no direct contact. So, an acetyl group and a hydroxyl look the same to the enzyme, which matches the docking scores: every substitution at these positions moved both substrates by the same amount, leaving the selectivity gap at +0.5 kcal/mol.

**Combo 8 (R215A + V263I)** is the only variant that moved the preference by more than the **±0.5 kcal/mol** noise, shifting it **0.8 kcal/mol** from the wild type, and the combination is structurally well tolerated (**ΔΔG = +1.42 REU**). It is also the only one that does so without giving up affinity (<Ref target="tab-nn49s" />): **progesterone at −8.5** and **testosterone at −9.4** are close to the wild-type values, so the gap comes from holding testosterone more tightly rather than from binding everything more weakly.

**R215** makes a **direct contact** with the **C17 acetyl tail** of progesterone, so replacing it with alanine removes a stabilising interaction that only progesterone uses (<Ref target="fig-6qd5d" /> and <Ref target="fig-t5egj" />). **V263I** works on the same region from the other side: isoleucine is bulkier than valine and appears to **crowd the acetyl tail**, while testosterone, which carries only a compact 17β-hydroxyl at that position, fits the branched side chain more comfortably and binds slightly better.

The two mutations behave very differently on their own. V263I shifts the preference by only −0.2 selectivity and weakens progesterone binding to −7.4 kcal/mol, so the gain in Combo 8 has to come from removing the arginine at 215 (<Ref target="tab-nn49s" />).

**Combo 13 (R215A + V263I + Q100T)** adds Q100T to the Combo 8 backbone (<Ref target="fig-jss2d" />, <Ref target="fig-m43ze" />). **Q100T** offers the same hydrogen-bonding capacity from a shorter side chain. In combination the variant reaches **ΔΔG = −7.19 REU**, the most stable candidate in the set, which helps in obtaining enough soluble protein to characterise. The cost is selectivity: the preference falls to −0.5 kcal/mol, within the noise floor. Combo 13 is therefore the safer choice if stability proves limiting, rather than the better one on specificity.

## **Mathematical modelling of enzyme production and activity**

### **Overview**

The aim was to predict how much enzyme a culture produces, and what that enzyme does once both steroids are present. Three layers were built and run as one system: a **growth model** for the culture, an **expression model** for how much enzyme each cell carries, and an **activity model** for how the enzyme divides its time between testosterone and progesterone.

Growth follows a logistic curve fitted to our own OD600 measurements, which sets the maximum growth rate, the carrying capacity and the starting density. Expression covers transcription, translation and dilution by growth. The activity layer treats the two steroids as competing for a single active site, so the preference predicted from docking determines which one is turned over.

Because this layer depends on binding, it is tied to the specificity work. Since AKR1D1 showed no useful shift in preference, the **activity model was run for ci2350 and its variants**, using Michaelis constants derived from the docking scores.

Most parameters are literature values rather than measurements from our own strain, so the model was tested rather than taken at face value. Each was varied by **±20% one at a time** to see which ones the output depends on, and a Monte Carlo analysis drew all of them at random together to check whether the conclusions hold across the plausible range. The results of both analyses were reported below.

The full description of the three layers, their equations, and assumptions is on the [Modelling page](https://wiki.igemlund.org/modeling#:~:text=Integrated%20growth%20and%20expression%20model).

### **Prediction of integrated enzyme production model**

#### **Baseline behaviour**

With the baseline parameters, the culture reaches **OD600 = 0.5 at 1.58 h**, which is the induction point, and 99% of its carrying capacity (K = 15) at 6.96 hr. mRNA rises almost immediately after induction and reaches 99% of its steady state (<Formula latex="m* = α/δm = 0.525" />) within **0.48 hr**. **Enzyme builds up more slowly**. Per cell, it reaches 4.9 relative units when growth saturates and then keeps rising almost linearly to **31.7** at 24 hr. After growth stops, dilution is almost zero, so everything translated from the still-present mRNA stays in the cell (<Ref target="fig-njrcu" />).

This baseline result shapes the sweeps that follow. The enzyme level is set mainly by the mRNA supply (α, δm) and the translation rate (β), while growth acts mostly through dilution.

#### **Sensitivity to individual parameters**

Each parameter was swept by ±20% with all others held at baseline, and the change in the final value at 24 hr was recorded and compared with the baseline result (<Ref target="tab-n9je3" />).

In the sweeps, the amount of enzyme in each cell follows the mRNA supply, while the growth parameters mostly decide how many cells there are. Transcription (α) and mRNA decay (δm) set the mRNA pool through <Formula latex="m* = α/δm" />, and the enzyme follows that pool. A 20% change in α changes mRNA and enzyme by the same 20%, and biomass is unaffected (<Ref target="fig-3zpvt" /> and <Ref target="fig-tq7xd" />). The response to δm is not symmetric, because mRNA falls as 1/δm: a 20% lower decay rate raises the enzyme by 25%, while a 20% higher one lowers it by 17%.

Together, these results show that each cell’s own expression machinery (transcription, mRNA lifetime and translation) decides how much enzyme it holds, while growth decides how many cells there are. The total enzyme in the culture is enzyme per cell multiplied by biomass, which changes with K. Consequently, the expression parameters, especially the poorly known mRNA half-life, change enzyme production strongly by more than 2.5-fold during sweep analysis, which requires careful experimental design.

#### **Expression uncertainty**

To ask how much the enzyme level could vary if all inputs are uncertain, three Monte Carlo analyses were run with 500 draws each, sampling parameters with a 10% standard deviation around their baseline values.

When only the growth parameters (K, µmax) were varied, the final enzyme stayed in a narrow band (−4% to +3% change) around the baseline, due to the dilution effect (<Ref target="fig-1qxmw" />). When only the expression parameters (α, δm) were varied, the spread was much wider (<Ref target="fig-qz0oh" />). Varying all four together gave almost the same spread as seen from the expression parameters, which means uncertainty in growth hardly matters (<Ref target="fig-v5bah" />). From the uncertainty, the estimated enzyme was around 31 relative units per cell.

### **Prediction of activity model**

To predict how the engineered enzyme would behave in the cell, the expression model was linked to a competitive Michaelis–Menten model of testosterone and progesterone conversion This section tests R215A + V263I, the variant selected in the [Enzyme specificity engineering results](https://wiki.igemlund.org/results#enzyme-specificity-engineering). We first show the baseline run and then test how robust it is to the expression and growth settings, the kcat ratio, the docking scores, and random errors in all of them together. Throughout, the speed ratio (t50 of progesterone divided by t50 of testosterone) measures which steroid is converted first.

#### **Baseline and sweep effect on enzyme activity**

For R215A + V263I, the culture reaches OD 0.5 and is induced at 1.58 hr. Enzyme per cell then rises to about 3 relative units by 5.58 hr, when both steroids are added at 50 µM. By then the culture is close to its maximum density (about OD 14). After substrate addition, testosterone is consumed first and is nearly gone within about 1.1 hr, with progesterone following within about 1.4 hr (<Ref target="fig-1sg1g" />). The half-times (t50) are 29.1 min for testosterone and 58.5 min for progesterone, a speed ratio (<Formula latex="t50(P) / t50(T)" />) of 2.0 in favour of testosterone.

#### **Overall findings:**

* **Target selection:** Of the two enzymes, only ci2350 offered a way to favour testosterone, since in AKR1D1 the mutated residues sit too far from C17 to tell the two steroids apart.
* **Best candidate:** R215A + V263I is the strongest design. It works by weakening progesterone binding while testosterone binding stays strong, and the enzyme stays stable.
* **Integrated model:** Growth and expression together determine how much enzyme each culture makes. Enzyme per cell keeps rising after growth stops, and mRNA half-life matters most for how much enzyme is produced. This tells us which expression conditions to control in the experiments.
* **Predicted behaviour:** In the cell model, the R215A + V263I variant is expected to convert testosterone before progesterone, and this is its most robust property among the variants tested.
* **Predictive layer:** The mathematical model links the design, the expression and the activity steps into one framework. It predicts which conditions to control and what to measure first, so it can guide experimental design and be updated as results come in.

## **Developing an enzymatic activity assay**

#### Establishing a reliable spectrophotometric readout

To investigate the testosterone-transforming activity of our candidate enzymes, we developed a continuous spectrophotometric assay based on monitoring reduced-cofactor absorbance at approximately 340 nm.

Our initial experiments used CI2350–MBP preparations in a microplate format. However, the resulting absorbance traces showed abrupt fluctuations and considerable variability between replicates. Some reactions without protein exhibited larger absorbance decreases than enzyme-containing samples, and increasing protein concentration did not produce a consistent response. Multiple troubleshooting iteration identified errors in reaction preparation, including a tenfold stock-concentration calculation error and visible testosterone precipitation. We corrected the assay composition and improved substrate preparation, after which no visible crystals were observed and the absorbance traces became more stable.&#x20;

NADPH calibration experiments carried in microplates <Ref target="fig-77hua" /> failed to establish a reproducible and logical relationship between cofactor concentration and absorbance readout. Preparing fresh cofactor did not fully resolve this issue. We therefore switched to a different spectrophotometer and UV-transparent microcuvettes. Wavelength scans revealed the expected absorbance peak near 340 nm, confirming that the reduced-cofactor signal could be detected under the revised measurement conditions. Based on these observations, subsequent measurements were performed using cuvettes rather than microplates.

### Evaluating Testosterone-Associated Cofactor Consumption

In our final NADH-based measurements, we compared CI2350–MBP reactions containing testosterone with corresponding reactions lacking the steroid substrate.

As reported in figure () the testosterone-containing reaction showed a 12.6% decrease in absorbance at 340 nm over 30 minutes. However, the no-substrate control showed a nearly identical decrease of 12.5%. This similarity indicated that most of the observed cofactor consumption was not dependent on the addition of testosterone under the tested conditions. To facilitate comparison, we also expressed the measurements as the change in absorbance relative to the initial value. This transformation removed differences in starting absorbance while preserving the original reaction and control signals.

The resulting traces confirmed that reactions with and without testosterone exhibited comparable decreases, providing no clear evidence of additional testosterone-associated cofactor consumption. These findings were particularly relevant considering the limitations identified during protein purification. Despite IMAC purification and subsequent centrifugal concentration, the resulting preparations contained additional proteins, which may have contributed to background cofactor consumption.

However, the available measurements could not identify the source of this background activity or determine whether CI2350 contributed to the observed signal.

Overall, our experiments established a more reliable spectrophotometric measurement format and identified important limitations in interpreting cofactor consumption. **The final assay did not provide sufficient evidence to confirm CI2350-dependent testosterone conversion.**

Further work will require improved protein purification, appropriately matched controls, and direct analysis of steroid substrates and products, for example using LC–MS/MS. Complementary androgen-receptor assays would then be necessary to determine whether any confirmed transformation affects androgenic activity.

## **Future Experiments**

Future work regarding enzyme activity include going back to protein purification and trying to obtain pure protein samples, adding ELISA, LC/MS-MS and androgen receptors assays in such a way testosterone quantities can be identified directly, even when enzyme kinetic is not clear enough.

Next steps in In-Silico experiments:

* **Consensus docking:** Re-dock the top variants with a second tool (e.g. GNINA or DiffDock) and keep only the variants that rank well in both.
* **Other steroids:** Dock the top variants against related steroids that could compete in the cell, to see whether the testosterone preference holds beyond progesterone.
* **Cofactor in the model:** Add NADPH supply to the activity model, since the reaction depends on it.
* **Dose and conditions:** Run the integrated model for different starting steroid concentrations, culture densities and induction timings, to find the conditions where the preference is largest.
* **Experimental values in the model:** The model can be run again with the measured activity values in place of the docking scores, to see how the predictions change. &#x20;
