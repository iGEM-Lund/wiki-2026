title: Results
---
Two Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductases were taken forward for engineering: human **AKR1D1** and the bacterial enzyme **ci2350** from *Clostridium innocuum*. Of the two, only ci2350 offered a route to testosterone selectivity, whereas in AKR1D1 the engineered residues sit too far from C17 to distinguish the two steroids, and every substitution moved both ligands equally. R215A + V263I emerged as the strongest ci2350 design, weakening progesterone binding while keeping testosterone affinity and structural stability intact, and the mathematical model predicted a 2-fold testosterone preference under realistic kinetic and expression conditions, setting the benchmark for the wet lab. Expression work ran in parallel: AKR1D1 reached at best 37 ± 4% solubility with TrxA at 0.2 mM IPTG but was never purified or assayed, while ci2350-MBP was recovered by IMAC, though every eluate carried persistent \~38 kDa and \~70 kDa contaminants and the induced empty-vector control gave about three-quarters as much total protein by Bradford. In the final NADH-based activity assay, the wild-type ci2350-MBP testosterone-containing reaction and the no-substrate control showed nearly identical absorbance decreases (12.6% vs 12.5%), so even baseline activity could not be confirmed, leaving the predicted 2-fold preference of R215A + V263I untested. Cleaner preparations, matched controls, and direct LC–MS/MS quantification of steroid conversion will be needed to close the loop between the computational predictions and experimental evidence.

## <Formula />**Enzyme specificity engineering**

### **Overview**

The aim was to shift substrate preference towards testosterone and away from progesterone, two steroids that differ only at the <Tooltip text="The chemical group attached to carbon 17 of the steroid ring system, where testosterone and progesterone differ.">**C17 substituent**</Tooltip>.

Structures were obtained from the <Tooltip text="Protein Data Bank, a public database of experimentally determined protein structures.">PDB</Tooltip> (AKR1D1, [**3CAV**](https://www.rcsb.org/structure/3CAV)) and from <Tooltip text="An AI program that predicts a protein's 3D structure from its amino acid sequence.">**AlphaFold**</Tooltip> (ci2350), and the functional pocket in each was located with <Tooltip text="Software that finds cavities (pockets) on a protein's surface where molecules could bind.">**fpocket**</Tooltip> and confirmed against <Tooltip text="A tool that matches a protein sequence to known protein domains and marks their key positions.">**NCBI CD Search**</Tooltip> annotations. <Tooltip text="A computer simulation that predicts how well a molecule fits into a protein's binding site.">Docking</Tooltip> both substrates into the <Tooltip text="The natural, unmodified version of an enzyme.">wild-type</Tooltip> enzymes gave similar absolute affinities but opposite preferences: **AKR1D1 favored progesterone by 0.5 kcal/mol,** while **ci2350 showed a marginal testosterone preference of 0.1 kcal/mol,** within the scoring noise.

Residues within **4 <Tooltip text="Angstrom: unit of length equaling 10^−10 m">Å</Tooltip>** of each docked ligand were sorted by which substrate they contacted. This identified three specificity determinants in AKR1D1 (**V309, L311, I57**) and six in ci2350 (**A107, R215, V263, V59, F71, Q100**). Substitutions at these positions were designed to weaken progesterone binding or strengthen testosterone binding, then screened for structural stability in <Tooltip text="Software for modeling protein structures and predicting how mutations change their stability.">**PyRosetta**</Tooltip>. Of 24 AKR1D1 variants, 13 passed the **+5 REU** threshold; of 31 ci2350 variants, 17 passed. These variants were then docked with both substrates to assess their specificity towards testosterone.

The full workflow for both AKR1D1 and ci2350, from protein structure acquisition through to mutant docking, is described on the [Model page](/model#enzyme-specificity-engineering).&#x20;

### **Docked poses of the AKR1D1 variants**

Thirteen stable AKR1D1 variants were docked with both steroids. Selectivity is reported as <Formula latex="ΔG(testosterone) − ΔG(progesterone)" />, so a **positive value means progesterone is bound more tightly**; the wild type sits at +0.5 kcal/mol. A variant improves only if this value falls.

No variants shifted the preference. The largest gain was 0.1 kcal/mol, far below the ±0.5 kcal/mol noise of the <Tooltip text="The formula the docking software uses to estimate how strongly a molecule binds.">scoring function</Tooltip>, so V309S, V309E and I57Y cannot be called improvements. The only changes large enough to read as real went the wrong way: I57N moved the preference 0.4 kcal/mol further towards progesterone.&#x20;

The mutations alter how well the pocket holds a steroid, not which steroid it holds. Testosterone and progesterone differ only at C17, and if a substitution cannot distinguish that one group, it will always move both ligands together.

Two substitutions were examined in detail: V309T, which replaces a valine methyl group with the <Tooltip text="An -OH group: an oxygen atom bonded to a hydrogen atom.">hydroxyl</Tooltip> of threonine, and L311S, which trims a leucine side chain down to serine. Both were intended to introduce polarity near the steroid and make the pocket less comfortable for progesterone's <Tooltip text="A small group of atoms (CH3-C=O) attached at C17 in progesterone.">acetyl group</Tooltip>. Both sit above the plane of the progesterone, over the B and C rings, while the C17 substituent extends past them into the open end of the pocket (<Ref target="fig-tonmw" />, <Ref target="fig-bifbg" />, <Ref target="fig-m5401" /> and <Ref target="fig-4z0rf" />).

Since neither mutated side chain reaches the C17 group, there is no direct contact. So, an acetyl group and a hydroxyl look the same to the enzyme, which matches the docking scores: every substitution at these positions moved both substrates by the same amount, leaving the selectivity gap at +0.5 kcal/mol.

**Combo 8 (R215A + V263I)** is the only variant that moved the preference by more than the **±0.5 kcal/mol** noise, shifting it **0.8 kcal/mol** from the wild type, and the combination is structurally well tolerated (**ΔΔG = +1.42 <Tooltip text="Rosetta Energy Units, the score for how stable a protein structure is. Lower is more stable.">REU</Tooltip>**). It is also the only one that does so without giving up affinity (<Ref target="tab-nn49s" />): **progesterone at −8.5** and **testosterone at −9.4** are close to the wild-type values, so the gap comes from holding testosterone more tightly rather than from binding everything more weakly.

**R215** makes a **direct contact** with the **C17 acetyl tail** of progesterone, so replacing it with alanine removes a stabilizing interaction that only progesterone uses (<Ref target="fig-6qd5d" /> and <Ref target="fig-t5egj" />). **V263I** works on the same region from the other side: isoleucine is bulkier than valine and appears to **crowd the acetyl tail**, while testosterone, which carries only a compact 17β-hydroxyl at that position, fits the branched side chain more comfortably and binds slightly better.

The two mutations behave very differently on their own. V263I shifts the preference by only −0.2 selectivity and weakens progesterone binding to −7.4 kcal/mol, so the gain in Combo 8 has to come from removing the arginine at 215 (<Ref target="tab-nn49s" />).

**Combo 13 (R215A + V263I + Q100T)** adds Q100T to the Combo 8 backbone (<Ref target="fig-jss2d" /> and <Ref target="fig-m43ze" />). **Q100T** offers the same hydrogen-bonding capacity from a shorter side chain. In combination the variant reaches **ΔΔG = −7.19 REU**, the most stable candidate in the set, which helps in obtaining enough soluble protein to characterize. The cost is selectivity: the preference falls to −0.5 kcal/mol, within the noise floor. Combo 13 is therefore the safer choice if stability proves limiting, rather than the better one on specificity.

V263K also reaches −0.5 kcal/mol selectivity, but by a different route. ## **Modeling enzyme production and activity**

### **Overview**

The aim was to predict how much enzyme a culture produces, and what that enzyme does once both steroids are present. Three layers were built and run as one system: a **growth model** for the culture, an **expression model** for how much enzyme each cell carries, and an **activity model** for how the enzyme divides its time between testosterone and progesterone.

Growth follows a <Tooltip text="A growth curve that starts exponential and levels off as the culture fills its container.">logistic curve</Tooltip> fitted to our own OD600 measurements, which sets the maximum growth rate, the <Tooltip text="The maximum culture density that the medium can support.">carrying capacity</Tooltip> and the starting density. Expression covers transcription, translation and <Tooltip text="As cells divide, the enzyme in each cell is shared between the two daughters, which lowers its concentration.">dilution by growth</Tooltip>. The activity layer treats the two steroids as competing for a single active site, so the preference predicted from docking determines which one is turned over.

Because this layer depends on binding, it is tied to the specificity work. Since AKR1D1 showed no useful shift in preference, the **activity model was run for ci2350 and its variants**, mainly on R215A + V263I, using <Tooltip text="The substrate concentration at which an enzyme works at half its top speed (Km). Lower means tighter binding.">Michaelis constants</Tooltip> derived from the docking scores.

Most parameters are literature values rather than measurements from our own strain, so the model was tested rather than taken at face value. Each was varied by **±20% one at a time** to see which ones the output depends on, and a Monte Carlo analysis drew all of them at random together to check whether the conclusions hold across the plausible range. The results of both analyses are reported below.

The full description of the three layers, their equations, and assumptions is on the [Model page](/model#modeling-enzyme-production-and-activity).

### **Prediction of integrated enzyme production model**

#### **Baseline behavior**

With the baseline parameters, the culture reaches **OD600 = 0.5 at 1.58 h**, which is the induction point, and 99% of its carrying capacity (K = 15) at 6.96 hr. mRNA rises almost immediately after induction and reaches 99% of its steady state (<Formula latex="m* = α/δm = 0.525" />) within **0.48 hr**. **Enzyme builds up more slowly**. Per cell, it reaches 4.9 relative units when growth saturates and then keeps rising almost linearly to **31.7** at 24 hr. After growth stops, dilution is almost zero, so everything translated from the still-present mRNA stays in the cell (<Ref target="fig-njrcu" />).

This baseline result shapes the sweeps that follow. The enzyme level is set mainly by the mRNA supply (α, δm) and the translation rate (β), while growth acts mostly through dilution.

#### **Sensitivity to individual parameters**

Each parameter was swept by ±20% with all others held at baseline, and the change in the final value at 24 hr was recorded and compared with the baseline result (<Ref target="tab-n9je3" />).

  | δm (mRNA decay)   | 0%          | +25% / −17% | +25% / −17%         |

In the sweeps, the amount of enzyme in each cell follows the mRNA supply, while the growth parameters mostly decide how many cells there are. Transcription (α) and mRNA decay (δm) set the mRNA pool through <Formula latex="m* = α/δm" />, and the enzyme follows that pool. A 20% change in α changes mRNA and enzyme by the same 20%, and biomass is unaffected (<Ref target="fig-3zpvt" /> and <Ref target="fig-tq7xd" />). The response to δm is not symmetric, because mRNA falls as <Formula latex="1/δm" />: a 20% lower decay rate raises the enzyme by 25%, while a 20% higher one lowers it by 17%.

Together, these results show that each cell's own expression machinery (transcription, mRNA lifetime and translation) decides how much enzyme it holds, while growth decides how many cells there are. The total enzyme in the culture is enzyme per cell multiplied by biomass, which changes with K. Consequently, the expression parameters, especially the poorly known mRNA half-life, change enzyme production strongly by more than 2.5-fold during sweep analysis, which requires careful experimental design.

#### **Expression uncertainty**

To ask how much the enzyme level could vary if all inputs are uncertain, three Monte Carlo analyses were run with 500 draws each, sampling parameters with a 10% standard deviation around their baseline values.

When only the growth parameters (K, µmax) were varied, the final enzyme stayed in a narrow band (−4% to +3% change) around the baseline, due to the dilution effect (<Ref target="fig-1qxmw" />). When only the expression parameters (α, δm) were varied, the spread was much wider (<Ref target="fig-qz0oh" />). Varying all four together gave almost the same spread as seen from the expression parameters, which means uncertainty in growth hardly matters (<Ref target="fig-v5bah" />). From the uncertainty, the estimated enzyme was around 31 relative units per cell.

### **Prediction of activity model**

To predict how the engineered enzyme would behave in the cell, the **expression model was linked to a competitive Michaelis–Menten model** of testosterone and progesterone conversion. This section tests **R215A + V263I**, the variant selected in the [Enzyme specificity engineering results](/results#enzyme-specificity-engineering). We first show the baseline run and then test how robust it is to the expression and growth settings, the <Tooltip text="The turnover number: how many substrate molecules one enzyme converts per second when fully busy.">kcat</Tooltip> ratio, the docking scores, and random errors in all of them together. Throughout, the speed ratio <Formula latex="t50(P)/t50(T)" /> measures which steroid is converted first.

#### **Baseline and sweep effect on enzyme activity**

For R215A + V263I, the culture reaches OD 0.5 and is induced at 1.58 hr. Enzyme per cell then rises to about 3 relative units by 5.58 hr, when both steroids are added at 50 µM. By then the culture is close to its maximum density (about OD 14). After substrate addition, testosterone is consumed first and is nearly gone within about 1.1 hr, with progesterone following within about 1.4 hr (<Ref target="fig-1sg1g" />). The half-times (t50) are **29.1 min** for testosterone and **58.5 min** for progesterone, a **speed ratio of 2.0 in favor of testosterone**.

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/dry-lab-3d-model/layer3-baseline-t50.avif" alt="image of layer3_baseline_t50" caption="Baseline simulation for R215A + V263I. Testosterone is consumed first (t50 29.1 min against 58.5 min)" numbered refId="fig-1sg1g" scale={75} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

The total enzyme depends on the number of cells, transcription (α), translation (β), kcat, E\_scale, growth rate (µmax), culture density (K) and later induction. #### **Overall findings:**

* **Target selection:** Of the two enzymes, only ci2350 offered a way to favor testosterone, since in AKR1D1 the mutated residues sit too far from C17 to tell the two steroids apart.
* **Best candidate:** R215A + V263I is the strongest design. It works by weakening progesterone binding while testosterone binding stays strong, and the enzyme stays stable.
* **Integrated model:** Growth and expression together determine how much enzyme each culture makes. Enzyme per cell keeps rising after growth stops, and mRNA half-life matters most for how much enzyme is produced. This tells us which expression conditions to control in the experiments.
* **Predicted behavior:** In the cell model, the R215A + V263I variant is expected to convert testosterone before progesterone, and this is its most robust property among the variants tested.
* **Predictive layer:** The mathematical model links the design, the expression and the activity steps into one framework. It predicts which conditions to control and what to measure first, so it can guide experimental design and be updated as results come in.

## ci2350: Expression, Purification and Purity

ci2350 was expressed as an N-terminal fusion (6xHis, MBP, ci2350; \~112 kDa). ci2350-MBP was expressed and captured by <Tooltip text="Immobilized metal affinity chromatography: purifying His-tagged proteins by binding them to nickel beads.">IMAC</Tooltip>, but every <Tooltip text="The liquid that comes off a column after the protein has been released from it.">eluate</Tooltip> contained other bands, mainly at \~38 and \~70 kDa. Our preparations were therefore not pure. Each SDS-gel was a single experiment without replicates, so the differences are observations and not tested conclusions.

### Expression in whole cells

ci2350-6xHis (\~70 kDa) showed no visible band at 0, 0.25 or 0.4 mM IPTG, after 3 h or overnight at 37 °C (<Ref target="fig-e658y" />). ci2350-MBP showed a band at the expected \~112 kDa in all lanes. It was faint at 0 mM after 3 h and strongest at 0.25 and 0.4 mM after 3 h. A <Tooltip text="A line fitted to bands or samples of known size or amount, used to estimate unknown ones.">standard curve</Tooltip> from this gel put the band at \~114 kDa (five lanes, 111.8 to 117.0 kDa; <Ref target="fig-5l3za" />).

At 0.4 mM IPTG, ci2350-MBP expression was strongest after 3 h at 37 °C and weaker after overnight growth at 25 °C. No 112 kDa band was visible before induction (<Ref target="fig-f0ilx" />).

### IMAC purification

ci2350-6xHis (0.8 mM, 3 h, 37 °C) showed a \~70 kDa band in the flow-through, and the eluate was empty (<Ref target="fig-6dgv4" />). The protein was expressed but did not bind the column. We did not test why. This run is not matched to the MBP runs, which used other temperatures and times.

For ci2350-MBP, the eluate from 0.8 mM IPTG at 17 °C overnight had a thick \~112 kDa band. The eluate from 0.4 mM at 37 °C overnight had none (<Ref target="fig-1x3gz" />). Concentrating the 17 °C eluate on a <Tooltip text="A spin filter that concentrates a protein sample by pushing liquid through a membrane that holds the protein back.">Vivaspin column</Tooltip> (30 kDa cut-off) removed most of the 112 kDa band.&#x20;

In a second run, 37 °C for 3 h and 25 °C overnight (both 0.4 mM IPTG) were purified one after the other on the same column, with a wash in between (<Ref target="fig-nwl0y" />). Each eluate was run on the gel before and after concentration on a Vivaspin column. Bands read from the gel can be found in <Ref target="tab-h8top" />.

## **Developing an enzymatic activity assay**

#### Establishing a reliable spectrophotometric readout

To investigate the testosterone-transforming activity of our candidate enzymes, we developed a continuous spectrophotometric assay based on monitoring reduced-cofactor absorbance at approximately 340 nm.

Our initial experiments used ci2350–MBP preparations in a microplate format. However, the resulting absorbance traces showed abrupt fluctuations and considerable variability between replicates. Some reactions without protein exhibited larger absorbance decreases than enzyme-containing samples, and increasing protein concentration did not produce a consistent response. Multiple troubleshooting iterations identified errors in reaction preparation, including a tenfold stock-concentration calculation error and visible testosterone precipitation. We corrected the assay composition and improved substrate preparation, after which no visible crystals were observed and the absorbance traces became more stable.&#x20;

NADPH calibration experiments carried out in microplates <Ref target="fig-77hua" /> failed to establish a reproducible and logical relationship between cofactor concentration and absorbance readout. Preparing fresh cofactor did not fully resolve this issue. We therefore switched to a different spectrophotometer and <Tooltip text="Tiny sample holders that let ultraviolet light through, so absorbance at 340 nm can be measured.">UV-transparent microcuvettes</Tooltip>. <Tooltip text="Measuring how much light a sample absorbs across a range of wavelengths.">Wavelength scans</Tooltip> revealed the expected absorbance peak near 340 nm, confirming that the reduced-cofactor signal could be detected under the revised measurement conditions. Based on these observations, subsequent measurements were performed using cuvettes rather than microplates.

#### AKR1D1: exploratory measurements&#x20;

AKR1D1 preparations were also investigated during assay development, including constructs carrying different fusion tags: TrxA and SUMO.

Following the inconsistent results obtained in microplates, we performed further measurements using UV-transparent microcuvettes. Although these experiments allowed us to investigate the behavior of AKR1D1-containing preparations under revised and reliable assay conditions, initial enzyme activity is shown in graphs A <Ref target="fig-77hua" /> and B <Ref target="fig-77hua" />; at this point only NADPH natural oxidation was monitored as a negative control. Consequently, AKR1D1 measurements have to be considered exploratory as for now we did not establish testosterone-dependent enzymatic activity. All measurements were performed in duplicate and/or triplicate, including negative controls. As we assumed correct enzymatic activity we proceeded with ci2350 enzyme, prioritizing the construct for further assay development. Unlike AKR1D1, ci2350 had not been characterized for testosterone conversion in our experimental system and was also the focus of our computational enzyme-engineering work. This would have allowed us to investigate the experimentally produced ci2350 variants, particularly the R215A + V263I double mutant, with the longer-term objective of evaluating their predicted substrate preferences.

### ci2350: Investigating Cofactor Consumption and Background Activity

Previous work identified NADH as a cofactor for ci2350 (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />),. However, because NADPH was initially the only reduced cofactor available to us, we began our experiments using NADPH. This was an exploratory choice informed by reports of cofactor utilization among related bacterial steroid reductases (<Citation reference="changli-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />). Once NADH became available, we incorporated it into subsequent activity measurements.

An important challenge during assay development was determining whether the observed cofactor consumption could be attributed to ci2350 or to other components of the enzyme preparations. We introduced additional controls, based on input from Savvina we decided to introduce two new negative controls, to assess whether enzyme activity could realistically be attributed to our candidate enzyme, data are shown in  <Ref target="fig-77hua" />. The controls include heat-treated assay preparations and material derived from *E. coli* carrying the empty expression vector. The empty-vector control was used to assess potential background contributions from host-derived proteins or the purification procedure.

### Evaluating Testosterone-Associated Cofactor Consumption

In our final NADH-based measurements, we compared ci2350–MBP reactions containing testosterone with corresponding reactions lacking the steroid substrate.

As reported in figure () the testosterone-containing reaction showed a 12.6% decrease in absorbance at 340 nm over 30 minutes. However, the no-substrate control showed a nearly identical decrease of 12.5%. This similarity indicated that most of the observed cofactor consumption was not dependent on the addition of testosterone under the tested conditions. To facilitate comparison, we also expressed the measurements as the change in absorbance relative to the initial value. This transformation removed differences in starting absorbance while preserving the original reaction and control signals.

The resulting traces confirmed that reactions with and without testosterone exhibited comparable decreases, providing no clear evidence of additional testosterone-associated cofactor consumption. These findings were particularly relevant considering the limitations identified during protein purification. Despite IMAC purification and subsequent centrifugal concentration, the resulting preparations contained additional proteins, which may have contributed to background cofactor consumption.

  However, the available measurements could not identify the source of this background activity or determine whether ci2350 contributed to the observed signal. The final assay did not provide sufficient evidence to confirm ci2350-dependent testosterone conversion. This means the docking-based predictions of R215A + V263I, which forecast a 2-fold testosterone preference, could not be validated at this stage.  &#x20;

## **Hypoxia-responsive promoter**

To implement a safety mechanism that restricts enzyme production to the anaerobic gut environment, we designed hypoxia-inducible constructs for AKR1D1-SUMO-6xHis and ci2350-MBP-6xHis. These constructs placed enzyme expression under the control of the hypoxia-responsive promoter **PfnrS** ([BBa\_25DMNRIB](https://registry.igem.org/parts/bba-25dmnrib)), and the <Tooltip text="A DNA sequence that tells the cell's machinery to stop copying the gene.">lambda t0 terminator</Tooltip> ([BBa\_26P9V9ID](https://registry.igem.org/parts/bba-26p9v9id)), ensuring production would only occur in low-oxygen conditions typical of the intestinal tract.

Both constructs were ordered as clonal genes from Twist Bioscience, synthesized directly into the pET-Blank-Amp backbone with a custom promoter-RBS region containing PfnrS, spacer after it, and <Tooltip text="Ribosome binding site: where the ribosome attaches to the mRNA to start making protein.">RBS</Tooltip> (<Ref target="fig-wzks1" /> and <Ref target="fig-rc36p" />).
