## Introduction

While talking with different stakeholders during our project, they often asked us "What are Human Practices?" Initially we answered that human practices are interviewing experts on certain topics, and getting their feedback. But as time passed, and many times we had answered this question, we realized that this is not what the true meaning of human practices is. We realized that it is not bombarding academics with questions and awaiting their replies, or asking companies if they would be interested in selling our product, or asking clinicians about how they conduct their clinical practice. It is about listening, about carefully reading between the lines, about looking at the bigger picture. We learned that every person has their unique perspective and story to tell, and it is on us to share it. 

Every talk we had was different, with different topics discussed, different opinions, and different questions arising. Each talk was individually assessed, to see how we can implement it into our project, how we can give it a voice, and how we can contribute to bridging the gaps mentioned. Even though every talk was different, most of them agreed on one thing: there is not enough research on PMOS.

<Quote quote="I love when you do research on PMOS because honestly, there really isn't enough." quotee="Eleni Dubé Zinatelli" figure="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/human-practices/eleni-dub-zinatelli.avif" />

After many talks, we became increasingly aware of how big the gap can be. It shocked us. Diagnostic delay, lack of proper medication and lack of awareness, just to name a few. They can all be addressed by doing more research on PMOS, because currently, there is no known cause for this syndrome, nor a proper way to treat it. We wanted to bridge that gap, at least a little, because 10-13% of women suffer from this condition. That is why we dedicated our project to PMOS, to raise awareness about it. There can never be enough awareness.

## Stakeholders

For a project to move beyond a proof-of-concept, it has to consider, and be considered by, people outside of the laboratory. A therapeutic can reach a patient only if it can be developed, manufactured, classified, prescribed, afforded and trusted. Each of those steps belongs to a different group of people with its own priorities and reasons to say no. Throughout the project, we have tried to reach most of these groups, and treat them as participants in the design by applying their feedback, rather than treating them as obstacles.

By mapping the path our product would have to travel from a construct in the lab to a therapeutic in the clinic, we have identified five groups of stakeholders. They do not form a list, but rather an interdependent cycle: researchers develop, manufacturers produce, regulators establish safety, clinicians prescribe, and the patients give their feedback, redirecting the research. Each group affects the one after it, and the last one closes the cycle back to the first.

We spoke with specialists in steroid-transforming enzymes, PMOS and hyperandrogenism research, microbiome containment, sustainability practice, clinical practice, and innovation management, just to name a few. Each conversation is summarized below, together with what we learned and how it shaped the project.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>

    Summary of interview:

    We consulted Dr. Anna Benrick, whose research focuses on the molecular link between androgen excess and the development of reproductive disorders, obesity, and type-2 diabetes in women. The interview was centered on the feasibility of the AnTigone project, whether locally reducing testosterone in the gut could meaningfully affect systemic testosterone levels and reduce hyperandrogenism symptoms. Despite promising preclinical findings, Dr. Benrick emphasized that the gut microbiota-PMOS field is still young, and it remains unclear whether microbiota changes drive PMOS or result from it.

    She pointed out a recent paper on artemisin, an antimalarial compound shown to lower testosterone production by inhibiting a testosterone synthesis enzyme, suggesting an alternative approach to reducing systemic testosterone levels. A recurring topic in the interview was the uncertainty about gut-to-serum steroid circulation: how much testosterone actually passes through the gut relative to what is produced by ovaries and adrenal glands. This uncertainty was raised to answer one of our main questions, whether our approach could achieve a biologically relevant effect. We had discussed the possibility of incorporating a testosterone-sensing mechanism to induce enzyme expression and deactivate it if reduction became too high. She advised not to pursue this, but to prioritize stable, strong enzyme expression, since insufficient effect was deemed more likely to happen than excessive androgen lowering. Furthermore, she did not anticipate a compensatory increase in androgen production in response to our treatment.

    The conversation also touched on validation strategy, including in vitro options such as testosterone-producing theca cells, and candidate biomarkers such as testosterone, androstenedione and hydroxyandrogens, alongside clinical symptoms such as menstruation frequency or body hair. Dr. Benrick raised the possibility that a gut-localized, low-risk approach could be relevant for PMOS treatment during pregnancy, a period in which most hormonal therapies are avoided due to their possibility of affecting fetal development.

    Integration:

    * **Expression system design** - adopting a hypoxia-responsive promoter as a fixed gut on/off switch
    * **Project framing** - framing the project as an early-stage hypothesis rather than a validated therapeutic mechanism

    Summary of interview:

    We contacted Dr. Chan because of his expertise in genetic circuits and biocontainment systems. We wanted his perspective on what biosafety measures we should implement.  

    Dr. Chan outlined two main approaches for biocontainment strategies: non-canonical amino acid incorporation and kill-switches, and pros and cons of each. Dr. Chan informed us that modern biocontainment is multi-layered, and uses 3-4 different mechanisms simultaneously, since no single mechanism is sufficient. We also discussed his novel kill-switch design, a CRISPR-based genetic circuit, that instead of killing bacteria, uses CRISPR to destroy only the engineered genes \[should we insert citation to that paper?]. This method could be preferable to traditional kill-switches, since he said that toxin-based killing is difficult to maintain efficiently in gastrointestinal systems.

    He also shared his experience as an iGEM judge, and gave advice on the feasibility of our gut-based approach, and the appropriate experimental scope for iGEM.  He also advised that chassis selection depends critically on where testosterone is reabsorbed (small intestine favors *Lactobacillus*; colon favors *E. coli Nissle*).

    Most importantly, Dr. Chan recommended focusing exclusively on demonstrating the metabolic pathway works *in vitro*, as biocontainment alone could be an entire iGEM project. While the hypoxia gate that we implemented could be considered a biosafety layer, since it limits environmental spread, is not sufficient alone. Thus proper biosafety implementation for our product is something that future iGEM teams can build on.

    Eleni Dubé Zinatelli is a PhD candidate, whose research focuses on the impact of PMOS on mental health, with an emphasis on women’s health and how reproductive hormones influence brain function. We reached out to her to get her point of view on PMOS-related mental health outcomes and her feedback on the project.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    Summary of interview:

    We consulted Dr. Trevor M. Penning, whose research focuses on steroid transforming aldo-keto reductases, including AKR1D1. He has written many papers on AKR1D1, some of which are considered the backbone of our project. Dr. Penning was consulted to receive feedback regarding the use of AKR1D1 as an effector, and to consult on potential issues, including solubility problems and broad substrate specificity.

    Due to other articles having issues with AKR1D1 solubility and most of the protein ending up in inclusion bodies, we discussed a potential solution by adding solubility tags to increase AKR1D1’s solubility. Dr. Penning noted how the formation of inclusion bodies is unavoidable, and the soluble fraction is usually sufficient to do the work, though he emphasized this depends on how much protein is needed. Based on Dr. Benrick’s advice on expressing as much enzyme as possible, we decided to pursue increasing the yield of soluble protein by adding solubility tags.

    While discussing issues with substrate specificity, Dr. Penning explained how any substrate with Δ4-3-ketosteroid structure in the steroid ring is a plausible 5β-reduction substrate, which is a primary reason single-steroid specificity is hard to engineer on Δ4-3-ketosteroid reductases. To achieve single steroid specificity, engineering would require multiple point mutations across the substrate pocket. Dr. Penning cautioned us how that would be an extremely difficult task, considering our limited time for the project.

    Regarding the question of constitutive or inducible expression for the final product, Dr. Penning pointed us towards bacterial promoters inducible under gut-relevant conditions. Based on his recommendation to pursue a gut-context-inducible promoter, along with Dr. Benrick’s input, we decided to move forward with a hypoxia promoter. 

    Integration:

    **Solubility and yield** - addition of solubility tags to enzyme constructs was done to maximize the yield of expressed protein

    **Substrate specificity** - decided to pursue multiple point mutations across the binding pocket

    **Expression system design** - choosing a gut-relevant inducible bacterial promotor

Importantly, she highlighted the potential value of a gut-based approach for women seeking pregnancy, since several current anti-androgen treatments cannot be used during this period.   Dr. Juan Pablo del Río researches how prenatal and adolescent hyperandrogenism affects brain development and neuropsychiatric outcomes, including the "dual-hit hypothesis" linking early androgen exposure to later mental health risk. He was contacted to understand how a gut-localized, androgen-lowering therapeutic like ours might intersect with the neuropsychiatric burdens seen in PMOS. His insight gave us a new perspective on whether local (gut) testosterone reduction could plausibly extend to systemic or brain-level effects. 

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    Summary of interview:

    We primarily contacted Dr.del Río to understand how targeting gut testosterone might cause neurodevelopmental and mental health related issues with PMOS. We also wanted to ask him which part of the dual hit hypothesis is most actionable for early intervention. We also discussed which neurodevelopmental pathways are most sensitive to hyperandrogenism and how and how to discuss PMOS and neurodevelopment without stigmatizing language. 

    He explained that pinpointing the specific androgen responsible for PMOS pathology is difficult, since testosterone is often used as a proxy due to ease of measurement, but hyperandrogenism isn't driven by testosterone alone. Testosterone can convert into estrogen and progesterone, and other androgenic metabolites likely contribute, so any therapeutic targeting testosterone specifically should account for these broader steroid interconversions. 

    On neurodevelopment, he described growing evidence that prenatal androgen exposure affects microglial activity, pushing microglia toward a more inflammatory phenotype that can affect the prefrontal cortex and memory/social recognition circuits which could possibly be a synergistic effect involving hormonal, inflammatory, and metabolic factors together, not testosterone alone. He also referenced fMRI research showing reduced prefrontal cortex–amygdala connectivity in women with PMOS, which may underlie increased anxiety and depression, and suggested we follow up with the researcher behind that study.

    On therapeutic implications, he noted there's currently limited direct evidence linking androgen reduction to improved mental health outcomes, and highlighted a bidirectional relationship between hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance that makes it hard to isolate the effect of androgen reduction specifically. On our project, he raised open questions about whether reducing gut testosterone could produce unintended endocrine effects, how much systemic androgen levels would actually be affected by a gut-localized approach, and whether inducible expression would offer more safety and control than constitutive secretion. He also pointed us toward further contacts :  Crisosto (pregnant women with PMOS and developmental outcomes), Moliqueo (rat models of amygdala inflammation), and Simon Baron-Cohen (prenatal androgen exposure and neurodevelopment). 

    Integration:

    **Communication/Awareness building** - Being careful not to overstate mental health benefits given the limited direct evidence linking androgen reduction to psychiatric outcomes.

    Summary of interview:

    Dr. Lina Zabulienė is an endocrinologist with extensive clinical and research experience in PMOS. We reached out to her for her clinical perspective on our target population, the effects of reducing androgen levels, and the safety considerations of using an engineered probiotic. When discussing who could benefit from our therapy, she highlighted women who experience intolerance to current treatments such as oral contraceptives or metformin, as well as those with contraindications to contraceptives. She also suggested that our approach could be relevant for women planning to conceive, who cannot use certain anti-androgenic treatments. However, she advised against using a live bacterial therapeutic during pregnancy.

    She supported lowering testosterone as a therapeutic approach, explaining that reducing elevated testosterone could improve not only hyperandrogenic symptoms but potentially also folliculogenesis and spontaneous ovulation. However, she emphasized that testosterone should not be reduced excessively, as low androgen levels can affect libido, muscle strength and bone health. From her clinical experience, a reduction of around 30% can already improve symptoms, making controlled rather than maximal androgen reduction important for our therapeutic.

    Regarding our 5β-reduction strategy, she highlighted the importance of enzyme specificity. Since 5β-reductase can act on other steroids, including progesterone and cortisol, she encouraged us to ensure that our enzyme preferentially targets testosterone to minimize unwanted effects on other steroid hormones.

    Finally, she identified systemic hypoandrogenism and bacterial translocation across the intestinal barrier as two important safety concerns. To improve bacterial containment, she supported chromosomal integration rather than plasmids, gut specific expression, and a kill switch to prevent the engineered bacteria from surviving and spreading outside their intended environment.

    Integration:

    **Target population** - Her feedback led us to focus on patients with intolerance or contraindications to current treatments, while considering preconception rather than pregnancy as a potential use case.

    **Controlled reduction** - We shifted our goal from maximizing androgen reduction to achieving a controlled reduction, minimizing the risk of hypoandrogenism.

    **Enzyme specificity** - Her concerns about effects on progesterone and cortisol strengthened our focus on engineering the enzyme toward preferential testosterone conversion.

    **Bacterial containment** - Her safety concerns reinforced our three-layer containment strategy: chromosomal integration, gut specific expression, and a kill switch.

She also mentioned the practical barriers such as lack of PMOS clinics in her region, many of her patients cannot afford a nutritionist, a gynecologist, supplements and pills together.     Summary of interview:

    Simon Ekström and Anton Sellerberg are researchers at SciLifeLab working with protein structural analysis and mass spectrometry. We reached out to them for advice on how we could experimentally characterize our androgen converting enzyme and use computational methods to guide its further improvement.

    When discussing how to demonstrate enzyme activity, they recommended starting with simpler and faster assays, such as commercial testosterone or estradiol assays, rather than immediately relying on mass spectrometry. Mass spectrometry would be more valuable at a later stage when investigating substrate specificity or identifying reaction products, which would also require appropriate reference compounds.

    For improving the enzyme, Anton and Simon recommended starting computational work early and running it in parallel with wet-lab experiments. They suggested using docking and molecular dynamics to evaluate enzyme substrate interactions and guide the selection of promising mutations. Rather than introducing several mutations at once, they recommended focusing on point mutations and comparing multiple computational models before selecting candidates for experimental testing.

    They also emphasized that producing and characterizing the enzyme should come before more complex experimental systems. When we discussed using microfluidics to better mimic physiological hormone concentrations, they advised us to postpone this until we had first demonstrated enzymatic conversion in vitro. For protein purification, they considered a His-tag suitable and unlikely to significantly interfere with the enzyme's catalytic activity.

    Integration:

    **Experimental priority** - Their recommendation to first establish enzyme activity made us prioritize protein expression, purification and straightforward androgen conversion assays before moving to more complex characterization methods.

    **Computational modelling** - Their advice led us to start docking and molecular dynamics alongside our wet lab work, allowing computational results to guide which enzyme variants should be experimentally tested.

    **Mutation strategy** - Their recommendation to focus on point substitutions shaped our protein engineering strategy, where individual mutations could be modelled and compared before selecting promising variants for experimental validation.

    **Project scope** - Their feedback on microfluidics led us to deprioritize complex physiological models and focus our available time and resources on demonstrating enzyme production and androgen conversion first.

## PMOS Surveys

**To assess our approach, we developed two surveys.**&#x20;

The first targets **clinicians**, covering current treatment practices, frustrations with existing PMOS options, and clinical reaction to a hypothetical gut-targeted probiotic treatment, including safety concerns, positioning against existing treatments, and likelihood to prescribe.&#x20;

The second targets **patients** directly, covering symptom severity and quality-of-life impact, treatment experience, gut health, and openness to a non-hormonal, probiotic-based alternative; **however**, it was not published, as we lacked time to secure the ethical review required for processing health-related personal data.&#x20;

Both surveys were designed with **GDPR and data protection** requirements from the start, including explicit consent, anonymous handling, and a full privacy notice.

    We wanted to know, from people who actually **treat** this condition, where current treatment options fall short, and whether a gut-targeted, non-hormonal approach would be seen as clinically useful, not just scientifically interesting.

    **What we covered:**&#x20;

    * **Clinical background:** role, patient volume, years of experience.
    * **Current treatment practices:** what's commonly prescribed, whether treatment targets root cause or symptoms, whether subtypes are distinguished clinically, and biggest frustrations with existing options.
    * Opinion to a **hypothetical** daily oral **probiotic** tablet (a genetically engineered bacterium expressing a 5β-reductase only under gut-like, high-androgen conditions), including initial clinical reaction, positioning against existing treatments, safety concerns, patient openness, likelihood to prescribe if safety and efficacy were proven, and cost sensitivity.

    The survey was distributed by email. We identified clinics and clinicians treating PMOS patients and sent the survey to at least 5 contacts per clinic simultaneously, rather than one at a time. Since the survey is anonymous, this matters for more than just reach: a response can't be attributed to a specific recipient based on timing alone.
