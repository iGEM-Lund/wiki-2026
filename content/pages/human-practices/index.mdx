While talking with different stakeholders during our project, they often asked us ‘What are Human Practices?’ Initially we answered that human practices are interviewing experts on certain topics, and getting their feedback. But as time passed, and many times we had answered this question, we realized that this is not what the true meaning of human practices is. We realized that it is not bombarding academics with questions and awaiting their replies, or asking companies if they would be interested in selling our product, or asking clinicians about how they conduct their clinical practice. It is about listening, about carefully reading between the lines, about looking at the bigger picture. We learned that every person has their unique perspective and story to tell, and it is on us to share it. 

Every talk we had was different, with different topics discussed, different opinions, and different questions arising. Each talk was individually assessed, to see how we can implement it into our project, how we can give it a voice, and how we can contribute to bridging the gaps mentioned. Even though every talk was different, most of them agreed on one thing: there is not enough research on PMOS.

\*picture of Eleni :  Eleni Dubé Zinatelli: ‘I love when you do research on PMOS because honestly, there really isn't enough.’

After many talks, we became increasingly aware of how big the gap can be. It shocked us. Diagnostic delay, lack of proper medication and lack of awareness, just to name a few. They can all be addressed by doing more research on PMOS, because currently, there is no known cause for this syndrome, nor a proper way to treat it. We wanted to bridge that gap, at least a little, because 13% of women suffer from this condition. That is why we dedicated our project to PMOS, to raise awareness about it. There can never be enough awareness.

  **Summary of interview:**

  **We consulted Dr. Anna Benrick, whose research focuses on the molecular link between androgen excess and the development of reproductive disorders, obesity, and type-2 diabetes in women. The interview was centered on the feasibility of the AnTigone project, whether locally reducing testosterone in the gut could meaningfully affect systemic testosterone levels and reduce hyperandrogenism symptoms. Despite promising preclinical findings, Dr. Benrick emphasized that the gut microbiota-PMOS field is still young, and it remains unclear whether microbiota changes drive PMOS or result from it.**

  **She pointed out a recent paper on artemisin, an antimalarial compound shown to lower testosterone production by inhibiting a testosterone synthesis enzyme, suggesting an alternative approach to reducing systemic testosterone levels. A recurring topic in the interview was the uncertainty about gut-to-serum steroid circulation: how much testosterone actually passes through the gut relative to what is produced by ovaries and adrenal glands. This uncertainty was raised to answer one of our main questions, whether our approach could achieve a biologically relevant effect. We had discussed the possibility of incorporating a testosterone-sensing mechanism to induce enzyme expression and deactivate it if reduction became too high. She advised not to pursue this, but to prioritize stable, strong enzyme expression, since insufficient effect was deemed more likely to happen than excessive androgen lowering. Furthermore, she did not anticipate a compensatory increase in androgen production in response to our treatment.**

  **The conversation also touched on validation strategy, including in vitro options such as testosterone-producing theca cells, and candidate biomarkers such as testosterone, androstenedione and hydroxyandrogens, alongside clinical symptoms such as menstruation frequency or body hair. Dr. Benrick raised the possibility that a gut-localized, low-risk approach could be relevant for PMOS treatment during pregnancy, a period in which most hormonal therapies are avoided due to their possibility of affecting fetal development.**

  **Integration:**

  **Expression system design - adopting a hypoxia-responsive promoter as a fixed gut on/off switch**

  **Project framing - framing the project as an early-stage hypothesis rather than a validated therapeutic mechanism**
