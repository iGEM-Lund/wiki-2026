title: Human Practices
---
While talking with different stakeholders during our project, they often asked us: "**What are Human Practices**?" Initially, we answered that human practices are interviewing experts on certain topics, and getting their feedback. But as time passed, and many times we had answered this question, we realized that this is not what the true meaning of human practices is. We realized that it is not bombarding academics with questions and awaiting their replies, or asking companies if they would be interested in selling our product, or asking clinicians about how they conduct their clinical practice. It is about listening, about carefully reading between the lines, about looking at the bigger picture. We learned that every person has their unique perspective and story to tell, and it is on us to share it. 

**Every talk we had was different**, with different topics discussed, different opinions, and different questions arising. Each talk was individually assessed, to see how we can implement it into our project, how we can give it a voice, and how we can contribute to bridging the gaps mentioned. Even though every talk was different, most of them agreed on one thing: **there is not enough research on PMOS**.

<Quote quote="I love when you do research on PMOS because honestly, there really isn't enough." quotee="Eleni Dubé Zinatelli" figure="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/human-practices/eleni-dub-zinatelli.avif" figureAlt="Portrait of Eleni Dubé Zinatelli" />

After many talks, we became increasingly aware of how big the gap can be. It shocked us. **Diagnostic delay, lack of proper medication and lack of awareness**, just to name a few. They can all be addressed by doing more research on PMOS, because currently, **there is no known cause for this syndrome, nor a proper way to treat it**. We wanted to bridge that gap, at least a little, because 10–13% of women suffer from this condition (<Citation reference="world-health-organization-2026" narrative={false} />). That is why we dedicated our project to PMOS, to [**raise awareness**](/education#pmos-awareness) about it. There can never be enough awareness.

Our conversations fell into **three groups**, which we treat as the pillars of our human practices work:

## Stakeholders

For a project to move beyond a <Tooltip text="A first test showing that an idea can work, before it is developed into something usable.">proof-of-concept</Tooltip>, it has to consider, and be considered by, people outside of the laboratory. A therapeutic can reach a patient only if it can be **developed, manufactured, classified, prescribed, afforded and trusted.** Each of those steps belongs to a different group of people with its own priorities and reasons to say no to it. Throughout the project, we have tried to reach most of these groups, and treat them as participants in the design by implementing their feedback, rather than treating them as obstacles.

By mapping the path our product would have to travel from a construct in the lab to a therapeutic in the clinic, we have identified **five groups of stakeholders**. They do not form a list, but rather an interdependent cycle: academics develop, industry produces, regulators establish safety, clinicians prescribe, and patients give their feedback, redirecting the research and development. Each group affects the one after it, and the last one closes the cycle back to the first.

  Anna Benrick's research focuses on hyperandrogenism and its link to reproductive diseases in females. She was contacted to help us with understanding more about PMOS, hyperandrogenism and the feasibility of our approach. Dr. Benrick confirmed **5β-reductase-targeted androgen reduction is a valid therapeutic approach**, and advised us **against an androgen-inducible expression strategy, favoring <Tooltip text="Making the cells produce a lot of the enzyme all the time, rather than only when a signal switches it on.">stable overexpression</Tooltip>** instead.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    **Summary of interview:**

    We consulted Dr. Anna Benrick, whose research focuses on the molecular link between androgen excess and the development of reproductive disorders, obesity, and <Tooltip text="A long-term condition in which the body cannot use insulin properly, so blood sugar stays too high.">type 2 diabetes</Tooltip> in women. The interview was centered on the feasibility of the AnTigone project, whether locally reducing testosterone in the gut could meaningfully affect systemic testosterone levels and reduce hyperandrogenism symptoms. Despite promising <Tooltip text="Results from laboratory and animal studies, before any trial in people.">preclinical findings</Tooltip>, Dr. Benrick emphasized that the <Tooltip text="The community of microbes living in the gut.">**gut microbiota**</Tooltip>**-PMOS field is still young**, and it remains unclear whether microbiota changes drive PMOS or result from it.

    A recurring topic in the interview was the uncertainty about gut-to-serum steroid circulation: how much testosterone actually passes through the gut relative to what is produced by ovaries and adrenal glands. This uncertainty was raised to answer one of our main questions, whether our approach could achieve a biologically relevant effect. We had discussed the possibility of incorporating a testosterone-sensing mechanism to induce enzyme expression and deactivate it if reduction became too high. She advised not to pursue this, but to prioritize stable, strong enzyme expression, since insufficient effect was deemed more likely to happen than excessive androgen lowering. Furthermore, she did not anticipate a compensatory increase in androgen production in response to our treatment.

    The conversation also touched on validation strategy, including in vitro options such as testosterone-producing <Tooltip text="Cells in the ovary that make androgens.">theca cells</Tooltip>, and candidate <Tooltip text="A measurable substance that signals what is going on in the body.">biomarkers</Tooltip> such as testosterone, <Tooltip text="A hormone made in the ovaries and adrenal glands that the body can convert into testosterone.">androstenedione</Tooltip> and hydroxyandrogens, alongside clinical symptoms such as menstruation frequency or body hair. Dr. Benrick raised the possibility that a gut-localized, low-risk approach could be relevant for PMOS treatment during pregnancy, a period in which most hormonal therapies are avoided due to their possibility of affecting fetal development.

    **Integration:**

    * **Expression system design** - adopting a [hypoxia-responsive promoter](/results#hypoxia-responsive-promoter) as a fixed gut on/off switch
    * **Project framing** - framing the project as an early-stage hypothesis rather than a validated therapeutic mechanism

    **Summary of interview:**

    Given her research background in teacher professional development, Helena Berglund was contacted to get early feedback on our high school outreach concept and to identify the approaches with the highest impact.

    The main topics discussed were: ideal target age, format, the structure of the Swedish school system, the relevance of school segregation, and language requirements. Helena confirmed 15/16 to 18 (pre-specialization and post-specialization = from 9th year onward) as the ideal target age. 

    She recommended hands-on laboratory work and workshops over lectures alone, as these have a high learning and retention effect on students. She also noted that teachers are generally looking for activities that are easy to fit into an already busy curriculum. She explained the segregation between municipal schools and non-municipal, often profit-driven schools (<Tooltip text="Swedish independent schools, often run for profit.">friskolor</Tooltip>) as a reason for unequal access to practical activities, pointing us to <Tooltip text="The Swedish Schools Inspectorate.">Skolinspektionen</Tooltip> for insight into school quality data and suggesting we could prioritize socioeconomically weaker areas as high-impact targets. She confirmed English-language material is fine given students' strong proficiency. She also highlighted linking science to its societal relevance as the most engaging angle for student interest.

    **Integration:**

    * **Format** - She strengthened our decision to develop a lecture + workshop package rather than a lecture alone. We also kept this in mind across almost all educational talks/lectures, integrating at least some interactive component into each one. As one example, following her advice to prioritize practical activities, we replaced a planned lecture with a lab exercise for the <Ref target="card-sw63l" linkText="Rays for Excellence workshop" />, even though a lecture had originally been suggested by them.
    * **Material accessibility** - Based on her point about school segregation and unequal access to resources, we kept material low-cost and accessible. For example, using pasta rings to illustrate plasmid parts (promoter, enhancer, etc.) in the Student workshop at <Ref target="card-gi1sc" linkText="Ung Vetenskapssport" />.
    * **Language** - Her reassurance that English is widely understood by Swedish students let us proceed with the high school package in English, which was necessary at the time since we had no Swedish-speaking team member.
    * **Content framing** - Her advice to link science to its societal relevance reinforced the approach we started with, using iGEM to show the range of experts involved in synthetic biology (ethics, policymakers, researchers, etc.).

  Dr. Clement Chan is an expert in genetic circuits which can be used for biocontainment. He was contacted to get insight into the possible biocontainment strategies for our product. Based on his feedback, we decided to **focus our experimental work on *in vitro* proof-of-concept only**, but discuss the [biocontainment](/biosafety#environmental-biocontainment) theory thoroughly, postponing its practical implementation to future work. We also had to consider our choice of future probiotic <Tooltip text="The host organism that carries the engineered genes.">chassis</Tooltip> based on where testosterone is actually reabsorbed in the gut.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    **Summary of interview:**

    We contacted Dr. Chan because of his expertise in genetic circuits and biocontainment systems. We wanted his perspective on what biosafety measures we should implement.

    Dr. Chan outlined two main approaches for biocontainment strategies: <Tooltip text="Making an organism depend on an artificial amino acid that does not exist in nature.">non-canonical amino acid incorporation</Tooltip> and <Tooltip text="A genetic circuit that kills the bacterium under defined conditions.">[kill-switches](/biosafety#kill-switch)</Tooltip>, and pros and cons of each. Dr. Chan informed us that **modern biocontainment is multi-layered**, and uses 3-4 different mechanisms simultaneously, since no single mechanism is sufficient. We also discussed his novel kill-switch design, a <Tooltip text="A gene-editing system that cuts DNA at a sequence chosen with a guide RNA.">CRISPR</Tooltip>-based genetic circuit, that instead of killing bacteria, uses CRISPR to destroy only the engineered genes. This method could be preferable to traditional kill-switches, since he said that toxin-based killing is difficult to maintain efficiently in gastrointestinal systems.

    He also shared his experience as an iGEM judge, and gave advice on the feasibility of our gut-based approach, and the appropriate experimental scope for iGEM. He also advised that chassis selection depends critically on where testosterone is reabsorbed (small intestine favors *Lactobacillus* sp., while colon favors *E. coli* Nissle 1917).

    Most importantly, Dr. Chan recommended **focusing exclusively on demonstrating the metabolic pathway works *in vitro***, as biocontainment alone could be an entire iGEM project. While the hypoxia gate that we implemented could be considered a biosafety layer, since it limits environmental spread, it is not sufficient alone. Thus proper biosafety implementation for our product is something that future iGEM teams can build on.

    **Integration:**

    * **Chassis validation** - we had to determine primary testosterone reabsorption site (small intestine vs. colon) before finalizing end chassis choice (*E. coli* Nissle 1917 or *Lactobacillus* sp.).
    * **Biocontainment** - we removed biocontainment from planned experimental work and repositioned it as "future implementation" discussion only.

    **Summary of interview:**

    Eleni Dubé Zinatelli is a PhD candidate, whose research focuses on the impact of PMOS on mental health, with an emphasis on women's health and how reproductive hormones influence brain function. We reached out to her to get her point of view on PMOS-related mental health outcomes and her feedback on the project.

    Eleni agreed that reducing testosterone in a context of a therapeutic like ours could meaningfully help the physical symptoms of hyperandrogenism such as hirsutism, acne and <Tooltip text="Hair loss or thinning.">alopecia</Tooltip>. She stressed how the body image tied to these symptoms is one of the central burdens in women with PMOS. She noted how this disorder is multifaceted and cannot be addressed with a single intervention. However, improving even one of the previously mentioned symptoms could meaningfully help patients, not only in mental health but also body image and identity.

    She was positive about the project's framing. Since PMOS is increasingly understood as a polyendocrine, metabolic condition rather than a purely hormonal one, she saw value in an approach that isn't a hormonal treatment or an insulin sensitizing approach, **describing our project as a ‘different lens' the field needs**. She emphasized the gut-microbiome angle as underexplored in PMOS research, mentioning it should be investigated further.

    Eleni raised a concern regarding PMOS <Tooltip text="One of the four PMOS phenotypes: irregular ovulation and polycystic ovaries, but no excess androgens.">phenotype D</Tooltip> (the non-hyperandrogenic phenotype). Patients with this phenotype are **often dismissed by doctors** because they lack the more visible hyperandrogenic symptoms. This leaves many women undiagnosed and unsupported for years while they continue searching for answers. She identified this as a systemic diagnostic gap, not something our therapeutic addresses, but a gap in the current system that deserves more attention, helping women with less visible hyperandrogenic symptoms get recognized and diagnosed sooner.

    She noted multiple times that PMOS remains significantly under-researched, and welcomed the increasing attention to it from student research teams.

    **Integration:**

    * **Raising awareness** - Eleni's comments on phenotype D shaped our decision to include it in the [PMOS Awareness Month](/education#pmos-awareness-month) activities, where we aimed to raise awareness of that diagnostic gap.
    * **Gut-localized approach** - By pointing to <Tooltip text="Transferring stool from a healthy donor into a patient's gut to change their gut microbes.">fecal-microbiota transplant</Tooltip> studies, Eleni strengthened the rationale in our project, for why a gut-localized therapeutic is a reasonable approach to pursue.

  Trevor M. Penning is an expert in steroid-transforming aldo-keto reductases, including AKR1D1. He was consulted to assess the use of AKR1D1 in our approach, and to discuss potential limitations. He **confirmed the enzyme converts testosterone to <Tooltip text="5β-dihydrotestosterone, the less active form of testosterone after 5β-reduction.">5β-DHT</Tooltip> quite well**, affirming our <Tooltip text="The part of the system that carries out the intended action, here the enzyme that transforms the steroid.">effector</Tooltip> choice. He noted that **AKR1D1’s broad substrate affinity** could present an issue and that <Tooltip text="Changes to single amino acids in a protein, made by altering one letter of its gene.">multiple point mutations</Tooltip> across the binding pocket would be needed to overcome this challenge, which could pose difficulties within our limited timeline.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    **Summary of interview:**

    We consulted Dr. Trevor M. Penning, whose research focuses on steroid-transforming aldo-keto reductases, including AKR1D1. He has written many papers on AKR1D1, some of which are the backbone of our project, including the purification protocol our experimental work is based on (<Citation reference="penning-2023" narrative={false} />). Dr. Penning was consulted to receive feedback regarding the use of AKR1D1 as an effector, and to consult on potential issues, including solubility problems and broad substrate specificity.

    Due to other articles having issues with AKR1D1 solubility and most of the protein ending up in <Tooltip text="Clumps of misfolded protein that form inside bacteria and are hard to use.">inclusion bodies</Tooltip>, we discussed a potential solution by adding solubility tags to increase AKR1D1’s solubility. Dr. Penning noted how the formation of inclusion bodies is unavoidable, and the soluble fraction is usually sufficient to do the work, though he emphasized this depends on how much protein is needed. Based on <Ref target="card-8mtim" linkText="Dr. Benrick" />’s advice on expressing as much enzyme as possible, we decided to pursue increasing the yield of soluble protein by adding [solubility tags](/engineering#akr1d1-fusion-tag-and-induction-strength).

    While discussing issues with substrate specificity, Dr. Penning explained how any substrate with Δ4-3-ketosteroid structure in the steroid ring is a plausible 5β-reduction substrate, which is a primary reason single-steroid specificity is hard to engineer on Δ4-3-ketosteroid reductases. To achieve single steroid specificity, engineering would require multiple point mutations across the substrate pocket. Dr. Penning cautioned us how that would be an extremely difficult task, considering our limited time for the project.

    Regarding the question of <Tooltip text="Constitutive means always switched on; inducible means switched on only when a specific signal is present.">constitutive or inducible expression</Tooltip> for the final product, Dr. Penning pointed us towards bacterial promoters inducible under gut-relevant conditions. Based on his recommendation to pursue a gut-context-inducible promoter, along with <Ref target="card-8mtim" linkText="Dr. Benrick" />’s input, we decided to move forward with a [hypoxia promoter](/results#hypoxia-responsive-promoter). 

    **Integration:**

    * **Solubility and yield** - addition of solubility tags to enzyme constructs was done to maximize the yield of expressed protein.
    * **Substrate specificity** - decided to pursue multiple point mutations across the binding pocket ([enzyme specificity engineering](/engineering#rational-engineering-of-enzyme-specificity)).
    * **Expression system design** - choosing a gut-relevant inducible bacterial promoter.
  </Expandable>

A start-up is realistic even with a small core group, as long as at least one or two members commit long term. Whether we continue beyond iGEM will be decided once the competition project is finished.

    **Integration:**

    * **Market analysis** - The incubator entry report gave us our first structured view of market, competitors, customers, and regulation.&#x20;
    * **Market validation** - Their requirement for market evidence confirmed our focus on stakeholder input, which we pursued through expert interviews, the <Ref target="card-tr7bj" linkText="clinician survey" />, and the [economic assessment](/economical-assessment).
    * **Entrepreneurship training** - Team members were introduced to the program **INSIGHTS** to learn the basics of turning an idea into a venture.
    * **Future route** - The incubator place and free legal advice remain available if we decide to continue after iGEM.

## PMOS Surveys

**To assess our approach, we developed two surveys and refined them with expert feedback on their legal and clinical aspects.**

The **clinician survey** asked 36 clinicians about current PMOS treatment, their frustrations, and their view of a hypothetical gut-targeted probiotic; 13 responded. The **patient survey** covered symptoms, treatment experience and openness to a non-hormonal alternative. We did not publish it, because we lacked time to secure the ethical review required for processing health-related personal data.

  We asked **36 clinicians** about current PMOS treatment, their frustrations, and their view of a hypothetical gut-targeted probiotic. **13 responded** (36%). Most saw the idea as interesting but wanted more data. Their main concerns were long-term safety, colonization and missing trial data.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    **Summary:**

    We wanted to know from people who treat PMOS where current options fall short, and whether a non-hormonal, gut-targeted approach would be clinically useful and not only scientifically interesting.

    The survey covered:

    * Role, patient volume and years of experience.
    * Current treatments, treatment goals, subtypes and frustrations.
    * Reaction to a hypothetical daily oral tablet with a genetically engineered bacterium that expresses a 5β-reductase only under gut-like, high-androgen conditions. We asked about initial reaction, place among current treatments, concerns, patient openness, likelihood to prescribe and cost.

    We sent the survey by email to clinicians who treat or have treated PMOS. It was sent to at least 5 clinicians at the same time, so a response cannot be traced back to a recipient by its timing. It was available in English, Italian and Spanish (English version below). 13 clinicians completed it. Our note on data policy is at the end.

    In the open questions, respondents asked for clinical evidence: “results from large multicenter <Tooltip text="Randomized controlled trials: studies where patients are randomly assigned to treatment or comparison groups.">RCTs</Tooltip>” and “evidence of lowering testosterone levels, both biochemical and clinical.”

    **Limitations and outlook:**

    Only 13 of 36 clinicians responded, 11 of them gynecologists, so the results **do not cover other specialties**, and the 23 who did not respond may think differently. Respondents judged a product that we described ourselves, so their answers reflect our description and not a real product. With so few answers we report counts only and run no statistical tests.

    With more time, we would send the survey earlier and to more clinicians, including more endocrinologists and general practitioners, so that we could compare specialties and test differences statistically.

    **Note on data policy:**

    We collect no patient data. Consent is built into the survey, following our interview with (<Ref target="card-6g7ql" linkText="Ana Nordberg" />), and input from the university's Research Ethics Advisor and Personal Data Protection Coordinator. Responses are anonymous. Quoting an open answer needed a separate optional consent, and we quote only respondents who gave it. The privacy notice can be found here: [Privacy notice (English)](https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/human-practices/surveys/clinicians-gdpr.pdf).

    One clinician suggested adding a country-of-practice question. We removed ours on advice, because country, job and patient volume together could identify a clinic.

    **Summary:**

    Clinicians can tell us whether our approach is clinically plausible. Only patients can tell us whether it matters to them day to day.

    The survey would have covered:

    * Diagnosis journey, including PMOS phenotype and time to diagnosis.
    * Symptom severity and its effect on self-esteem, social life, work or study and mental health.
    * Treatments tried, satisfaction and side effects.
