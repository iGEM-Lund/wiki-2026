## Our Engineering Approach&#x20;

Engineering a biological system requires careful experimental evaluation of each individual design. Each design&#x20;

Throughout the development of our project we applied the Design-Test-Build-Learn (DTBL) cycle framework to progressively investigate technical challenges in the different layers of our proposed design.

## Engineering overview

Each cycle generated observation that informed subsequent experiments, alternative design or changes in results interpretation. As if we look at the bigger picture of our project the whole project can be seen as a big DBTL cycle, but inside of the experimental work we can divide and extract DBTL cycles themselves that, once completed helped going one step further the final envisioned design. The main DBTL cycle would describe the evolution of the whole project, while the smaller cycles would show the iterative optimization happening inside each individual phase.

Our main challenges and small DBTL cycles refers to:

1. **Construct Assembly**: establishing a reliable cloning and verification strategy.
2. **Enzyme production** and **protein recovery**: improving recombinant enzyme expression, solubility, and recovery.
3. **Activity characterisation**: developing reliable assays for evaluating testosterone-associated enzymatic activity.

Improvements in cloning allowed the production of alternative enzyme constructs, while expression and activity experiments revealed additional requirements for future optimizations.

## Improving the cloning workflow

<Card>
  <CardTitle>
    Challenge
  </CardTitle>

  The first engineering challenge was assembling correct expression constructs for the candidate enzymes, AKR1D1 and CI2350. Our initial restriction-cloning workflow using pET-11a repeatedly produced low DNA recovery and unsuccessful transformation. In addition, incomplete restriction digestion could not be reliably identified as NdeI and BamHI restriction sites differs only by approximately 40 base pairs (bp).
</Card>

### **D*esign***

Our initial restriction-ligation strategy used NdeI and BamHI to insert the genes into pET-11a. Following inconsistent results and unsuccessful attempts, we investigated whether plasmid purity, restriction efficiency, and vector preparation contributed to the cloning difficulties.

We subsequently redesigned the workflow using pET-BLANK, a pET-derived construct containing CI2350–MBP. Digestion was expected to release an approximately 3 kb insert, leaving a vector backbone of approximately 5.4 kb. Unlike the original strategy, this would allow the backbone to be separated from the released insert by agarose gel electrophoresis.

### B*uild*

We first extracted pET-11a using the Jena Bioscience miniprep kit and prepared the vector through sequential NdeI and BamHI digestion. Following **poor recovery**, we repeated plasmid preparation using the E.Z.N.A. kit and investigated alternative digestion conditions.

The **revised strategy** involved digesting pET-BLANK, gel-extracting the approximately 5.4 kb backbone, and ligating it with the selected AKR1D1 and CI2350 inserts. Following **unsuccessful initial transformations**, we also repeated ligation using an extended overnight incubation at 16°C.

### &#x54;***est***

We **improved first gel extraction yield and purity using different kits** and we evaluated the resulting constructs through careful choice of controls and screening of samples to include in colony PCR, restriction digestion, sequencing. However, colony PCR still revealed unsuccessful cloning attempts. Further investigation **suggested incomplete NdeI digestion**. Because the two restriction sites in pET-11a were separated by only approximately 40 bp, conventional agarose gel electrophoresis could not reliably distinguish singly digested from fully digested vector.

In contrast, **pET-BLANK digestion produced distinguishable backbone and insert fragments**. Following the revised ligation procedure, colony-PCR-positive AKR1D1–SUMO and AKR1D1–TrxA candidates were obtained.

> Check our results here.

### L*earn*

Our results highlighted **two limitations in the initial workflow**: potentially inaccurate plasmid DNA quantification and insufficient verification of complete vector digestion.

The **pET-BLANK strategy** enabled clearer separation of the intended backbone from the released insert, improving the reliability of backbone selection for subsequent cloning.

After having identified our problem and chosen the strategy to continue **we managed to successfully cloned our construct and transform our colony**, aligned non-mutated sequences were carried forward for protein expression.&#x20;

## Developing a Reliable Enzyme Activity Assay

<Card>
  <CardTitle>
    Challenge
  </CardTitle>

  After producing our candidates enzymes, we needed to determine whether they could support the intended testosterone-transforming reaction. Our initial assay measured the consumption of the cofactor NAD(P)H spectrophotometrically. The development of this assay was complicated by substrate precipitation, inconsistent absorbance measurements, inadequate signal detection, and background cofactor consumption. These limitations required several rounds of troubleshooting before the measurements could be interpreted reliably.
</Card>

### Design

Building from existing literature (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="penning-2023" narrative={false} />) we developed a continuous spectrophotometric assay based on monitoring the decrease in NAD(P)H absorbance at 340 nm.

We designed assays with different enzyme concentrations to investigate whether the absorbance signal depended on the amount of enzyme-containing preparation. The following iterations focused on improving substrate preparation, selecting appropriate measurement conditions, and introducing controls to distinguish enzyme-associated activity from background reactions.

### Build&#x20;

We investigated activity for the following constructs:

* CI2350–MBP&#x20;
* CI2350–MBP R215A/V263I double mutant
* AKR1D1–SUMO

#### **Iteration 1** - Optimising Reaction Preparation and Microplate Measurement

We initially performed assays in 96-well plates using potassium phosphate buffer at pH 6.5, testosterone, and a pyridine nucleotide cofactor.

During preparation, **testosterone precipitation was observed**. We therefore modified the testosterone master mix and used **warming and mixing to improve its dissolution** before measurement.

The assay was conducted at 350 nm, rather than 340 nm, to reduce interference attributed to the plate material. A measurement at 600 nm was also included as a baseline check.&#x20;

#### **Iteration 2** - Revising Substrate Precipitation and Measurement Format

The initial measurements **did not produce consistent absorbance trends**. We subsequently identified that NAD⁺ had mistakenly been used instead of the intended reduced cofactor in one experiment. Following this finding, we revised the reaction preparations and performed additional assays using NADPH.

We also compared enzyme concentrations and investigated cuvette-based measurements, including tests with AKR1D1 preparations, to **overcome limitations associated with the microplate format**. Inconsistent results were attributed to microplate plastics as initial waves can acquisitions, to check correct cofactor absorbance in spectrophotometer cuvettes showed correct signal.

#### **Iteration 3** - Investigating Signal Intensity

Further assays using CI2350–MBP and AKR1D1–SUMO showed inadequate absorbance signals at the lower NADPH concentrations tested.

We therefore increased the amount of NADPH added to the reaction and performed additional wavelength scans and kinetic measurements using the NanoSpectre.

* Negative controls without enzyme were included to establish the absorbance signal associated with the reaction components alone and not to NADPH natural oxidation.

#### **Iteration 4** - Investigating Background Activity

To assess if cofactor consumption was specific to the recombinant enzymes, we introduced:

* empty-vector preparations produced and purified using the same general workflow
* negative controls reaction without the substrate, to establish if the change in absorbance was due to other enzymes.

We also measured protein concentrations in recombinant enzyme and empty-vector preparations and repeated activity measurements using CI2350–MBP and its R215A/V263I double mutant.

### Test

Initial enzyme-containing samples showed decreases in NAD(P)H absorbance. However, similar decreases were also observed in empty-vector preparations, preventing enzyme-specific interpretation of the signal.

Bradford analysis subsequently revealed substantial protein concentrations in the empty-vector controls, including 0.926 mg/mL in the induced control, compared with 0.695 mg/mL in the CI2350–MBP double-mutant preparation.

These findings suggest non-specific protein recovery or possible carryover during purification, although the cause was not established. When the assays were repeated using CI2350–MBP and its double mutant, no clear NADPH consumption signal was observed. Prolonged storage of the enzyme preparations at 4°C was considered a possible explanation.

> Check our results here.

### Learn

The experiments demonstrated that **NAD(P)H consumption alone could not establish enzyme-specific testosterone conversion** as background cofactor consumption, uncertain sample composition, and variability between experiments limited the interpretation of our results. The available measurements could not confirm the intended testosterone transformation or any reduction in androgenic activity. Further testing should **include Mass Spectrometry and/or Androgen Receptor Assay to confirm product formation and eventual changes in androgenic activity**.
