PMOS is widely under-diagnosed and many people, including those at risk, lack awareness of this syndrome. The **gap** in public knowledge became one of the **driving forces** behind our education work.\
From there, our goals expanded: to offer insight into the world of **synthetic biology**, both to inspire young students and to help bridge the gap between the scientific community and the general public. **Sustainability** was a constant thread running through the project: These three pillars held the foundation of our education aims.

## **PMOS Awareness**

PMOS affects roughly **1 in 10 women**, yet remains widely underdiagnosed, in part because both **public and clinical awareness** of it (and, until recently, even its correct name) lags behind its prevalence. Events under this pillar aim to **close that gap** directly, through **low-barrier activities** like yoga or running, **more focused events** like an awareness workshop, and correct use of the new PMOS terminology across everything we produce. Some events go deeper into the science, others simply get the key facts in front of more people; both raise awareness.

### Using PMOS Instead of PCOS

<Card>
  **Since:** 12 May 2026, ongoing throughout the project (introduced via an Instagram post) 

  **Audience:** Everyone who encounters our work, wiki visitors, event attendees, survey respondents, social media followers

  We started using PMOS following the **Endocrine Society's press release on 12 May 2026** (link/reference), and began using it consistently across our own wiki, slides, socials, and printed materials. The rename reflects a real clinical and scientific shift: "polycystic" is a misleading, stigmatizing term for a condition that's about more than ovarian cysts. Using PMOS from the start let us model the change we're advocating for, rather than waiting for it to become common knowledge first.

  In May 2026, The Lancet published the results of a **14-year** global consensus process, led by **Professor Helena Teede** of **Monash University** with input from 56 professional societies and thousands of patients across six continents, renaming PCOS to **Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)**. The old name is inaccurate: "polycystic" implies the condition is defined by abnormal ovarian cysts, but research shows there's no actual increase in pathological cysts in PCOS. What's really going on is a broader endocrine and metabolic condition affecting insulin regulation, cardiovascular health, weight, and mental health, which the old name obscured entirely. Full clinical implementation is planned as a three-year rollout, with the new name expected in the 2028 International Guideline update, so at this stage, most people, including many clinicians, still don't recognize the term. As a project built around this exact condition, we felt we should be part of **driving** that **change** rather than waiting for it to arrive (LINK/Reference).

  We use "PMOS" throughout our wiki, presentation slides, and social media. For every new event, at the start we explained the terminology, "PMOS (formerly/also known as PCOS)", since the new name isn't yet widely recognized.

## Synthetic Biology Literacy

Where PMOS Awareness is about reach, this pillar is about depth: teaching real, transferable skills rather than just talking about synthetic biology. From dry lab bioinformatics  to plasmid design and molecular docking, we scaled the content to the audience, hands-on and technical for students, simplified but still concrete and engaging for younger (coloring book) or general audiences (Podcast), but never just a passive introduction.

### The Tiny Helpers Inside You: Coloring Book & Library Visit

  An introduction to Bioinformatic workshop for two high school classes. We ran an internal mock-up of the presentation on 29 September to test the format before delivering it live.

  The bioinformatic workshop introduced students in the natural science program at Lars Erik Larsson Gymnasiet to iGEM, synthetic biology, and bioinformatics through a combination of both a lecture and a workshop. The students analyzed and compared protein sequences using BLAST, PDB, and UniProt, connecting their findings to the AnTigone project. Overall, the feedback was positive, with students enjoying the workshop and visualization of proteins. 

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    The lecture aimed to give students a practical understanding of how biotechnology and bioinformatics are applied in real research, going beyond the theory typically encountered in textbooks. It supported the AnTigone pillar of  Synthetic Biology Literacy by engaging the students with real-world examples and research tools.

    The session began with an introduction to iGEM and synthetic biology, followed by an introduction to bioinformatics. Students then worked with two mysterious protein sequences, analyzing them using BLAST and identifying them through PDB or UniProt before comparing the proteins. The sequences were also compared using Clustal Omega to find the conserved regions. The results were discussed and connected to the AnTigone project, including the identification of one sequence as the androgen receptor. The students were also briefly introduced to ChimeraX and the PMOS project before completing a feedback form.

    The feedback from the 19 students was mostly positive. Students particularly appreciated seeing how biotechnology is applied in real life and enjoyed visualizing and comparing proteins. The introduction to ChimeraX also sparked interest. One quote from a student is:

    “Complicated things made simple, Also feminism.”

    The group demonstrated strong biological knowledge, suggesting that the material could have been more advanced. For future lectures, we should ask teachers beforehand about the students' expected knowledge level, as this can vary between groups. The presentation could also be made more engaging through additional interactive parts such as more Mentimeter questions and more detailed workshop instructions. 

    By giving students hands-on experience in bioinformatics and connecting the workshop to the AnTigone project, the lecture demonstrated how bioinformatic tools and databases can support real-world synthetic biology research. &#x20;

## Sustainability

This pillar is about **practicing what we preach**, in that order. We **built our own understanding first**, through the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate certification and internal workshop in June, before teaching it outward in the August lecture. Everything shared externally had already been tested internally, which is also why the two events below are directly cross-linked.&#x20;

    Education wasn't only an external goal for us. It was an internal one too. Lab energy consumption runs roughly **five times** higher than office space of the same size, and single-use plastic is estimated at around 2% of global plastic waste <Citation reference="my-green-lab-n-d" />; those numbers alone made us want to learn more about lab sustainability and what could realistically be done about it.

    We found My Green Lab, an NGO that certifies labs through a graded certification program; since the full certification takes a long time, we opted instead for the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate. The team members **working in the laboratory** (9 out of 14 people), watched the four videos together, took the quiz, and discussed what was actually feasible given how little time we spend in the lab relative to a permanent lab group.

    <Layout2>
      <LayoutColumn>
        The whole wet lab committee took part and discussed the material together. This led to a follow-up meeting with Johan Teljer to learn more about sustainability practices at Kemicentrum. Many easy fixes, such as lowering water pressure and using non-toxic chemicals, are already implemented there. The session also raised day-to-day awareness among people spending time in the lab.

        ---

        Everyone thought about lab sustainability at least once as a result of this session. It likely would have had a bigger impact with consistent, recurring sustainability check-ins across our time in the lab, rather than a single session.

    We wanted a way to raise sustainability awareness beyond our own wet lab committee (see internal workshop LINK), and ideally make it a recurring, mandatory session for incoming first-year students, so sustainable lab habits get built early rather than picked up ad hoc later. We weren't able to get it made mandatory within this project's timeframe, so we ran it as a free, optional lecture instead. The content was checked in advance by Christina Wanneberg. 

    17 students attended, despite the lecture being optional rather than mandatory. Feedback suggested students were, if anything, more interested in AnTigone itself than in the sustainability content specifically: one response to "Are there any topics, activities, or event formats you would like us to cover in the future?" simply read, *"More detail in personal project."*

We acted on both: we reused the Mentimeter-based format for the Future by Lund PMOS workshop that followed a few weeks later, and made sure the project itself was included more to that workshop than it had been here. The lecture's comparatively lower engagement is itself part of what we learned this semester about matching format to what an audience actually wants.

    This lecture is the direct outward-facing follow-on from the internal Sustainability in the Lab workshop: that session built our own understanding, and this one was our first attempt at sharing it with the wider student body. Its feedback fed forward directly into the design of the Future by Lund PMOS Awareness Workshop (LINK).
