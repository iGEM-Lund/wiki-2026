### **Our Approach to Education** 

<Tooltip text="Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome
">PMOS</Tooltip> is widely underdiagnosed, and many people, including those at risk, lack awareness of the syndrome <Citation reference="world-health-organization-2025-1" />. From there, our goals expanded: to offer insight into the world of synthetic biology, both to inspire young students and to help bridge the gap between the scientific community and the general public. ## **PMOS Awareness**

PMOS affects between 10 and 13% of women worldwide <Citation reference="world-health-organization-2025-1" />, yet it remains widely underdiagnosed, in part because both public and clinical awareness lag behind its prevalence. Until recently, it did not even have an accurate name. Events under this pillar aim to close that gap directly, through low-barrier activities such as yoga or running and more focused events such as an awareness workshop. We also use the new PMOS terminology consistently across everything we produce. Some events go deeper into the science, while others simply put the key facts in front of more people; both raise awareness.&#x20;

### Using PMOS Instead of PCOS

<LayoutSidebar>
  <LayoutColumn>
    <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/2026-05-12-pmos-name-change/face1.avif" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom />
  </LayoutColumn>

  <LayoutColumn>
    Following the <Tooltip text="Professional, international medical organization in the field of endocrinology and metabolism, founded in 1916.">Endocrine Society</Tooltip>’s press release on May 12, 2026 <Citation reference="endocrine-society-2026" />, we adopted the term PMOS and began using it consistently across our wiki, slides, social media, and printed materials. The rename reflects a real clinical and scientific shift: “polycystic” is a misleading and stigmatizing term for a condition that involves far more than ovarian cysts. Adopting PMOS early allowed us to model the change we are advocating for, rather than waiting for it to become common knowledge.
  </LayoutColumn>
</LayoutSidebar>

**Why we did it:** In May 2026, *The Lancet* published the results of a 14-year global consensus process that renamed PCOS as polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) <Citation reference="teede-et-al-2026" />. The process was led by Professor Helena Teede of Monash University, with input from 56 professional societies and thousands of patients across six continents. The old name is inaccurate: “polycystic” implies that the syndrome is defined by abnormal ovarian cysts, while it is instead a broader endocrine and metabolic condition that affects insulin regulation, cardiovascular health, weight, and mental health, all of which the old name obscured.&#x20;

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/2026-05-12-pmos-name-change/article-screen.avif" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

This mismatch between name and reality has contributed to delayed diagnosis, fragmented care, and stigma, and it has held back research and policy attention for decades. Full clinical implementation is planned as a three-year rollout, with the new name expected to appear in the 2028 International Guideline update. As a result, most people, including many clinicians, do not yet recognize the term. As a project built around this condition, we felt we should help drive that change rather than wait for it to arrive. Our interview with [Dr. Mahnaz Bahri-Khomami](https://wiki.igemlund.org/human-practices#:~:text=Mahnaz%20Bahri%2DKhomami) from Monash University, a lead researcher on the name-change consensus process, confirmed our decision to adopt the new name and shaped how we communicate about PMOS in our education work. [Eleni Dube Zinatelli](https://wiki.igemlund.org/human-practices#:~:text=Eleni%20Dub%C3%A9%20Zinatelli,-PhD), [Lina Zabulienė](https://wiki.igemlund.org/human-practices#:~:text=Lina%20Zabulien%C4%97), [Elisabet Stener-Victorin](https://wiki.igemlund.org/human-practices#:~:text=Elisabet%20Stener%2DVictorin), and [Angelica Lindén Hirschberg](https://wiki.igemlund.org/human-practices#:~:text=Angelica%20Lind%C3%A9n%20Hirschberg) also encouraged us to keep using the new name consistently even while public awareness is still low, since normalizing it early is part of how a name change takes hold.&#x20;

**What we did:** We use "PMOS" throughout our wiki, presentation slides, and social media. For every new event, at the start we explain the terminology, "PMOS (formerly/also known as PCOS)". This allowed to spread awareness to everyone who encounters our work: event attendees, wiki visitors, survey respondents, social media followers, collaborators.

### PMOS Awareness Month

Although raising awareness was a constant effort throughout our project, the core of this work took place during PMOS Awareness Month in September. We organized a series of informal activities linked to PMOS, each paired with a short piece of awareness content and our printed fact sheet.

For an event of this size, it would help to have several team members circulating and starting conversations after the talk rather than leaving it as a single speech. Since food was served right afterward, this was a natural moment to talk with people, and one we didn’t fully use this time.

## Scientific Literacy

Where the PMOS awareness pillar focuses on reach, this pillar focuses on depth: teaching practical, transferable skills talking about synthetic biology in a concrete way. Our content ranged from dry-lab bioinformatics to plasmid design and we adapted it to each audience. For students, the activities were hands-on and technical. For younger and general audiences, through our coloring book and podcast, they were simplified but still concrete and engaging. In every case, we aimed for active learning rather than a passive introduction.

## Sustainability

This pillar is about **practicing what we preach**, in that order. We **built our own understanding first**, through the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate certification and internal workshop in June, before teaching it outward in the August lecture. Everything shared externally had already been tested internally, which is also why the two events below are directly cross-linked.&#x20;

      During an iGEM project most of the resource use happens in the lab.       ---

      The certificate covers **four topics**: energy consumption, plastic waste, water consumption, and the principles of green chemistry, each with a video and follow-up questions. Alongside the certificate, we discussed how the general advice could apply to our own situation in the wet lab.
    </LayoutColumn>

    <LayoutColumn>
      <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-2-sustiabaility/sustainability-internal-people-watching.avif" caption="Figure 1: Wet-lab team watching the My Green Lab videos." plain={false} noZoom={false} />

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    ---

    **Why we did it:** **Why we did it:** Education was not only an external goal for us but an internal one too. Labs consume five to ten times more energy than offices of the same size <Citation reference="Freese et al." />, and lab plastic waste is estimated to account for about 2% of global plastic production <Citation reference="urbina-et-al-2015" />. Those numbers alone made us want to learn more about lab sustainability and what could realistically be done about it.

    **What we did:** We found My Green Lab, an NGO that runs a tiered certification program for laboratories. Since full lab certification is a lengthy process, we chose the My Green Lab Ambassador certificate instead. The team members working in the lab (9 out of 14) watched the four videos together, took the quiz, and discussed what was actually feasible, given how little time we spend in the lab compared with a permanent lab group.

    <Layout2>
      <LayoutColumn>
        The whole wet lab committee took part and discussed the material together. This led to a follow-up meeting with the work environment coordinator Johan Teljer to learn more about sustainability practices at Kemicentrum. Many easy fixes, such as lowering water pressure and using non-toxic chemicals, are already implemented there. The session also raised day-to-day awareness among people spending time in the lab.

        ---

        **Outcome:** everyone thought about lab sustainability at least once as a result of this session. It likely would have had a bigger impact with consistent, recurring sustainability check-ins across our time in the lab, rather than a single session.
