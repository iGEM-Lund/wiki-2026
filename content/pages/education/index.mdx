**Our Approach to Education** 

PMOS is widely under-diagnosed and many people, including those at risk, lack awareness of this syndrome. This gap in public knowledge became one of the driving forces behind our education work.\
From there, our goals expanded: to offer insight into the world of synthetic biology, both to inspire young students and to help bridge the gap between the scientific community and the general public. Sustainability was a constant thread running through the project: These three pillars held the foundation of our education aims.

## **PMOS Awareness**

PMOS awareness wasn't confined to a single month, we raised it continuously through our social media, every event we ran regardless of pillar, collaborations, and iGEM meet-ups (DTU BioBrick Workshop, iGEM European Málaga \[!!add link!!]). The concentrated push was in PMOS Awareness Month, officially September: a series of small, informal social activities (a run, a study night, volleyball, yoga) rather than one large lecture, each paired with a short piece of awareness content and, from the second event on, a printed fact-sheet. The naming initiative, the Run, the Future by Lund workshop, Volleyball, the K-Guild Study Night, and Yoga are all part of it.

### Using PMOS Instead of PCOS

<Card>
  **Since:** 12 May 2026, ongoing throughout the project (introduced via an Instagram post) 

  **Audience:** Everyone who encounters our work, wiki visitors, event attendees, survey respondents, social media followers

  **Pillar(s):** PMOS Awareness

  We started using PMOS following the Endocrine Society's press release on 12 May 2026 (link/reference), and began using it consistently across our own wiki, slides, socials, and printed materials. The rename reflects a real clinical and scientific shift: "polycystic" is a misleading, stigmatizing term for a condition that's about far more than ovarian cysts. Using PMOS from the start let us model the change we're advocating for, rather than waiting for it to become common knowledge first.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    In May 2026, The Lancet published the results of a 14-year global consensus process, led by Professor Helena Teede of Monash University with input from 56 professional societies and thousands of patients across six continents, renaming PCOS to Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS). The old name is genuinely inaccurate: "polycystic" implies the condition is defined by abnormal ovarian cysts, but research shows there's no actual increase in pathological cysts in PCOS. What's really going on is a broader endocrine and metabolic condition affecting insulin regulation, cardiovascular health, weight, and mental health, which the old name obscured entirely. That mismatch between name and reality has contributed to delayed diagnosis, fragmented care, and stigma, and has held back research and policy attention for decades. Full clinical implementation is planned as a three-year rollout, with the new name expected in the 2028 International Guideline update, so at this stage, most people, including many clinicians, still don't recognize the term. As a project built around this exact condition, we felt we should be part of driving that change rather than waiting for it to arrive (LINK/Reference).

    We use "PMOS" throughout our wiki, presentation slides, and social media. For every new event, at the start we explain the terminology, "PMOS (formerly/also known as PCOS)", since the new name isn't yet widely recognized. This wasn't limited to the first months of the project; we did it consistently, event after event, whenever we were introducing the topic to a new audience.

    Similar information appeared on flyers handed out across almost every PMOS Awareness Month event, the Future by Lund workshop, the Volleyball event, the K-Guild Study Night, and the Yoga session, so anyone encountering our materials in person had the new terminology explained, and further information material linked. At the K-Guild Study Night, we overheard a group of attendees discussing amongst themselves why we kept saying "PMOS" during the presentation; we addressed it directly afterward, which turned into one of the more genuine engagement moments of that event. 

    Below, the PMOS awareness flyer is shown. 

    <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/awareness-flyer.avif" />

    We printed around 25 pages of flyers (4 per A4 sheet, 100 flyers total) and handed out at least 80 of them across events so far, with roughly 20 remaining. 

    We received feedback from Eleni Dube Zinatelli, Mahnaz Bahri Khomami, Lina Zabulienė, Elisabet Stener-Victorin, and Angelica Lindén Hirschberg (LINK THEM ALL), encouraging us to keep using the new name consistently even while public awareness is still low, since normalizing it early is part of how a name change like this actually takes hold.

    Leading with new terminology before it's familiar carries a real risk of confusing people, and we did have to explain it more than once, the K-Guild moment being the clearest example. But we'd make the same choice again: a project centered on this condition modeling outdated, inaccurate terminology would have undercut the destigmatization message we're trying to send everywhere else.

### &#x20;K-Guild Study Night

In collaboration with Studiemästeriet, the sub-committee behind K-Guild's regular study nights, iGEM Lund co-hosted a study night with free food, a shared study space, and a short talk on iGEM and PMOS. The event introduced Swedish students to iGEM while introducing international students to K-Guild's traditions, and raised PMOS awareness as part of PMOS Awareness Month. Approximately 70 students joined the study night and learned about PMOS and iGEM.&#x20;

K-Guild (K-sektionen) is the student guild for Chemical Engineering and Biotechnology at LTH, one of the subject-specific guilds within TLTH, Lund University's Faculty of Engineering student union. Studiemästeriet is the sub-committee within K-Guild that runs their regular study nights; this time, they partnered with us to co-host one built around PMOS and iGEM. The day before the event, we cooked together with Studiemästeriet to prepare the food. On the day, students could arrive from 5:00 pm, bringing their own lunch box and cutlery to fill with free food, then study together, with dessert served later in the evening. Tickets were free but required, booked in advance via Hitract so the team knew numbers ahead of time. The meal was PMOS-friendly and consisted of chickpea patties  and oven vegetables (sweet potato, potatoes, broccoli and parsnip) with Tzatziki. The dessert was an apple crumble. Since one of the symptoms of PMOS is insulin resistance, which somewhere between 35 - 80% of patients experience, a diet change towards a Mediterranean‑style and DASH-style can help to reduce symptoms such as inflammation or weight gain (<Citation reference="pmos-diet-2026" />). Diets alongside physical activity have shown to improve metabolic health, other parts of the illness are not as affected by it (<Citation reference="tellez-villagomez-et-al-2026" />). This highlights once more, why more research and alternative treatments are necessary. Nevertheless, we tried to implement these dietary requirements  into the food that was served. Before the meal, there was a short talk introducing iGEM and PMOS.

## Synthetic Biology Literacy

Where PMOS Awareness is about reach, this pillar is about depth: teaching real, transferable skills rather than just talking about synthetic biology. From dry lab bioinformatics  to plasmid design and molecular docking, we scaled the content to the audience, hands-on and technical for students, simplified but still concrete and engaging for younger (coloring book) or general audiences (Podcast), but never just a passive introduction.

### Student workshop at Ung Vetenskapssport

*25th April, Ekologihuset, Lund university LTH campus with 30 Highschool students from all over Sweden*

As a first event to promote synthetic biology literacy, we cooperated with [Ung Vetenskapssport](https://ungvetenskapssport.se/), an organization that holds every year  science and problem solving events for students from all over Sweden. During their biology camp at Lund university we held a lecture and workshop on the topics of molecular and synthetic biology. The activity was greatly appreciated and we received meaningful feedback on how to improve our activities.

For this first activity, we welcomed high school students from all over Sweden to Lund University for a hands-on session designed to build on their existing biology knowledge while introducing them to synthetic biology and its applications.

We opened with a short presentation covering the basics of molecular biology and an introduction to synthetic biology and our project, using guided questions rather than direct answers to keep students engaged, and closed with a quiz. Students then split into groups for a workshop activity, proposing their own solutions to a real-world problem they cared about. After a short exercise on codon–amino acid pairing, they got creative with a craft activity, building a plasmid from the different parts they'd learned about. We wrapped up with a group discussion where each team presented their solution and reflected on its ethical implications.

Around 30 students took part and gave us positive feedback. One wrote: "10/10, great! The crafts that were connected to it were so much fun and the lecturers were absolutely fantastic. How interesting!" We were glad to see several students particularly engaged with our project, asking follow-up questions about both the topic and the technical side of our solution.

Their feedback also helped us improve. Since students came from across Sweden with varying levels of background knowledge, we learned how important it is to keep everyone engaged and on the same page, adapting material on the fly when needed.

### Colouring book: The Tiny Helpers Inside You

Hormones, cells and biological signals can be difficult topics to explain to young children. We therefore realized *The Tiny Helpers Inside You*, a coloring and activity book for children aged 3–5. The book introduces these topics through a short story, illustrations, coloring and simple activities.

#### **What is inside the book?**

The story presents cells as “tiny helpers” working together inside the body. It introduces the idea that cells can send and receive signals, and shows what can happen when these signals become unbalanced. The explanations are kept short and are supported by pictures and activities. Children can color the characters, follow the story and complete simple exercises such as matching and finding differences.

#### **From the first draft to the final booklet**

The booklet started with an early concept draft made within the team. It set out the target age group, the idea of cells as “tiny helpers”, and a simple story about balance in the body. The draft was left open for changes and comments as the booklet developed.

From there, the illustrations were redrawn and refined for the final booklet. The characters, page layouts and other visual elements were adjusted along the way so that the pages remained clear and suitable for young children. The content was also adapted where needed to fit the illustrations and activities.

## Sustainability

This pillar is about **practicing what we preach**, in that order. We **built our own understanding first**, through the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate certification and internal workshop in June, before teaching it outward in the August lecture. Everything shared externally had already been tested internally, which is also why the two events below are directly cross-linked.&#x20;

<Card>
  ### **Sustainability in the Lab: Internal Workshop**

  **Date & location:** 1 June 2026, Biochemistry Library 

  **Target audience:** iGEM Lund wet lab team (9 people) 

  **Pillar(s):** Sustainability

  Since most of our actual resource use happens in the lab, we ran a dedicated sustainability event for the wet lab committee, centered on acquiring the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate from the non-profit My Green Lab. The certificate covers four topics: energy consumption, plastic waste, water consumption, and the principles of green chemistry, each with a video and follow-up questions. Alongside the certificate, we discussed how the general advice could apply to our own situation in the wet lab.

  <Expandable defaultOpen={false}>
    Education wasn't only an external goal for us. It was an internal one too. Lab energy consumption runs roughly five times higher than office space of the same size, and single-use plastic is estimated at around 2% of global plastic waste; those numbers alone made us want to learn more about lab sustainability and what could realistically be done about it.

    We found My Green Lab, an NGO that certifies labs through a graded certification program; since the full certification takes a long time, we opted instead for the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate. The team members working in the laboratory (9 out of 14 people), watched the four videos together, took the quiz, and discussed what was actually feasible given how little time we spend in the lab relative to a permanent lab group.

    <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-2-sustiabaility/2026-08-26-sustainability-lecture/group-2.avif" />

    The whole wet lab committee took part and discussed the material together. This led to a follow-up meeting with Johan Teljer to learn more about sustainability practices at Kemicentrum. Many easy fixes, such as lowering water pressure and using non-toxic chemicals, are already implemented there. The session also raised day-to-day awareness among people spending time in the lab.

    Everyone thought about lab sustainability at least once as a result of this session. It likely would have had a bigger impact with consistent, recurring sustainability check-ins across our time in the lab, rather than a single session.

    Alongside our goal of educating others on PMOS, synthetic biology, and sustainability, it was important to build this understanding internally first, so it could then be shared externally, including in the lecture about sustainability in the lab .

Feedback suggested students were, if anything, more interested in AnTigone itself than in the sustainability content specifically: one response to "Are there any topics, activities, or event formats you would like us to cover in the future?" simply read, *"More detail in personal project."*

    <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-2-sustiabaility/2026-08-26-sustainability-in-the-lab-lecture.avif" />

    That feedback told us two things. First, the interactive elements, Mentimeter and group discussion, worked and were worth keeping. Second, audiences consistently wanted more of the actual project, not less. We acted on both: we reused the Mentimeter-based format for the Future by Lund PMOS workshop that followed a few weeks later, and made sure the project itself was included more to that workshop than it had been here. The lecture's comparatively lower engagement is itself part of what we learned this semester about matching format to what an audience actually wants.

    This lecture is the direct outward-facing follow-on from the internal Sustainability in the Lab workshop: that session built our own understanding, and this one was our first attempt at sharing it with the wider student body. Its feedback fed forward directly into the design of the Future by Lund PMOS Awareness Workshop (LINK).
