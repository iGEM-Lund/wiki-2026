### **Our Approach to Education** 

<Tooltip text="Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome
">**PMOS**</Tooltip> is widely underdiagnosed, and many people, including those at risk, lack awareness of the syndrome (<Citation reference="world-health-organization-2026" narrative={false} />). This **gap** in public **knowledge** became one of the driving forces behind our education work. From there, our goals expanded: to offer insight into the world of <Tooltip text="Designing and building new biological parts and systems, or redesigning existing ones.">**synthetic biology**</Tooltip>, both to inspire young students and to help bridge the gap between the scientific community and the general public. **Sustainability** was a constant thread running through the project, both in educating ourselves and in sharing that knowledge with others.&#x20;

Together, these three **pillars** formed the **foundation** of our education aims.

## **PMOS Awareness**

PMOS affects **between 10 and 13%** of women worldwide (<Citation reference="world-health-organization-2026" narrative={false} />), yet it remains widely underdiagnosed, in part because both public and clinical awareness lag behind its prevalence. Until recently, it did not even have an accurate name. Events under this pillar aim to close that gap directly, through low-barrier activities such as yoga or running and more focused events such as an awareness workshop. We also use the new PMOS terminology consistently across everything we produce. Some events go deeper into the science, while others simply put the key facts in front of more people; both raise awareness.&#x20;

### Using PMOS Instead of PCOS

<LayoutSidebar>
  <LayoutColumn>
    <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/2026-05-12-pmos-name-change/face1.avif" alt={{"decorative":true,"text":"MISSING"}} numbered={false} refId="fig-cvm9c" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom />
  </LayoutColumn>

  <LayoutColumn>
    Following the <Tooltip text="Professional, international medical organization in the field of endocrinology and metabolism, founded in 1916.">Endocrine Society</Tooltip>’s **press release** on May 12, 2026 (<Citation reference="endocrine-society-2026" narrative={false} />), we adopted the term PMOS and began using it consistently across our wiki, slides, social media, and printed materials. The rename reflects a real clinical and scientific shift: “polycystic” is a misleading and stigmatizing term for a condition that involves far more than ovarian cysts. Adopting PMOS early allowed us to model the change we are advocating for, rather than waiting for it to become common knowledge.
  </LayoutColumn>
</LayoutSidebar>

**Why we did it:** In May 2026, <Tooltip text="A leading international medical journal.">***The Lancet***</Tooltip> published the results of a 14-year global <Tooltip text="A structured process in which experts and stakeholders work towards an agreed position.">consensus process</Tooltip> that renamed PCOS as polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) (<Citation reference="teede-et-al-2026" narrative={false} />), see <Ref target="fig-jewn5" />. The process was led by **Professor Helena Teede** of **Monash University**, with input from 56 professional societies and thousands of patients across six continents. The old name is inaccurate and implies that the syndrome is defined by abnormal ovarian cysts, while it is instead a broader endocrine and metabolic condition that affects insulin regulation, cardiovascular health, weight, and mental health, all of which the old name obscured .&#x20;

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/2026-05-12-pmos-name-change/article-screen.avif" alt="Headline of The Lancet 2026 article on PCOS name change to PMOS. The title is  &#x22;Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, the new name for polycystic ovary syndrome: a multistep global consensus process&#x22;." caption="Headline of The Lancet 2026 article on PCOS name change to PMOS" numbered refId="fig-jewn5" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

This mismatch between name and reality has contributed to delayed diagnosis, fragmented care, and stigma, and it has held back research and policy attention for decades. Full clinical implementation is planned as a three-year rollout, with the new name expected to appear in the 2028 International Guideline update. As a result, most people, including many clinicians, do not yet recognize the term. As a project built around this condition, we felt we should help drive that change rather than wait for it to arrive. Our interview with <Ref target="card-9nxm9" linkText="Dr. Mahnaz Bahri-Khomami" /> from Monash University, a lead researcher on the name-change consensus process, confirmed our decision to adopt the new name. <Ref target="card-b6bm7" linkText="Eleni Dube Zinatelli" />, <Ref target="card-bn189" />, <Ref target="card-vw0vs" />, and <Ref target="card-vif4x" /> also encouraged us to keep using the new name consistently even while public awareness is still low, since normalizing it early is part of how a name change takes hold.&#x20;

**What we did:** We use "PMOS" throughout our wiki, presentation slides, and social media. For every new event, at the start we explain the terminology, **"PMOS (formerly/also known as PCOS)"**. This allowed us to spread awareness to everyone who encounters our work: event attendees, wiki visitors, survey respondents, social media followers, collaborators.

### Outcome and what we learned

Across this month’s activities we asked participants for feedback on our approach. Response rates were low at the activity-first events (4 of 26 at the volleyball session, 4 of 30 at yoga), so we treat these results as indicative rather than conclusive. Within that limit, respondents consistently appreciated the simpler formats.

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/pillar-1-pmos-awareness/pmos-yoga-feedback.avif" alt="PMOS awareness yoga result from feedback: 4 out 4 respondants say they would participate again to low barrier events like yoga. " caption="Answers to the feedback form for the PMOS yoga event." numbered refId="fig-vmeid" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

We also observed that self-reported PMOS learning appeared lower at activity-first events.  We did not measure knowledge directly, so we cannot quantify the gap. We judged the trade-off worth making: these events reached people who would not have attended a dedicated workshop, and conveyed the basic message that PMOS is common and frequently undiagnosed.

At our more structured workshop, all respondents reported learning something new about PMOS, and 46% of them said they learned a lot.&#x20;

**Going forward**, we would measure learning with a short pre- and post-event question so the comparison rests more on data, and extend beyond the student population we mostly reached this year.

## Scientific Literacy

Where the PMOS awareness pillar focuses on reach, this pillar focuses on **depth**: teaching practical, transferable skills. Our content ranged from <Tooltip text="Computational work, as opposed to experiments at the bench.">dry-lab</Tooltip> <Tooltip text="Using computers to analyse biological data such as DNA and protein sequences.">bioinformatics</Tooltip> to <Tooltip text="Planning which DNA parts go into a plasmid and in what order.">plasmid design</Tooltip> and we adapted it to each audience. The events were designed integrating the feedback we received from <Ref target="card-fdgxp" /> on how to better approach educational outreach. For students, the activities were hands-on and technical. For younger and general audiences they were simplified but still **concrete and engaging**. In every case, we aimed for active learning rather than a passive introduction.&#x20;

### The Tiny Helpers Inside You: Coloring Book & Library Visit

<Layout2>
  <LayoutColumn>
    Hormones, cells, and biological signals can be difficult topics to explain to young children. We created ***The Tiny Helpers Inside You***, a coloring and activity book for children aged 3–5 that introduces these topics through a short story, illustrations, coloring, and simple activities. The story presents cells as “tiny helpers” working together inside the body, introduces the idea that cells send and receive signals, and shows what can happen when those signals become unbalanced. Explanations are kept short and supported by pictures and activities. Children can color the characters, follow the story, and complete simple exercises such as matching games and spot-the-difference puzzles.

## Sustainability

This pillar is about **practicing what we preach**, in that order. We **built our own understanding first**, through the My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate certification and internal workshop in June, before teaching it outward in the August lecture. Everything shared externally had already been tested internally, which is also why the two events below are directly cross-linked.&#x20;

    ### Sustainability in the Lab: Internal Workshop
  </CardTitle>

<Layout2>
  <LayoutColumn>
    During an iGEM project most of the resource use happens in the lab. Therefore we organized a dedicated sustainability event for our <Tooltip text="The experimental part of a project, done at the bench.">wet lab</Tooltip> members, centered on acquiring the [My Green Lab Ambassador Certificate](https://mygreenlab.org/programs/ambassador-program/) from the non-profit [My Green Lab](https://mygreenlab.org/).&#x20;

    ---

    The certificate covers **four topics**: energy consumption, plastic waste, water consumption, and the principles of <Tooltip text="Designing chemical processes to reduce waste and hazardous substances.">green chemistry</Tooltip>, each with a video and follow-up questions. Alongside the certificate, we discussed how the general advice could apply to our own situation in the wet lab.

    **Why we did it:** Education was not only an external goal for us but an internal one too. Labs consume five to ten times more energy than offices of the same size <Citation reference="freese-et-al-2024" narrative={false} />, and lab plastic waste is estimated to account for about 2% of global plastic production <Citation reference="urbina-et-al-2015" narrative={false} />. Those numbers alone made us want to learn more about lab sustainability and what could realistically be done about it.
  </LayoutColumn>

  <LayoutColumn>
    <FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/education/pillar-2-sustiabaility/sustainability-internal-people-watching.avif" alt="Wet-lab team watching the My Green Lab videos." caption="Wet-lab team watching the My Green Lab videos." numbered refId="fig-71dtj" plain={false} noZoom={false} />
  </LayoutColumn>
</Layout2>

**What we did:** We found My Green Lab, an <Tooltip text="Non-governmental organisation: a non-profit group independent of government.">NGO</Tooltip> that runs a tiered certification program for laboratories. Since full lab certification is a lengthy process, we chose the My Green Lab Ambassador certificate instead. The team members working in the lab (9 out of 14) watched the four videos together, took the quiz, and discussed what was actually feasible, given how little time we spend in the lab compared with a permanent lab group.

<Layout2>
  <LayoutColumn>
    The whole wet lab committee took part and discussed the material together. This led to a follow-up meeting with the work environment coordinator <Ref target="card-xwbvu" /> to learn more about sustainability practices at <Tooltip text="The chemistry building on Lund University's campus.">Kemicentrum</Tooltip>. Many easy fixes, such as lowering water pressure and using non-toxic chemicals, are already implemented there. The session also raised day-to-day awareness among people spending time in the lab.

    ---

    **Outcome:** everyone thought about lab sustainability at least once as a result of this session. It likely would have had a bigger impact with consistent, recurring sustainability check-ins across our time in the lab, rather than a single session.
  </LayoutColumn>
