An **economic assessment** checks whether a product is **feasible** outside the lab: who would use it, what it costs to make and what it could sell for.

A complete assessment of **AnTigone**, a genetically modified <Tooltip text="A medicine made of live microorganisms, here a bacterium that works inside the body.">live biotherapeutic</Tooltip> product (GMO-LBP) that lowers androgen levels locally in the gut for a <Tooltip text="Reaching and acting on the whole body, not just the gut.">systemic effect</Tooltip>, would include: market analysis, <Tooltip text="Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats (SWOT) and Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal and Environmental (PESTLE): the outside factors that affect a project.">SWOT/PESTLE</Tooltip>, <Tooltip text="An existing product that a new product is compared against.">comparator evaluation</Tooltip>, regulatory pathway, cost analysis, <Tooltip text="Moving from small laboratory production to large-scale manufacturing.">scale-up</Tooltip> economics, price estimation, business model, equity and accessibility, and funding and risks.

At this stage in the project, our economic assessment focuses on two parts: a **comparator evaluation** (where does AnTigone fit among current PMOS treatments in Sweden?) and a **price analysis** (what price range is realistic?).

Metformin, spironolactone and the contraceptive pills cost **SEK 34 to 135** per month. Weight-management drugs cost **SEK 2,600 to 5,200** per month <Tooltip text="Without the state paying part of the cost.">without subsidy</Tooltip>. AnTigone would act locally in the gut and lower testosterone throughout the body.&#x20;

In order to compete, we ***suggest*** a target price of **SEK 300 to 1,000 per month** as it is greater than that of <Tooltip text="Copies of a brand-name medicine sold after its patent expires, usually much cheaper.">generic drugs</Tooltip> and over-the-counter supplements but below weight-management drugs. This is a ***target range***, not a calculated price, because production and regulatory costs are still unknown.

## Price Analysis

We classify our product as a **live biotherapeutic product (LBP)** and compare four product categories (**conventional probiotics, GMO probiotics, LBPs and GMO-LBPs**), because no comparable PMOS product exists.

### Product Classification

The product would be an oral capsule with live, genetically engineered bacteria for a **therapeutic** purpose, so it would be developed as a **live biotherapeutic product (LBP).** Because the bacterial strain is genetically modified, additional safety assessments and <Tooltip text="Genetically modified organism.">GMO</Tooltip>-related regulatory requirements may apply in Sweden and the EU. The final regulatory classification and applicable requirements would need to be confirmed with the [relevant authorities](/biosafety) (<Citation reference="committee-for-medicinal-products-for-human-use-chmp-2006" narrative={false} />; <Citation reference="rouanet-et-al-2020" narrative={false} />).

This price analysis compares four categories: **conventional probiotic supplements, GMO probiotic supplements, <Tooltip text="Non-Genetically Modified Organism used as a Live Biotherapeutic Product">non-GMO-LBP</Tooltip>s**, and **GMO-LBPs**. Swedish products are used as the primary market benchmarks. No directly comparable product exists for PMOS, so the comparison is only a rough guide.

The closest reference is [**ZBiotics Sugar-to-Fiber**](https://zbiotics.com/products/sugar-to-fiber) in the US, a drink mix with genetically engineered *Bacillus subtilis*, which costs **USD 80** (≈ €72) for a 30-day supply (<Citation reference="z-biotics-company-n-d" narrative={false} />). ### **Cost Estimation**

AnTigone would be a **GMO-LBP,** so its cost structure is closer to an LBP than to a probiotic supplement. It would carry the LBP costs (clinical development, GMP manufacturing, regulatory approval) plus GMO-specific ones (environmental risk assessment, biocontainment).&#x20;

**Two things that could likely lower its costs:**&#x20;

* It uses one defined *E. coli* strain and not screened human stool, so no <Tooltip text="Testing stool donors for infections and other health risks before their material is used.">donor screening</Tooltip> is needed, as with Rebyota and Vowst.&#x20;
* And since PMOS is chronic, development costs can be recovered through monthly sales to a larger group of patients.

These comparisons show what drives the price but not AnTigone’s actual cost. We therefore set a **preliminary target** of **SEK 300 to 1,000 per month** based on competition. It is above generics, pills and supplements (SEK 34 to 289), which it must justify as a new non-hormonal option, and below weight-management drugs (SEK 2,600 to 5,200). It is a target, ***not*** a cost-based estimate. [ZBiotics](https://zbiotics.com/) sells an engineered live bacterial product at about SEK 790 per month, which suggests the upper end is more realistic, though it is not a medicine and cannot predict AnTigone’s costs.

A more accurate estimate needs the **daily bacterial dose**, **production costs** and **development costs** divided over the expected patient population. That cost model is beyond this assessment.

## **Limitations and Outlook**

**We asked whether AnTigone could work outside the lab: where it fits among current PMOS treatments, and what price is realistic.**&#x20;

As a GMO-LBP, its costs would be closer to approved LBPs than to supplements, so we set a competition-based target of **SEK 300 to 1,000 per month**, above generics, pills and supplements and below weight-management drugs. Our <Ref target="card-tr7bj" /> supports a competitive price: if the product were **significantly more expensive** and **not reimbursed,** 6 of 13 clinicians would recommend it only to patients who can afford it, and 4 called cost a major barrier.

The target is based on competitors’ prices, not a cost model, so it does not show what AnTigone would cost to make.&#x20;

The comparisons are imperfect: the only GMO probiotic reference is one US product, the approved LBPs treat a different condition, and no GMO-LBP is on the market. We also covered only the comparator and price parts of a full economic assessment, without market size, scale-up, funding or a business model.

The conclusion is that feasibility depends largely on **production** and **regulatory** costs, which a future cost model has to determine.
