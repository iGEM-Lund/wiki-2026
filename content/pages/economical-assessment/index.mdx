## Introduction

An economic assessment is an evaluation of the feasibility of a product considering its target market and consumers, production costs, and price estimation. It is useful for analyzing the costs and benefits of a project to determine whether it is feasible outside the lab and can be sold on the market.

A complete economic assessment for a product like AnTigone, a genetically modified <Tooltip text="A medicine made of live microorganisms, here a bacterium that works inside the body.">living biotherapeutic</Tooltip> (LBT) aimed at reducing androgen levels locally in the gut for a systemic effect, would typically include:

* Market analysis
* <Tooltip text="Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats: a framework for assessing a project's position.">SWOT</Tooltip>/<Tooltip text="Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal and Environmental: the outside factors that affect a project.">PESTLE</Tooltip> analyses
* <Tooltip text="An existing product that a new product is compared against.">Comparator</Tooltip> evaluation
* Regulatory pathway considerations
* Cost analysis
* <Tooltip text="Moving from small laboratory production to large-scale manufacturing.">Scale-up</Tooltip> economics
* Price estimation
* Business model
* Equity and accessibility considerations
* Funding and risks

However, given our resources and early stage as a student team, our economic assessment primarily focused on comparative analysis and price analysis.&#x20;

## Assessment

Traditional treatments for PMOS act systemically in the body and include metformin, spironolactone, and combined contraceptive pills, which have a monthly price range of SEK 34–135, while weight-management medications range from SEK 2,600–5,200 without subsidy. On the other hand, our product acts locally in the gut to induce a systemic effect, lowering testosterone levels throughout the body. In order for our product to compete with the comparators mentioned above, a monthly price of SEK 300–1,000 would be a reasonable target, as it is greater than that of <Tooltip text="Copies of a brand-name medicine sold after its patent expires, usually much cheaper.">generic drugs</Tooltip> and over-the-counter supplements but lower than that of the weight-management drugs. However, the price for our product has not yet been determined, and a preliminary price range will be estimated following further assessment and taking into account production and regulatory considerations. 

### Price Analysis

#### Product Classification and Pricing Approach

The proposed approach for PMOS is an oral capsule containing live, genetically engineered bacteria. As the product is intended for a therapeutic purpose, it will be developed as a live biotherapeutic product (LBT/LBP). Because the bacterial strain is genetically modified, additional safety assessments and <Tooltip text="Genetically modified organism.">GMO</Tooltip>-related regulatory requirements may apply in Sweden and the EU. The final regulatory classification and applicable requirements would need to be confirmed with the relevant [authorities](https://wiki.igemlund.org/biosafety) ([Environmental risk assessments for medicinal products containing, or consisting of, genetically modified organisms (GMOs) - Scientific guideline | European Medicines Agency (EMA)](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/scientific-guidelines/environmental-risk-assessments-medicinal-products-containing-or-consisting-genetically-modified-organisms-gmos)), ([Frontiers | Live Biotherapeutic Products, A Road Map for Safety Assessment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2020.00237/full)).

This price analysis compares three product categories: conventional probiotic supplements, non-GMO live biotherapeutic products (LBTs), and genetically modified LBTs (GMO-LBTs). Swedish products are used as the primary market benchmarks, with European and US examples included where relevant. As no directly comparable marketed product for the proposed PMOS treatment has been identified, an illustrative cost model is used to estimate its potential price.

**Genetically modified probiotics (GMO probiotics)**

GMO probiotics are genetically modified live bacteria that are genetically engineered or modified to carry out a particular function. In contrast to conventional probiotics, which contain naturally-occurring live microorganisms, GMO probiotics contain live, genetically altered strains. Because they are genetically modified, GMO probiotics are subject to additional regulations. An environmental risk assessment is required by EU GMO legislation, which is not mandatory for conventional probiotics, to ensure biocontainment and safety under Directive 2001/83/EC (EMA).

There are currently no GMO probiotics sold in the EU market, making a direct price comparison not possible. The nearest reference is ZBiotics in the US, which sells genetically engineered *Bacillus subtilis* in the form of their ZBiotics Sugar-to-Fiber probiotic drink mix, which has a one-time price of $80 (≈ €72) for a 30-day supply ([ZBiotics, n.d.](https://zbiotics.com/products/sugar-to-fiber?variant=53865150185844)). Comparing this to the average monthly price of conventional probiotics in Sweden (≈ €10) indicates that a genetically modified probiotic may lead to increased costs of roughly 590%.

**Live biotherapeutic products (LBPs)**

Live biotherapeutic products (LBPs) are products containing live microorganisms intended to prevent, treat or cure a disease. Unlike conventional probiotic supplements, which are generally marketed to support health, LBPs are developed for therapeutic purposes and require evidence of quality, safety and efficacy.

LBPs are priced considerably higher than probiotics, as they are associated with additional production costs. These increased costs mainly come from the presence of living organisms, which need to be produced and stored in specific conditions, typically anaerobic conditions with controlled humidity, to survive, as well as strict quality control measures that must be taken, leading to increased costs for the development and performance of strain-specific identity, potency, and anaerobic bioburden assays ([Microbiome Therapeutics Innovation Group & Barberio, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiomes/articles/10.3389/frmbi.2024.1441290/pdf)). There are currently only two LBPs on the market: Rebyota and Vowst, whose forms, doses, and prices are summarized below (tab-10er7).&#x20;

As the market grows, increased investment and more established testing and regulatory pathways may arise, simplifying the process and lowering the cost of bringing a new LBP like AnTigone to the market.

**Genetically modified live biotherapeutic products (GMO LBPs)**

Genetically modified live biotherapeutic products are therapeutic products containing live microorganisms that have been genetically modified to perform a specific function in the body. Their development may involve additional safety assessments and regulatory requirements associated with genetically modified organisms (GMOs), leading to an increased product cost ([Frontiers | Microbiome engineering: engineered live biotherapeutic products for treating human disease](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.1000873/full) ). 

The[ European Commission's guidance on GMO aspects of investigational medicinal products](https://health.ec.europa.eu/medicinal-products/product-types/advanced-therapies/genetically-modified-organism-gmo-aspects-investigational-medicinal-products_en) describes the additional regulatory considerations that can apply to medicinal products containing genetically modified organisms. As of this assessment, GMO LBPs remain under clinical development, and no products in this category have received approval for commercial sale.

**Estimated cost drivers and pricing of AnTigone**

Most of the cost drivers associated with approved LBPs would also apply to AnTigone, making its cost structure closer to that of a live biotherapeutic product than a conventional probiotic supplement. However, AnTigone may avoid some costs associated with Rebyota and Vowst because it uses a single, defined *E. coli* strain rather than screened human stool, reducing the need for donor screening. Furthermore, as PMOS is a chronic condition, development costs could be recovered over time through recurring monthly sales to a larger patient population. However, AnTigone would also face additional regulatory costs related to its genetically modified nature, including environmental risk assessment, biocontainment measures and GMO-specific clinical trial requirements.

These comparisons help identify the factors that may affect AnTigone's price, but they are not sufficient to determine its actual cost. The proposed monthly price range of SEK 300–1,000 falls between conventional probiotics and approved LBPs, but this price range needs further justification beyond a comparison of existing product prices. Therefore, the proposed range should be treated as a preliminary pricing target rather than a cost-based estimate. To assess whether this range is realistic, ZBiotics provides a useful market reference, with its engineered live bacterial product priced at approximately SEK 790 per month. However, ZBiotics is not sold as a medicine, whereas AnTigone would require clinical development, GMP-compliant manufacturing and regulatory approval as a medicinal product. Therefore, ZBiotics's price cannot directly predict AnTigone's costs. It does, however, provide a useful market comparison, suggesting that the upper end of the proposed SEK 300-1,000 monthly range may be more realistic. Nevertheless, the actual price would depend on AnTigone's development and manufacturing costs, which cannot be determined from the available comparisons alone. A more accurate price estimate would require detailed calculations of the daily bacterial dose, production costs and development expenses distributed across the expected patient population. However, this level of cost modeling is beyond the scope of this assessment.  

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, our economic assessment shows that conventional probiotics cost around SEK 40-220 per month, while GMO probiotics cost approximately SEK 790 per month. Approved non-GMO live biotherapeutic products (LBTs) are considerably more expensive, with treatment courses ranging from $9,338 to $19,231. As AnTigone is being developed as a therapeutic product, its costs are expected to be closer to those of LBTs than conventional probiotics, with additional expenses potentially arising from GMO-specific regulatory approval and environmental safety assessments. However, estimating its final price remains difficult because GMO-based LBTs are not yet widely available commercially, and their costs are still uncertain. Since AnTigone uses a defined bacterial strain, donor-screening costs may not apply. Although GMO-specific requirements may increase initial development costs, advances in manufacturing and regulatory processes could help reduce costs in the future.
