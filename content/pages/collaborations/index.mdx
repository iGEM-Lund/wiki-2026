  Beyond scientific discussions, we aim to bring the global iGEM community closer together, exchange ideas, and highlight the people and stories behind the science!

  Together with iGEM teams from Aarhus, Oslo, Stockholm, Copenhagen, <Tooltip text="University of Southern Denmark">SDU</Tooltip>, <Tooltip>BKE</Tooltip><Tooltip text="Bagsværd Kostskole in Copenaghen.">A</Tooltip>, and DTU, we exchanged project ideas and experiences through team pitches and discussions. We also had the chance to meet experts and <Tooltip text="Volunteers from the iGEM community who advise teams.">iGEM Ambassadors</Tooltip>, whose advice helped guide our project.

  And of course, we brought **iGEM Lund 2026 podcast** - [The Sequence](https://open.spotify.com/show/1anr7zYZ3pKdntKfGcVEvz?si=j3_TYq5HQr-aZCCynVjoTQ\&utm_source=copy-link) - to DTU!       **Wet Lab & Dry Lab Workshops**

      The workshop gave us the opportunity to strengthen both our experimental and computational skills. In the **wet lab**, we practiced <Tooltip text="Copying a piece of DNA and inserting it into a plasmid so it can be produced in bacteria.">molecular cloning</Tooltip>, including <Tooltip text="Joining DNA pieces together in a planned order.">DNA assembly</Tooltip>, <Tooltip text="Getting bacteria to take up new DNA.">bacterial transformation</Tooltip>, and <Tooltip text="Spreading bacteria on an agar plate so that single colonies grow.">plating</Tooltip>. Meanwhile, the **dry lab** introduced us to protein sequence analysis and molecular docking, where we used tools such as <Tooltip text="A tool that compares a sequence against databases of known sequences.">BLAST</Tooltip> and protein structure databases to identify proteins and explore their interactions with potential <Tooltip text="The molecules an enzyme acts on and converts.">substrates</Tooltip>.

      Both sessions provided valuable skills that supported our own experimental work, protein modeling, and enzyme optimization.

  **Human Practices & Wiki Development**

  Beyond technical skills, we learned how to make our project more **responsible** and **accessible**. The Human Practices workshop introduced us to <Tooltip text="Mapping the people and groups affected by a project and what each of them needs from it.">**stakeholder** **analysis**</Tooltip>, which we later applied to refine our project goals and safety considerations. The Wiki workshops provided practical guidance on page structure, visual design, and <Tooltip text="A platform for storing code and website files, which iGEM uses to host team wikis.">GitLab</Tooltip> collaboration, helping us organize and present our project more effectively.

  **Bringing The Sequence to DTU!**

  One of the highlights of the meetup was to record **The Sequence** in person! We welcomed iGEM Oslo and SDU for a joint episode and recorded a DTU Special with DTU BioBuilders. Through these conversations, we shared our projects, exchanged experiences, and brought the spirit of collaboration beyond the workshop.

  A big **thank you** to DTU BioBuilders and all the participating teams for making this such a memorable experience!

      **Mini Jamboree**

      At the Mini Jamboree, we participated in a presentation and poster competition, sharing our project and receiving valuable feedback from judges. They raised important questions about <Tooltip text="How selectively an enzyme acts on one molecule rather than on similar ones.">enzyme specificity</Tooltip>, testosterone regulation, and biosafety, encouraging us to consider <Tooltip text="A genetic circuit that switches on only when both inputs are present.">AND-gate</Tooltip> circuits, <Tooltip text="Circuits where the output regulates its own production.">feedback loops</Tooltip>, and <Tooltip text="Making the bacterium depend on a nutrient that is only supplied in the intended place, so it cannot survive elsewhere.">auxotrophy-based containment</Tooltip>.

      Based on their feedback, we placed greater emphasis on biosafety, exploring AND-gate systems, <Tooltip text="Using the bacteria's cell-density signalling to limit how many cells can build up.">quorum-sensing-based containment</Tooltip>, and auxotrophy to improve the safety of our engineered <Tooltip text="Live microorganisms that give a health benefit when swallowed.">probiotic</Tooltip>. More details can be found on our [Biosafety page](/biosafety).

      We were also proud to receive the *Best Overall Project Award!* 🏆 This recognition gave us greater confidence and motivation for the months ahead.
    </LayoutColumn>

      **Outcome:**

      We were asked two essential questions:&#x20;

      1. How do we know that testosterone is not reduced too much?
      2. How much testosterone can actually be reduced, and how much gets recirculated?

      For the first question, our biosafety work gives a partial answer in design: we propose combining the <Tooltip text="A switch that turns the enzyme on only when oxygen is low, as in the gut.">hypoxia-responsive gut gate</Tooltip> with a testosterone-responsive <Tooltip text="A protein that binds DNA to switch a gene on or off.">transcription factor</Tooltip> (<Tooltip text="A transcription factor, originally found in the bacterium Comamonas testosteroni, that responds to testosterone.">TeiR</Tooltip>), so that the enzyme is only produced when testosterone is above a threshold, and expression is expected to decrease as testosterone falls. For details, see our [biosafety](/biosafety#gut-gate) page. This is a proposed design and is untested.&#x20;

      For the second question we have no data. Both can only be answered by experiments and, much later, by clinical studies.
    </LayoutColumn>
