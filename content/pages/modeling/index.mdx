## Overview&#x20;

**AnTigone** targets hyperandrogenism driven by the gut-androgen axis, a pathway in which colonic bacteria convert steroid precursors into bioactive androgens, particularly testosterone, which are then reabsorbed into circulation. Our dry-lab work asks which engineered enzyme variants are worth building to reduce testosterone, and how those variants would be expected to behave once placed in a living bacterial system.&#x20;

Two enzymes are central to this axis. **AKR1D1** is the human hepatic **Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductase**, a member of the aldo-keto reductase superfamily, which inactivates androgens and glucocorticoids in the liver and initiates bile acid synthesis (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2014" />; <Citation reference="faucher-et-al-2008" />).

**ci2350** is a bacterial Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductase from *Clostridium innocuum*, identified by Arp et al. (2025) as a member of the **Old Yellow Enzyme family (COG1902)**, a flavin-dependent ene-reductase that couples steroid reduction to NADH oxidation under the anaerobic conditions of the gut. It has been shown to reduce progesterone, cortisone and hydrocortisone directly, and *C. innocuum* extracts have been reported to reduce testosterone (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" />, <Citation reference="stokes-et-al-1985" />).

The two enzymes are not homologous: they belong to unrelated families and have converged independently on the same 5β-reduction chemistry, with AKR1D1 acting on Δ4-3-ketosteroids spanning across **C18** to **C21 classes** (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2011" />), while ci2350 has been shown to reduce **C21 steroids** (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" />).

### **The selectivity problem**

The capacity of both enzymes to accept testosterone as a substrate makes it a candidate for engineering, but wild-type activity alone is insufficient. Effective therapeutic application requires that the enzyme preferentially reduces testosterone over structurally similar steroids, and progesterone is the most demanding comparison. The two molecules share the same core steroid scaffold and differ critically at a single position: testosterone carries a **17β-hydroxyl group**, whereas progesterone bears a **C17 acetyl substituent** (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2014" />). Any engineered selectivity must therefore be achieved by discriminating between these two chemically subtle substituents on an otherwise identical molecule.

This problem cannot be addressed experimentally without first knowing which variants are structurally worth testing. The sequence space of possible active site mutations is far larger than can be screened at the bench, so a computational filter is needed to narrow it to a tractable set.

Binding affinity alone, however, does not predict how a variant would behave in a bacterial system. Cell growth, enzyme expression levels, and competition between substrates all shape the outcome, and a variant that binds testosterone well in isolation may still perform poorly when those factors are considered.

## Our approach

Our dry lab work addresses both challenges through two integrated strands.

1. **Enzyme specificity:** Perform rational mutagenesis on ci2350 and AKR1D1 that make the enzyme prefer testosterone over progesterone, using molecular docking to guide which residues to change.
2. **Mathematical modelling:** Build a mechanistic model of bacterial growth, enzyme expression and competitive kinetics that uses the docking results to predict how each variant would perform in practice and which parameters most affect the prediction.

Together, these form a connected predictive layer: the first narrows a large design space to a small set of structurally plausible candidates, and the second takes those candidates and asks whether their effect would actually show up in a real bacterial system.

Three-dimensional structures for the two candidate enzymes were obtained through different routes, reflecting the availability of experimental data for each. The human AKR1D1 structure was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (<Citation reference="berman-et-al-2000" />) (PDB entry 3CAV), resolved at 1.90 Å in complex with NADPH (<Citation reference="faucher-et-al-2008" />).

### Structure preparation

All structures were prepared following the standard AutoDock receptor preparation protocol (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" />,<Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" />). Water molecules and non-essential heteroatoms were removed. NADPH was present in the PDB structure of AKR1D1 and was retained to preserve the biologically relevant binding geometry. For ci2350, no cofactor present, since it is a predicted structure.&#x20;

Hydrogen atoms were added, and protonation states were assigned at physiological pH (7.4), consistent with standard receptor preparation for molecular docking (<Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" />). Structures were then converted to PDBQT format using MGLTools (prepare\_receptor4.py) (<Citation reference="morris-et-al-2009" />), which assigns Gasteiger partial charges and defines rotatable bonds for the docking software.

### Ligand preparation

Testosterone and progesterone share the four-ring steroid scaffold, with rings labelled A to D from the end bearing the C3 ketone (<Citation reference="the-nomenclature-of-steroids-1989" />). They are identical across rings A to C, including the Δ4-3-keto motif on the A ring that is the site of reduction, and differ only at the C17 substituent on the D ring, with 17β-hydroxyl in testosterone and an acetyl group in progesterone (<Ref target="mod-zqfnc" /> and <Ref target="mod-az7ct" />, below).

### Conserved domain analysis

The sequence of each candidate enzyme was submitted to the NCBI Conserved Domain Search (CD Search) (<Citation reference="marchler-bauer-et-al-2017" />), which compares a query sequence against the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) to identify evolutionarily conserved functional domains and their annotated residue positions. These annotations cover substrate binding, cofactor coordination and catalytic function, and are inferred from structurally and biochemically characterised members of the same protein family. They provided a sequence-level map of the expected functional site and served as the reference for interpreting and validating the predicted active site pockets.

#### **For AKR1D1:**&#x20;

CD Search assigned the query sequence (NP\_005980.1) to the AKR\_AKR1D1-3 family within the aldo-keto reductase superfamily, returning 32 annotated active site positions and a separately annotated catalytic tetrad (<Ref target="tab-vkjtr" />).

E120 is what distinguishes AKR1D1 from the rest of the superfamily: it replaces the histidine conserved in all other AKRs and acts as a superacid, polarising the C3 ketone (<Citation reference="di-costanzo-et-al-2008" />).

### Active site identification

Binding pockets were identified computationally using fpocket v4.0 (<Citation reference="le-guilloux-et-al-2009" />), which detects cavities through Voronoi tessellation and alpha-sphere clustering and returns a ranked list of candidate pockets, each described by geometric and physicochemical descriptors. These predicted pockets were then cross-referenced against residue annotations from NCBI CD Search to identify which pocket best corresponded to the known catalytic and substrate-binding residues of each enzyme family. The pocket showing the greatest agreement with the annotated functional site was selected.

#### **For AKR1D1:**

fpocket identified 22 candidate pockets in the AKR1D1 structure. Cross-referencing against the CD Search annotations showed a single dominant match: Pocket 1 contained 29 of the 32 annotated active site residues and all four members of the catalytic tetrad (<Ref target="tab-ogugt" />). The remaining pockets carried five or fewer annotated residues each, and twelve contained none. Pocket 1 was therefore selected as the functional active site.

### Molecular docking&#x20;

Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.2 (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" />) with an exhaustiveness setting of 32 and an energy range of 4 kcal/mol. Vina combines a stochastic global search for scoring, which samples ligand position, orientation and torsion angles, with local gradient-based optimisation that refines promising candidates into nearby energy minima.

Each ligand was docked independently into each prepared enzyme structure, and the top-ranked pose was recorded along with its predicted binding affinity in kcal/mol. To establish a selectivity baseline, we defined a selectivity score as the difference in predicted affinity between testosterone and progesterone, ΔG(testosterone) – ΔG(progesterone). A negative value indicates that testosterone is preferred by the enzyme. The inherent stochastic variability of the Vina scoring function, approximately ±0.5 kcal/mol, was treated as the noise threshold (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" />), and selectivity differences below this value were not considered meaningful.

After docking, the two enzymes gave comparable absolute affinities but opposite directions of preference.

  | **AKR1D1** | −9.8                       | −9.3                       | +0.5                         | Progesterone   |
  | **ci2350** | −9.1                       | −9.2                       | −0.1                         | Testosterone   |

AKR1D1 bound progesterone 0.5 kcal/mol more tightly than testosterone. Converting this through ΔΔG (|ΔGT – ΔGP|) = RT ln(ratio), with RT = 0.616 kcal/mol at 310 K, gives approximately 2.3-fold selectivity for progesterone. This is consistent with AKR1D1's biological role, where progesterone intermediates are the primary physiological substrates.

### Contact analysis and residue identification

From the top-ranked docking pose, all enzyme residues within 4 Å of each docked ligand were identified. This is a standard cut-off for direct protein–ligand contacts and captures residues involved in van der Waals interactions and short-range electrostatics (<Citation reference="le-guilloux-et-al-2009" />). Residues in proximity to the C17 position were given particular attention, as this is the only position at which the two substrates differ, as discussed previously in the ligand preparation (<Citation reference="chen-et-al-2014" />).

#### **For AKR1D1:**

Both substrates share an identical Δ4-3-keto motif on the A ring, so the residues recognising that end cannot distinguish between them. Hence, the six shared contacts, two of which (Y58, E120) are the catalytic tetrad (<Ref target="tab-53c3s" />), confirm that both ligands dock in a catalytically productive orientation.

Progesterone contacts two hydrophobic residues, **V309** and **L311**, which can form a nonpolar surface that accommodates its acetyl group, while testosterone contacts **I57**, which could offer a hydrogen bond for the 17β-hydroxyl. V309 and L311 were targeted against progesterone, while I57 favours testosterone to increase affinity. All four were mutated singly and in combination.

#### **For ci2350:**

As in AKR1D1, the shared contacts cluster around the conserved Δ4-3-keto end of the molecule and include the catalytic residue, indicating that both ligands adopt a productive orientation. Y167 was excluded from mutagenesis, since altering it would be expected to compromise catalysis rather than selectivity.

**A107, R215** and **V263** appear to line the space progesterone’s acetyl group occupies (<Ref target="tab-zkjil" />). R215 is the most interesting of the three: the other two are small hydrophobic residues, but arginine can donate hydrogen bonds, so its contact may be a specific polar interaction rather than a shape fit, which would make it the cleanest target, along with **V59, F71, Q100, M254** and **L259** to increase the specificity towards testosterone.

### Rational mutagenesis

Rational mutagenesis is a protein engineering approach in which specific amino acid substitutions are chosen in advance from structural and mechanistic knowledge of the enzyme, rather than generating them at random and screening (<Citation reference="lutz-et-al-2010" />).

The residues in <Ref target="tab-53c3s" /> and <Ref target="tab-zkjil" /> were substituted on physicochemical grounds, altering the size, polarity or hydrogen-bonding capacity of the pocket near C17 in a direction consistent with a shift towards testosterone (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" />). Both single-point variants and multi-residue combinations were designed for both enzymes.

#### **For AKR1D1:**

At V309 and L311, the substitutions cover three different mechanisms: serine (S) and threonine (T) shrink the side chain and add polarity to pocket; tyrosine (Y), phenylalanine (F) and tryptophan (W) fill the cavity so the acetyl group has less room; and glutamate (E) and lysine (K) place a charge against a neutral substituent. Comparing them should indicate which property actually governs discrimination towards progesterone.

At I57, all five substitutions replace an aliphatic side chain with one capable of hydrogen bonding, any of which could pair with testosterone’s 17β-hydroxyl. Combinations test whether the two mutation effects are independent. If they are, the selectivity shifts should roughly sum.

  | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | **Disrupt progesterone C17 contacts**       | V309, L311                    | V309T/S/Y/F/W/E/K, L311S/T/Y/F/E | Remove the hydrophobic surface stabilising the acetyl group       |
  | **Enhance testosterone 17β-OH recognition** | I57                           | I57S/T/N/Q/Y                     | Introduce a hydrogen-bond donor or acceptor near the 17β-hydroxyl |

</TableWithCaption>

#### **For ci2350:**

At A107 and V263, the substitutions add volume, narrowing the space progesterone’s acetyl group occupies. For R215, the approach is removal rather than bulk. R215A and R215G strip the side chain back, which would eliminate any hydrogen bond it forms with the acetyl carbonyl.

For testosterone, F71Y adds a hydroxyl to an aromatic ring already in contact with the 17β-hydroxyl, and V59S/T and Q100S/T introduce or shorten a polar side chain so the hydrogen bond geometry can be optimised. Similarly, combination tests were performed.

### Stability screening

Structural stability was evaluated using PyRosetta (<Citation reference="chaudhury-et-al-2010" />) with the ref2015 scoring function (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" />). The ref2015 function returns a single score summing van der Waals packing, hydrogen bonding, electrostatics, implicit solvation and backbone and sidechain torsional strain, with lower, more negative values corresponding to a better-packed and more stable structure.

Local sidechain repacking was performed within a 5.0 Å radius of the mutated position, capturing the first layer of residues most likely to be directly affected by the substitution (Chaudhury et al., 2010). Stability was expressed as the difference between mutant and wild-type (ΔΔG = E(mutant) − E(wild-type)), cancelling the common baseline and isolating the effect of the substitution. A high positive ΔΔG indicates that the mutation destabilises the structure (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" />).

Variants with ΔΔG above +5 REU were excluded; those below this threshold were considered structurally acceptable and advanced to docking (<Citation reference="alford-et-al-2017" />, <Citation reference="kellogg-et-al-2011" />). The threshold is deliberately permissive. It is set to reject severely destabilised variants, such as unresolvable steric clashes or lost buried hydrogen bonds.

## **Mathematical modelling of enzyme production and activity**

Biological systems combine processes that run at different speeds and affect one another, so the outcome of an experiment is rarely obvious from the individual parts. Mathematical models address this by describing each process with an equation whose terms correspond to defined biological events (<Citation reference="kraikivski-et-al-2023" />). These equations are Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs), solved numerically. Writing the system this way states every assumption openly and lets us change one parameter at a time, which is rarely practical at the bench (<Citation reference="alon-et-al-2006" />).

The specificity analysis found no AKR1D1 variant that shifted preference towards testosterone, while ci2350 produced several promising candidates, so the model was built around ci2350 alone. These findings are described on the Results page (add link to the enzyme specificity result here)

### Integrated growth and expression model

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/dry-lab-3d-model/integrated-model.avif" alt="Image of workflow" caption="Workflow of the integrated growth and expression model. Growth data and literature values define the parameters of two coupled layers, which are solved numerically and then tested by parameter sweeps and Monte Carlo sampling." numbered refId="fig-ldjtv" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

#### System definition

The system modelled is ci2350 produced from a Twist pET vector in *E. coli* *BL21(DE3)*, with the gene under the T7 promoter and induced with 0.8 mM IPTG. The quantity of interest is the amount of enzyme per cell because it determines how much enzyme is available to act on the steroids. Described in Parts page. (add link to parts page)

#### Growth model

A batch culture in a closed flask grows exponentially while nutrients are plentiful and then slows as they are consumed. The logistic equation is the simplest description of this behaviour (<Citation reference="verhulst-et-al-1838" />).

dX/dt = µmax · X · (1 − X/K)

X is the culture density (OD600), µmax the maximum specific growth rate (per hour) and K the carrying capacity, the OD600 at which growth stops. The factor (1 − X/K) falls from 1 to 0 as the culture fills, so it acts as the brake on growth (<Ref target="fig-pllb8" />). A lag phase was not included, so the simulated culture starts to grow immediately from the starting density.

From our experimental data, the cells **double** **every 28 minutes** (µmax = 1.48 h⁻¹). We did not measure the plateau, so K was set to OD600 15, a plausible density for a rich medium, and its influence is examined in the sensitivity analysis. From these parameters, the growth saturates near to 7 h (<Ref target="fig-pllb8" />).

#### Expression model

Since mRNA and the enzyme behave differently inside the cell, we examined them separately. m is the mRNA level in the cell and E the enzyme level, both in relative units. mRNA is short-lived: messages are transcribed at rate α and degraded at rate δm, so the cell maintains a small working pool at a steady level (<Ref target="fig-ldjtv" />), giving the ribosomes a constant supply to work from (<Citation reference="alon-et-al-2006" />).

dm/dt = α − δm · m  (<Citation reference="alon-et-al-2006" />)

m\* = α / δm (steady state maintainance)

The enzyme is the opposite. It is built from those messages at rate β. We assume it is stable, so nothing degrades it, and the only loss is growth itself. At each division, the enzyme already made is shared between two daughter cells, so the faster the culture grows, the more it dilutes its own protein (Klumpp et al., 2009). This is where the two models are integrated: dilution takes the culture’s current growth rate, µ(t) = µmax · (1 − X/K), rather than a fixed value. Enzyme therefore stays low while the culture divides fast and accumulates as growth slows (<Ref target="fig-ldjtv" />) (<Citation reference="klumpp-et-al-2009" />).

dE/dt = β · m − µ(t) · E = β · m − µmax · (1 − X/K) · E (<Citation reference="alon-et-al-2006" />)

Five assumptions:

* ci2350’s mRNA turns over like a typical E. coli transcript, since its half-life has not been measured.
* The promoter is fully induced at 0.8 mM IPTG, so transcription runs at a constant rate.
* The enzyme is stable, so dilution by growth is its only loss.
* Expression does not burden the cell, so it does not slow growth.
* mRNA and enzyme are in relative units, since absolute counts would require data we do not have.

Bacterial messages break down within minutes of being made (<Citation reference="bernstein-et-al-2002" />). We used a half-life (3 to 8 mins) to predict the decay constants (δ = ln2 / t½) range and used their average of 9.53 h⁻¹. The transcription rate (α) and translation rate (β) are placeholders, as neither has been measured for ci2350.

#### Mathematical simulation and sensitivity of the enzyme level

The integrated three-equation system was solved in R using the deSolve package (<Citation reference="soetaert-et-al-2010" />), with the Runge–Kutta method ode45 over 24 h in 100 time steps, starting from X = 0.05, m = 0 and E = 0. Since several parameters are assumptions or literature ranges rather than measurements, we then asked how much the result depends on them, and which the experiments should pin down first (<Citation reference="saltelli-et-al-2008" />). Each of K, µmax, α and δm was sweep by ±20% around its baseline individually, a standard window for local sensitivity, and the whole system was re-run at every step, recording culture density, mRNA level and enzyme level after 24 h as a percentage change from baseline. Because all three are recorded together, a single run shows how much the enzyme level moves and how a growth parameter affects expression, and vice versa. β was not varied separately since the enzyme level depends on α and β only through their product.

#### Uncertainty analysis of the enzyme level

Sweeps change one parameter at a time, whereas in practice all of them are uncertain together. We therefore ran a Monte Carlo analysis (<Citation reference="saltelli-et-al-2008" />), drawing 500 parameter sets at random, each value taken from a distribution centred on its baseline with a spread of 10%, and recorded the enzyme level after 24 h for each. The 10% is an assumed level of uncertainty, not a measured one.

The analysis was run **three times**: with only the growth parameters (K, µmax) varied, with only the expression parameters (α, δm) varied, and with all four varied together. Comparing the three shows which layer contributes most to the uncertainty in the enzyme level and we reported the mean and the 95% interval.

The results of both per-parameter sweep and Monte Carlo analyses of enzyme production are presented on the Results page (Link to the Result of mathematical modelling). The scripts for the integrated model are available on GitLab (Link).

### Enzyme activity model

#### Activity model

Testosterone and progesterone are close in shape and compete for the same active site of ci2350, so each slows down the conversion of the other. This is described by Michaelis–Menten kinetics for two competing substrates. Each substrate has its own turnover rate, but the two rates share same denominator, which makes the substrates compete (<Citation reference="michaelis-et-al-1913" />).

v\_T = (kcat,T / Km,T) · E\_tot · T / (1 + T/Km,T + P/Km,P)

v\_P = (kcat,P / Km,P) · E\_tot · P / (1 + T/Km,T + P/Km,P)

T and P are the testosterone and progesterone concentrations (µM), and v\_T and v\_P are their rates of consumption (µM per hour). E\_tot is the enzyme concentration in the culture; Km,T and Km,P are the Michaelis constants (estimated from docking, see <Ref target="tab-2pa7e" />), and kcat,T and kcat,P are the turnover numbers. The ratio kcat/Km measures how efficiently each substrate is used, so the preference of a variant is the ratio of kcat,T/Km,T to kcat,P/Km,P.

The enzyme availability comes from the integrated model of enzyme production: enzyme per cell multiplied by culture density, converted to a concentration by a single scaling factor (model assumption). The enzyme in the flask therefore rises as the culture grows, and continues to rise more slowly once growth stops, because the enzyme is no longer diluted.

E\_tot = E\_scale · E · X (model assumption)

The three layers run together as one system of five equations:

1. dX/dt = µmax · X · (1 − X/K)
2. dm/dt = α · s\_ind(t) − δm · m
3. dE/dt = β · m − µmax · (1 − X/K) · E
4. dT/dt = −v\_T · s\_sub(t)
5. dP/dt = −v\_P · s\_sub(t)

The first three are the growth and expression layers described in the integrated model of enzyme production; the last two are the substrates being consumed. In the earlier model, the expression started at time zero. Here α is multiplied by s\_ind, so that mRNA is made only after IPTG is added, and a second switch, s\_sub(t), allows the steroids to be consumed only after they have been added. The switches reproduce the bench protocol: s\_ind(t) turns transcription on when the culture reaches OD600 0.5, which the growth model identifies at 1.58 h, and s\_sub(t) turns substrate consumption on four hours later, when substrate is added and enough enzyme has built up. Both steroids start at 50 µM, and the reaction is followed for eight hours.&#x20;

Seven assumptions underlie these equations which are listed below:

1. Testosterone and progesterone bind the same site and exclude each other (competitive binding).
2. NADH is present in saturating amounts, so it does not limit the rate.
3. kcat is the same for both steroids and for all variants.
4. The amount of enzyme per cell is the same for all ci2350 variants, because they are expressed under the same conditions.
5. The steroids are available to the enzyme at their added concentrations, without limits from uptake into the cells or from solubility.
6. Product inhibition is not included.
7. The ci2350 variants do not change folding or stability.

  | tind – time of induction                                | OD600 0.5 (1.58 h)  | Assumed experimental design; time from the growth model |
  | tsub – time of substrate addition                       | 4 h after induction | Assumed experimental design                             |
  | T0, P0 – starting concentration of each steroid         | 50 µM               | Assumed experimental design                             |

#### Linking docking to Km

<FigureWithCaption src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6160/wiki/dry-lab-3d-model/docking-to-km.avif" alt="Image of docking to Km" caption="From docking score to Km. Each docking score (ΔG) is converted to a binding constant, Kd = exp(ΔG/RT), and Km is assumed to follow Kd. The wild-type progesterone score is anchored to its measured Kd (0.432 µM), and each variant’s Km shifts by its score difference from wild type. A variant with a lower Km for testosterone than for progesterone prefers testosterone. This link between Km and Kd is an assumption, and docking errors are larger than most differences between variants." numbered={false} refId="fig-ldjtv" scale={100} plain={false} noZoom={false} />

The activity model needs a Michaelis constant (Km) for testosterone and progesterone for every ci2350 variant, but none has been published. The only kinetic information on the enzyme is the wild-type dissociation constant (Kd) for progesterone, 0.432 µM, measured by isothermal titration calorimetry (<Citation reference="arp-et-al-2025" />).

We therefore **propose** to use our docking results to estimate the missing values. Docking gives a binding energy ΔG, which relates to Kd = exp(ΔG/RT) (<Citation reference="gilson-et-al-2007" />). Here R is the gas constant and RT = 0.616 kcal/mol at 37 °C, so a more **negative ΔG** means tighter binding and a **smaller Kd**. Docking scores are not reliable as absolute energies, since Vina can deviate from experiment by 1 to 2 kcal/mol (<Citation reference="eberhardt-et-al-2021" />, <Citation reference="trott-et-al-2010" />), so we used only the differences between scores calculated with the same protocol.

We took the wild type with progesterone as the reference point and gave it the measured value, **0.432 µM**, as its Km. This treats **Km as proportional to Kd**, which is a **good approximation** when catalysis (kcat) is slower than the release of the steroid from the enzyme (Wilhelmová, 1996). Whether this holds for ci2350 is not known, because no kcat has been measured. From the reference, the Km values of all variants follow in two steps:

1. Km for progesterone is scaled from the wild-type value by the difference between the progesterone scores of the variant and the wild type (<Ref target="tab-2pa7e" />).
2. Testosterone has no measured value at all; its Km is calculated from the progesterone Km of the same variant, shifted by the difference between the two steroid scores of that variant (<Ref target="tab-2pa7e" />.

Km,P(variant) = 0.432 µM · exp\[(ΔG\_P,variant − ΔG\_P,wild type) / RT]

Km,T(variant) = Km,P(variant) · exp(ΔΔG\_T–P / RT);   ΔΔG\_T–P = ΔG\_T − ΔG\_P

A negative ΔΔG\_T–P means that the variant binds testosterone more tightly than progesterone, which gives testosterone the lower Km. We assumed the same kcat for both steroids because docking describes binding and not catalysis. The Km values obtained this way are predictions that guide the experiments, not measured constants. The assumptions behind them (Km proportional to Kd, equal kcat) are tested in the sensitivity analysis and are the first targets of the planned kinetic measurements.

#### Sensitivity analysis of activity model

The activity layer adds more uncertain values, such as kcat, E\_scale, the wild-type Km,P and the docking scores, on top of the uncertain expression parameters, so two checks were run.

For the variant with the largest predicted preference, R215A + V263I (see Result page; link to the enzyme selectivity engineering), each parameter was varied by ±20% with the others held at baseline, and the progesterone docking score was shifted by ±0.3 kcal/mol to see how a small docking error changes the outcome. The expression settings were swept more widely: α from 0.25 to 4 times baseline, β from 0.4 to 12 times, and the induction point from OD600 0.3 to 1.0.

#### Uncertainty analysis of activity

Most of the values in the model are guesses, so running it once would only tell us what happens if every guess is right. The Monte Carlo analysis instead ran the model 200 times for each variant, picking a different random value for each uncertain input every time.

Each variant was compared against a wild type given the same random expression and kcat values, so the only difference between the two was the docking scores. Every score was given a random error of about 1 kcal/mol, the typical accuracy of Vina, and the analysis was repeated with 0.5 kcal/mol as a more optimistic case. The other values were drawn over the ranges we consider plausible.

What comes out is a percentage for each variant: how often testosterone is preferred, and how often the variant beats the wild type. The wild type was also compared against a second random copy of itself, which shows how often a difference appears purely by chance when there is no real one.

## Results

### **Enzyme specificity engineering**

#### Overview

Two Δ4-3-ketosteroid 5β-reductases were taken forward for engineering: human **AKR1D1** and the bacterial enzyme **ci2350** from *Clostridium innocuum*. The aim was to shift substrate preference towards testosterone and away from progesterone, two steroids that differ only at the **C17 substituent**.

Structures were obtained from the PDB (AKR1D1, **3CAV**) and from **AlphaFold** (ci2350), and the functional pocket in each was located with **fpocket** and confirmed against **NCBI CD Search** annotations. Docking both substrates into the wild-type enzymes gave similar absolute affinities but opposite preferences: **AKR1D1 favoured progesterone by 0.5 kcal/mol,** while **ci2350 showed a marginal testosterone preference of 0.1 kcal/mol,** within the scoring noise.

Residues within **4 Å** of each docked ligand were sorted by which substrate they contacted. This identified three specificity determinants in AKR1D1 (**V309, L311, I57**) and six in ci2350 (**A107, R215, V263, V59, F71, Q100**). Substitutions at these positions were designed to weaken progesterone binding or strengthen testosterone binding, then screened for structural stability in **PyRosetta**. Of 24 AKR1D1 variants, 13 passed the **+5 REU** threshold; of 31 ci2350 variants, 17 passed. These variants were then docked with both substrates to assess their specificity towards testosterone.

The full workflow for both AKR1D1 and ci2350, from protein structure acquisition through to stability screening, is described on the [Modeling page](https://wiki.igemlund.org/technical_modeling#enzyme-specificity-engineering-towards-testosterone).&#x20;
